## Who this roadmap is for
You score around **1380–1450** on full tests and want **1500–1550 or above**. You probably reach the harder second module in both sections most of the time, rarely miss easy questions, and lose most points to a handful of hard items and the occasional slip.

At this level the problem changes shape. Below 1400, the main question is *what don't I know?* Above it, the main question is *why did I miss something I know?* The [1200 to 1400 Roadmap](/guide-1200-to-1400-roadmap) built the system; this one sharpens it into a precision tool.

**No score promises.** Nobody can guarantee a 1550. What this roadmap can do is remove the predictable sources of lost points and give you a way to measure whether that is happening.

## The arithmetic of the last 150 points
A 1550 is, for example, 780 + 770 or 760 + 790. Each section is out of 800. Two consequences:

- **Each remaining miss matters more.** You may have only a few misses left in a section, so removing even one or two error *types* is a big change.
- **Variance matters as much as average.** A student who scores 1520, 1460 and 1540 on three mocks has the ability for 1540 but not the reliability. Reducing bad days is as valuable as raising good ones.

Also remember that each module includes a small number of **unscored pretest questions** (the College Board specifications describe two per module) that you cannot identify. Treat every question as if it counts.

## Your three enemies at this level
1. **The hard-module trap.** Questions designed so the obvious method gives a plausible wrong answer (see the two hard-module guides).
2. **The slip.** A known skill applied wrongly once per test: a sign, a misread "not", an SPR entry.
3. **The time squeeze.** Spending three minutes on one item and rushing the last two.

Each cycle in this roadmap attacks one or more of these with a specific tool.

## The precision cycle: diagnose, fix, test (two weeks)
| Days | Phase | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Diagnose | Full adaptive mock under strict conditions |
| 2 | Diagnose | Forensic review of every miss, guess and slow question |
| 3–10 | Fix | Hard-only drills on the cycle's targets; zero-careless protocol |
| 11 | Test | Two timed upper-level modules (one per section) |
| 12–13 | Fix | Close remaining gaps from the test day |
| 14 | Reset | Variance and trend review |

Compared with the 1200–1400 cycle, the review is deeper (a whole day), the drills are harder and the test phase examines **slow** questions as well as wrong ones.

## Step 1: Mocks under strict conditions
- Use full adaptive tests only: official Bluebook practice tests and full mocks in the [Schood SAT simulator](https://sat.schood.online).
- Take them at the **time of day of your real test**, in one sitting, with the scheduled 10-minute break and nothing else.
- No pausing, no phone, no checking answers mid-test.
- Mark guesses *and* any question where you hesitated between two choices.

## Step 2: Forensic review
At this level, a miss list is not enough. For every **missed**, **guessed** or **slow** question (more than about two minutes in Math, more than about 90 seconds in R&W), record:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Question type and domain | Pattern spotting |
| Cause code | C (content), P (process), T (time), X (named slip), G (guess-right) |
| The exact moment it went wrong | "Chose B after eliminating A and C; did not test D against the goal" |
| The trap, if any | "True-but-irrelevant data" or "discriminant of wrong equation" |
| Rule sentence | One sentence for next time |
| Time spent | Find your time sinks |

**Slow-correct questions are hidden misses.** If a question took three minutes, it stole time from two others. Log it.

## Step 3: Choosing targets by trap, not by domain
Domain-level targets ("work on Advanced Math") are too broad here. Choose **two traps or habits** per cycle, for example:

- "Cross-text partial agreement" and "SPR decimal precision".
- "Rhetorical synthesis two-part goals" and "parameter questions with no-solution conditions".

Use the trap families in [R&W Hard-Module Traps](/guide-rw-hard-module-traps) and [Math Hard-Module Traps](/guide-math-hard-module-traps) as a menu. Your forensic review tells you which two to pick.

## Step 4: Hard-only drilling
- In the [SAT Practice Bank](https://practice.sat.schood.online/bank), filter to **hard** in the target skills. Medium questions no longer teach you much.
- **Set size:** 10–15 questions, timed at test pace, then full review.
- **Mix sets** after the first few days: interleave the target skill with other hard skills so you practise *recognising* the trap, not just solving it when you know it is coming.
- **Re-solve after 48 hours** every miss and every slow question, with no notes.

**Accuracy target for the drill phase:** aim to make your misses in the target skill rare across 30 hard questions. If misses persist, go back one step: untimed, with a written method, then time it again.

## Step 5: The zero-careless protocol
"Be more careful" is not a method. These are:

**Math**
- **Circle the ask.** Before computing, underline what the question wants (k? 2k? the sum of solutions?).
- **Sign sweep.** On every equation you write, re-read the signs once before solving.
- **SPR entry check.** Before leaving a question, compare the box with your work: sign, fraction bar, number of decimal places. The Bluebook directions allow 5 characters for positive answers and 6 for negative ones, and decimals that don't fit are truncated or rounded at the fourth digit, so fill the box when you convert.
- **Units and form.** Percent versus decimal, minutes versus hours, radius versus diameter.

**Reading and Writing**
- **Task restatement.** Say the task in three words before reading choices.
- **Negative-word alert.** Mark NOT, EXCEPT, LEAST and "weaken" when they appear.
- **Final-choice test.** Before confirming, ask: "Does every part of this choice fit the passage and the task?"
- **Pair check in boundaries.** If a supplementary element opens with a comma or dash, confirm it closes with the same mark.

**Track it.** Count X codes per mock. The protocol is working when X codes approach zero across two mocks in a row.

## Step 6: Time control in the upper modules
**Reading and Writing (32 minutes, 27 questions).**
- Conventions and transitions should be your fastest items. Aim for under 45 seconds each.
- Keep a time bank of a few minutes for cross-text, data and inference items near the end of skill groups.
- Use mark for review, not stubbornness: two minutes and still split between two choices means choose, mark and move on.

**Math (35 minutes, 22 questions).**
- First 8 questions: brisk, but verify.
- Questions 9–16: invest where needed, using Desmos routines from [Desmos Power Moves](/guide-desmos-power-moves).
- Questions 17–22: read the final sentence first; expect parameter, circle and statistics combinations.
- Reserve the last 3–4 minutes for flagged items and an SPR re-read.

**Remember:** when the module timer reaches zero, Bluebook moves you on automatically, and you cannot return to a module once you have left it.

## Step 7: Reducing variance
Write down the score of every mock. After three, look at the spread.

- **Wide spread, high best score:** your problem is reliability. Prioritise the zero-careless protocol, sleep before mocks, consistent timing and identical test-day routines.
- **Narrow spread, plateau:** your problem is a ceiling. Prioritise new trap families and harder sets.
- **Drop on one section only:** check whether that section's misses cluster at the end (fatigue or time) or throughout (content).

Practise your **break routine** in every mock: water, a small snack, a short walk if allowed, and no reviewing of the section you just finished.

## A forensic review, worked through
Two illustrative misses from a 1450-level mock, reviewed with the full protocol.

**Miss 1: Reading and Writing, cross-text.** The question asked how the author of Text 2 would respond to Text 1. The student chose an answer saying Text 2 *rejects* Text 1's claim entirely.

- **Re-solve before the explanation:** on a second read, the student notices Text 2 begins by accepting Text 1's evidence and only disputes its conclusion. Re-solved correctly.
- **Cause code:** P (process). The student knew how to do these questions but read Text 2 for its disagreement only.
- **Trap:** partial agreement.
- **Rule sentence:** *"Before choosing, write one word for what Text 2 accepts and one for what it rejects."*
- **Time spent:** about 2 minutes, which also makes it a slow question.

**Miss 2: Math, student-produced response.** The answer was −5/6, and the student entered −.83.

- **Re-solve:** the algebra was correct; the entry was not. The Bluebook directions allow 6 characters for a negative answer, so −.8333 fitted and −.83 lost precision.
- **Cause code:** X (named slip: "shortened a decimal").
- **Trap:** SPR precision.
- **Rule sentence:** *"When I convert to a decimal, I fill the box."*

**What the review produced:** two targets for the next cycle (partial-agreement cross-text items and SPR precision), each with a concrete habit. Neither required learning new content, which is common at this level.

## Building hard-only sets that teach
Random hard sets are better than medium sets, but designed sets are better still.

- **Block, then interleave.** Spend the first two days of a cycle on blocked sets (all one trap family), so you can learn the pattern. From day three, interleave: mix the target family with three or four other hard skills, so you practise *recognising* the trap without being told it is coming.
- **Decoy writing.** On every multiple-choice item in a set, write a five-word reason for rejecting each wrong choice. It is slow at first and is one of the most direct ways to stop falling for near-miss answers.
- **Speed tier.** Once accuracy is high, rerun similar sets at a slightly faster pace than test pace. This creates a time cushion for test day.
- **Cold start.** At least once a week, begin a session with a hard timed set before any warm-up. The first questions of the real test are not preceded by a warm-up set either.

## Stamina and full-test conditions
The test is 2 hours 14 minutes of testing plus a 10-minute break, and the second module of each section comes at the end of that section. Fatigue can cost the last points even when the knowledge is there.

- **Run full mocks at your test time of day,** in one sitting.
- **Practise the break** exactly as you will take it: drink, snack, a short stand or walk, no thinking about the R&W section.
- **Read dense nonfiction daily** so that the last R&W questions do not feel heavier than the first.
- **Notice end-of-module drift.** If your forensic review shows misses clustering in the last five questions of a module, add one extra timed module per week and practise the final-minute routine (SPR re-read, flagged items) until it is automatic.

## Using your real score report
After an official test, the score report's Knowledge and Skills section shows performance in the four R&W and four Math content areas. Use it as a cross-check, not as your only diagnosis:

- If a content area shows lower performance than the others and your mock reviews agree, it is a real target.
- If the score report and your mock reviews disagree, trust the pattern across several mocks, which contain far more of your answers than a single test.
- If you plan another attempt, read [Retake and Superscore Strategy](/retake-and-superscore-strategy) before booking.

## Weekly targets
| Target | Each week |
|---|---|
| Study hours | 8–12, in 5–6 sessions |
| Hard-only questions | 80–120, all reviewed |
| Re-solves | Every logged item from the previous week, after 48 hours |
| Timed upper-level modules | At least one per section |
| Error-log codes | X codes trending down; T codes trending down |
| Reading | 20–30 minutes daily of dense nonfiction |

**Weekly checkpoint (15 minutes):** count entries by trap family and cause code, and write the two targets for the following week.

## The Reading and Writing plan above 700
- **Dense passages.** Practise slowing down on the *one* sentence that carries the claim, finding or contrast, rather than slowing down on the whole passage.
- **Two-part goals in rhetorical synthesis.** Count goal verbs; require all of them.
- **Cross-text.** Name what Text 2 accepts and rejects before reading choices.
- **Quantitative evidence.** Translate the claim into the comparison it needs (difference, trend, ratio) before looking at the table.
- **Words in context.** Use punctuation and contrast words as the definition; avoid choices that are merely possible.
- **Conventions mastery.** At this level conventions should be near-perfect. Any remaining miss becomes an immediate rule sentence and a 20-question drill.

## The Math plan above 700
- **Parameters everywhere.** No-solution and infinitely-many conditions; discriminant tangency; vertex and root relationships.
- **Structure over computation.** Sum and product of roots, equivalent forms, factoring by substitution.
- **Modelling interpretation.** What a coefficient, base or intercept means in context; percent change per different period.
- **Geometry and trigonometry edges.** Radians, arc length, scaled volumes, complementary angles, circle equations with completed squares.
- **Statistics claims.** Random sampling versus random assignment; margin of error as a plausible range.
- **Desmos fluency.** Sliders, regressions with constraints and list statistics should be second nature.

## Advanced Math drill: structure before computation
Top scorers save time by spotting structure. Two original examples:

**Example 1 (student-produced response).** If x² + y² = 29 and xy = 10, what is the value of (x + y)²?

Expand: (x + y)² = x² + 2xy + y² = 29 + 2(10) = **49**. Solving for x and y separately would work (x and y are 2 and 5) but wastes time, and on harder versions the individual values are not integers.

**Example 2 (student-produced response).** The expression (2x + 7)/(x + 2) can be rewritten as a + b/(x + 2), where a and b are constants. What is a + b?

Rewrite the numerator around the denominator: 2x + 7 = 2(x + 2) + 3. So the expression equals 2 + 3/(x + 2), giving a = 2, b = 3 and **a + b = 5**. Desmos check: graph the original and `y = 2 + 3/(x + 2)`; they coincide.

**The habit:** before computing, ask "is there an identity or rearrangement that gives the requested quantity directly?" The requested quantity is often a combination (x + y, a + b, 1/r + 1/s) precisely because a structural route exists.

## Advanced R&W drill: decoy writing in practice
Decoy writing means writing a short reason against every wrong choice. On a quantitative-evidence item like the roof-coating example in [R&W Hard-Module Traps](/guide-rw-hard-module-traps), a finished decoy sheet looks like this:

| Choice | Five-word verdict |
|---|---|
| A | True; compares wrong quantity |
| B | Shows differing reductions: key |
| C | True; ignores the coating |
| D | Shows equal benefit: opposite |

**Why it works.** Most misses at this level are not caused by failing to see the right answer; they are caused by failing to *reject* a near-miss. Writing the verdict forces you to name the flaw, and the flaws repeat: wrong quantity, wrong scope, half the goal, too extreme, true but off-task. After two or three weeks, you start seeing the verdicts before you write them.

**How often.** Use decoy writing on every item in the first two days of each cycle, then on any item you hesitate over.

## The endgame of each section
**Reading and Writing.** If your last misses are spread across many skills, the issue is usually reading precision under time; add the claim-first habit and a daily dense-reading session. If they cluster in one skill, drill that skill with blocked then interleaved sets for one cycle.

**Math.** If your last misses are in the final third of the second module, the issue is usually time or setup on long items; practise reading the final sentence first and choosing a route (algebra, Desmos, or structure) within the first few seconds. If they are scattered SPR entry errors, the zero-careless protocol is the fix, not more content.

## What to stop doing at this level
- **Stop doing medium sets for comfort.** They feel productive and hide your real gaps.
- **Stop reviewing only wrong answers.** Slow and guessed answers are where your next points are.
- **Stop broad content review.** Re-reading whole chapters you already know wastes the hours you need for traps.
- **Stop chasing obscure topics.** If a topic is not in the official domain and skill lists, it is not on the test.
- **Stop taking mocks back to back.** A mock without a full forensic review day is a lost data point.
- **Stop judging yourself on one mock.** Use the trend of three.
- **Stop studying late the night before mocks.** Rehearse the real test-eve routine instead.

## What to start doing at this level
- **Teach-back:** after each forensic review, explain your two worst misses out loud as if to a student. If you cannot explain the trap, you have not learned it.
- **Decoy practice:** for hard multiple-choice questions, write down why *each* wrong choice is wrong, not just why the key is right.
- **Retake planning:** if you will test more than once, read [Retake and Superscore Strategy](/retake-and-superscore-strategy). The College Board notes that many colleges consider your highest section scores across test dates, but policies vary, so check each college.

| 2 | R&W: data and weaken/strengthen | Math: parameters | Timed upper modules |
| 3 | R&W: cross-text | Math: nonlinear systems | Mock 2 |
| 4 | R&W: rhetorical synthesis | Math: SPR precision | Timed upper modules |

## Checkpoints {checklist}
- Three consecutive full mocks within a narrow band near your target.
- X (named slip) codes rare across the last two mocks.
- No question took more than about three minutes in the last two mocks.
- Every trap family in both hard-module guides attempted and logged.
- All re-solves from the last two weeks correct.
- Break and test-eve routines rehearsed at least twice.

## Frequently asked questions
**Should I keep doing full mocks every week?** One per two-week cycle is usually enough, plus timed modules. At this level the forensic review is where improvement happens, and it takes a full day.

**My Math is 790 but R&W is 720. Should I still study Math?** Yes, briefly: two short hard-only sessions a week keep it sharp. Put the rest into R&W, which has more reachable points.

**I keep missing one or two hard Math questions per test. Is that just bad luck?** Usually not. Log them by trap family. If they come from different families each time, broaden your hard-only drills; if from the same family, drill that family until it is boring.

**How do I know I am ready to test?** When your last three mocks are in a tight band at or near your target, and your error log shows no repeated trap family.

## Summary {callout: The method in one line}
At this level you are not learning the SAT; you are removing the few ways you still lose points. Review forensically, drill only hard questions in two trap families per cycle, run the zero-careless protocol every time, and judge progress by the trend of three mocks.
