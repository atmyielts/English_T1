## Who this roadmap is for
You have a real score or a full-length practice score around **1150–1250**, and your target is **1400**. That means adding roughly 150–250 points, or about 75–125 per section if you split the work evenly. The public module [Score Goals and Study Plans](/score-goals-and-study-plans) explains how to set a target; this roadmap is the operating system for reaching this particular one.

**A plain warning about promises.** No honest guide can tell you how many weeks this will take. It depends on your starting profile, your hours and the quality of your review. What this roadmap gives you is a method whose progress you can *measure every two weeks*, so you always know whether it is working.

## What a 1400 looks like in the score structure
The SAT total runs from 400 to 1600 and is the sum of two section scores, each from 200 to 800. A 1400 is, for example, 700 + 700, 720 + 680 or 680 + 720. A 1200 is often around 600 + 600, or an uneven split such as 650 + 550.

**First decision: even or uneven gain?** Look at your two section scores.
- If they differ by **80 points or more**, most of your gain should come from the weaker section first, because it usually has more reachable points.
- If they are **within 50 points**, run both sections in parallel, with slightly more time on the one where your errors are mostly *content* gaps (they are cheaper to fix than reading-speed problems).

## Why the adaptive design matters for this jump
Each section has two modules. The College Board describes the first as a broad mix of easy, medium and hard questions, and says your performance on it determines whether the second module is harder or easier on average.

For a student moving from 1200 to 1400, this means:
- **First-module accuracy is your foundation.** Careless errors on easy and medium questions in module 1 cost the point *and* weaken the performance that routes you.
- **You must become comfortable in the harder module.** Students near 1200 often see a mix of routings. At 1400 you should expect the harder second module in both sections much of the time, so your practice must include hard questions, not just volume of medium ones.

## The two-week cycle: diagnose, fix, test
The whole roadmap runs on one repeating cycle.

| Days | Phase | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Diagnose | Full timed mock, then classify every miss |
| 2–11 | Fix | Targeted work on the top two error sources |
| 12–13 | Test | Timed modules on the fixed skills |
| 14 | Reset | Review the cycle, choose next targets |

Each cycle should end with **evidence**: did the targeted error type become rarer? If yes, move to the next one. If no, change the fix, not the target.

## Step 1 (Diagnose): taking a diagnostic mock that is worth reviewing
- Use a **full-length adaptive test**: either an official Bluebook practice test or a full mock in the [Schood SAT simulator](https://sat.schood.online). Alternate between them across cycles so you see both sources.
- Take it in **one sitting**, timed, with a 10-minute break between sections as on the real test.
- **Mark every question you guessed on** with a note, even if you got it right. A lucky correct answer is still a weakness.
- Do not check answers during the break.

## Step 2 (Diagnose): classifying every miss
Every missed or guessed question gets exactly one cause:

| Cause | Definition | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Content (C)** | You did not know the rule, formula or skill | Learn it from the matching module, then drill it |
| **Process (P)** | You knew it but set up or read it wrongly | Build a checking habit for that question type |
| **Time (T)** | You ran out of time or rushed | Pacing changes, faster routines |
| **Careless (X)** | Arithmetic slip, mis-click, misread choice | Specific check, not "be careful" |
| **Guess-right (G)** | Correct but unsure | Treat as a miss for review |

**Rule:** "careless" is only allowed when you can name the exact slip ("copied −3 as 3"). If you cannot name it, it is a process error.

## Step 3 (Diagnose): finding the two targets for the cycle
Count your misses by **domain** and by **cause**. Then weigh each domain by its official share of the test:

- **Math:** Algebra about 35%, Advanced Math about 35%, Problem-Solving and Data Analysis about 15%, Geometry and Trigonometry about 15%.
- **Reading and Writing:** Craft and Structure about 28%, Information and Ideas about 26%, Standard English Conventions about 26%, Expression of Ideas about 20%.

**Priority score** for each domain = (your misses in it) × (its weight). Your two highest priority scores are the cycle's targets. The official score report's Knowledge and Skills section, which shows performance in the eight content areas, is a good cross-check when you have a real test score.

**Typical 1200-level profile (common, not universal):** Standard English Conventions and Algebra are often the cheapest points available, because the skills are rule-based and the questions appear early in each skill group. If they are among your misses, fix them first.

## Step 4 (Fix): the targeted-practice day
Each fix day (about 60–90 minutes) has the same shape:

1. **Learn (15–20 min):** read the matching public module section, for example [Standard English Conventions](/reading-writing-conventions) for boundaries.
2. **Drill (30–40 min):** in the [SAT Practice Bank](https://practice.sat.schood.online/bank), filter to the target domain and skill. Start at medium difficulty; move to hard when you reach about 85% accuracy over 20 questions.
3. **Review (15–20 min):** every miss goes into the error log with a cause code and a *rule sentence* (see the next section).
4. **Spaced review (5–10 min):** clear the items due in [Reviews](https://practice.sat.schood.online/reviews).

**Volume guide:** 20–30 questions per fix day is enough. Quality of review matters more than count.

## Step 5 (Fix): the error log that actually changes behaviour
A useful error log is short and forces a rule. Use the [SAT Practice notebook](https://practice.sat.schood.online/notebook) or a paper notebook with these fields:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Date and source | Cycle 2, mock 1, Math M2 Q14 |
| Domain and skill | Advanced Math, nonlinear equations |
| Cause code | P |
| What I did | Set discriminant of x² − 6x + k + 3 instead of the combined equation |
| Rule sentence | "Before using b² − 4ac, write the single combined equation." |
| Re-solve date | 2 days later, without notes |
| Re-solve result | Correct / still wrong |

**The rule sentence is the point of the log.** If a miss does not produce a sentence you can apply to the *next* question of that type, the review is not finished.

**Re-solve:** two days after logging, redo the question from scratch with no notes. Only a correct re-solve closes the entry.

## Step 6 (Test): proving the fix worked
On days 12–13 of each cycle:
- Take **one timed module** in each section (27 R&W questions in 32 minutes; 22 Math questions in 35 minutes) using SAT Practice or a module from a Bluebook test you have not seen.
- Count misses **in the two target domains only**. Compare with the diagnostic.
- If target misses dropped clearly, the fix worked; keep a maintenance dose (10 questions twice a week) and choose new targets.
- If not, change the fix: more learning time, a different explanation source, or a slower untimed drill.

## A worked cycle, start to finish
Here is how one cycle looks in practice for an illustrative student, "Aziz", whose diagnostic mock gave 610 R&W and 590 Math. The details are an example of the method, not a prediction of results.

**Day 1: diagnose.** Aziz takes a full simulator mock. He marks nine questions as guesses.

**Day 2: classify.** He logs every miss and guess. In R&W, most entries are boundaries and transitions (mainly C codes: he did not know the semicolon-before-*however* rule). In Math, most entries are systems and linear functions, with many P codes (he set up correct equations but solved for the wrong variable).

**Day 3: choose targets.** Weighting by domain share, his two targets are **Standard English Conventions** (R&W) and **Algebra** (Math). He writes them at the top of his notebook page.

**Days 4–11: fix.** Each day he reads the matching section of a public module for 15 minutes, drills 20–25 Bank questions in the target, and writes rule sentences. Two of his rule sentences: *"Two complete clauses + however = semicolon before, comma after."* and *"Circle the variable the question asks for before solving."*

**Days 12–13: test.** One timed module per section. He counts misses in his two targets only and compares them with the diagnostic.

**Day 14: reset.** Conventions misses dropped sharply, so conventions moves to maintenance (10 questions twice a week). Algebra P codes dropped less, so Algebra stays a target for one more cycle, now with a new fix: he solves every system twice, once by algebra and once in Desmos, to catch setup errors. His second target becomes **Information and Ideas**, the next-highest priority score.

**What to copy from this example:** the targets came from counted, weighted data; every fix produced a sentence; and the decision at the end of the cycle followed the evidence, not a feeling.

## A review session, minute by minute
A 60-minute review session after a timed module looks like this:

- **0–5 min:** list every miss and guess with its question number.
- **5–30 min:** re-solve each one *before* reading any explanation. Note which ones you can now solve with time. Those are usually P or T codes.
- **30–45 min:** read explanations only for the questions you still cannot solve. Assign C codes to these.
- **45–55 min:** write one rule sentence per entry.
- **55–60 min:** schedule re-solve dates for every entry.

**If you are short on time,** cut the number of questions you review, not the steps. Ten fully reviewed questions teach more than thirty skimmed ones.

## Choosing practice material
Use each resource for what it does best:

| Resource | Best use in this roadmap |
|---|---|
| Official Bluebook practice tests | Full diagnostic mocks with the real interface |
| Schood SAT simulator | Additional full adaptive mocks |
| SAT Practice Bank | Targeted drills by domain, skill and difficulty |
| SAT Practice Reviews | Spaced repetition of missed items |
| College Board Student Question Bank | Extra official questions by domain and skill |
| Public Playbook modules | Learning the rule or method before drilling |

**Keep official tests for mocks.** If you drill from questions that appear in a Bluebook practice test, that test will no longer give an honest score estimate.

## Weekly targets
Targets here are *process* targets you control. Score targets come only from full tests.

| Target | Each week |
|---|---|
| Study hours | 8–10 hours, in 5–6 sessions |
| Targeted questions | 100–150 in the cycle's two domains |
| Error-log entries | Every miss and every guess logged with a rule sentence |
| Re-solves | All entries from the previous week re-solved |
| Timed work | At least one timed module per section |
| Spaced reviews | Reviews queue cleared at least four days a week |

**Weekly checkpoint (15 minutes, same day each week):** count entries by cause code. A falling share of P and X codes means your habits are improving even before your score moves.

## The Reading and Writing plan for this jump
**Phase A (cycles 1–2): conventions and transitions.** Boundaries and form/structure/sense rules are learnable in weeks. Aim for near-complete accuracy on medium conventions questions; they are among the fastest points on the test.

**Phase B (cycles 2–3): evidence and data.** Command of evidence (textual and quantitative) rewards a precise method: restate the claim, then choose the evidence that addresses exactly that claim. The premium [R&W Hard-Module Traps](/guide-rw-hard-module-traps) guide shows the true-but-irrelevant trap, a frequent source of lost points at this level.

**Phase C (cycles 3–4): words in context, structure, cross-text.** These depend on careful reading more than rules. Practise short daily sets rather than long weekly ones.

**Reading habit:** 20 minutes a day of varied nonfiction (science journalism, history, economics) builds the reading stamina that drills cannot.

## The Math plan for this jump
**Phase A (cycles 1–2): linear algebra to automatic.** Linear equations, linear functions, systems and inequalities. These appear at every difficulty level, and at 1400 you cannot miss medium ones. Work through [Math: Algebra](/math-algebra).

**Phase B (cycles 2–3): Advanced Math core.** Quadratics (factoring, vertex form, discriminant), exponential models, function notation and equivalent expressions. See [Math: Advanced Math](/math-advanced-math).

**Phase C (cycles 3–4): data and geometry essentials.** Percentages, ratios, mean and median, area and volume, right triangles and circles. Smaller domains, but cheap points once the formulas are memorised (and the reference sheet is checked).

**Desmos throughout.** Learn the basic routines in [Desmos and Calculator Strategy](/desmos-and-calculator-strategy): graphing both sides of an equation, finding intersections and reading a vertex. These alone remove many process errors.

## Pacing upgrades between 1200 and 1400
- **R&W:** about 71 seconds per question on average. Conventions items should become fast (30–45 seconds), freeing time for evidence and cross-text items.
- **Math:** a little over 95 seconds per question on average. Move quickly through early items, but never skip re-reading an SPR entry.
- **Use mark for review.** If a question passes about two minutes, choose your best answer, mark it and move on. Return only after finishing the module.
- **No blanks.** The College Board advises answering every question as well as you can; an empty answer cannot earn credit.

## What to stop doing
- **Stop taking full tests without full review.** A mock you do not review is a four-hour way to practise your current mistakes. One reviewed mock every two weeks beats three unreviewed ones.
- **Stop reading explanations and calling it review.** Reading an explanation feels like learning; re-solving the question two days later is learning.
- **Stop drilling only what you are good at.** It feels productive and changes nothing. Follow the priority score.
- **Stop memorising long vocabulary lists.** Words in context tests meaning in a sentence; reading widely and drilling the question type works better.
- **Stop saying "careless".** Name the slip or call it a process error.
- **Stop changing answers on a feeling.** Change only when you can point to a specific reason.
- **Stop avoiding the calculator.** It is permitted on every Math question.

## What to start doing
- Logging *guessed* correct answers as misses.
- Writing one rule sentence per miss.
- Re-solving every logged question after two days.
- Ending each week with a 15-minute checkpoint.
- Practising at least some hard questions every week from the first cycle on, so the harder module never feels foreign.

## Reading your progress honestly
- **Use full tests for score estimates,** not module scores or bank accuracy.
- **Expect noise.** Scores vary from test to test; College Board score reports show a score range for exactly this reason. Judge the trend over two or three mocks, not one.
- **Watch the cause mix.** If C codes are falling but P codes are not, you are learning content but not yet applying it under time. Add more timed modules.
- **Plateaus are information.** If two cycles pass with no change in the target domain, change the method: different explanations, slower practice, or a tutor review of your error log.

## Common profiles and what to do
**"Strong Math, stuck R&W" (for example 680 Math, 540 R&W).** Put 65–70% of hours into R&W. Conventions first, then evidence. Keep Math alive with 2 short sessions a week.

**"Strong R&W, stuck Math" (for example 650 R&W, 560 Math).** Put 65–70% into Math. Algebra and Advanced Math cover around 70% of the section; start there. Learn the Desmos routines early. The [Math Priority plan](/plans/math-priority) is built for this profile.

**"Even but slow" (600/600, runs out of time).** Your issue is often process, not content. Add a timed module in each section every week from the first cycle, and practise routines (Desmos moves, the claim-first method in R&W) until they are automatic.

**"Even and accurate but stuck at medium" (high accuracy on medium, low on hard).** Move to hard-only drills sooner, and read the two hard-module guides.

## Checkpoints before moving to the next roadmap {checklist}
- Two consecutive full mocks at or above about 1380.
- Conventions accuracy high and stable on medium and hard questions.
- No more than an occasional miss on medium Algebra questions.
- Error log shows P and X codes falling across three cycles.
- Comfortable finishing both sections on time with a few minutes to check.
- All error-log entries from the last two weeks re-solved correctly.
- When all of these hold, move on to the 1400 to 1550 Roadmap.

## Frequently asked questions
**How many hours a week do I need?** Most of the structure above assumes 8–10 hours. With 5–6 hours, stretch each cycle to three weeks rather than cutting review.

**Should I take a full mock every week?** Not in the fix phase. One full mock per two-week cycle, plus timed modules, gives you enough data without stealing review time.

**Is SAT Practice or the official question bank better?** Use both. The College Board Student Question Bank filters by domain, skill and difficulty; Schood SAT Practice adds reviews, a notebook and daily sessions. Avoid repeating questions from Bluebook practice tests you plan to use as mocks.

**When should I book the test?** When your plan leaves at least two full cycles before the test date, and your last two mocks are close to your target. The [Final 14 Days](/guide-final-14-days) guide covers the last stretch.

## Summary {callout: The method in one line}
Diagnose with a reviewed full mock, fix the two highest-priority domains for ten days, prove the fix with timed modules, and repeat. Measure process every week and score every two weeks.
