## What this guide adds

The public [Take an Interview](/speaking-take-an-interview) module explains the task. This guide is the training system. It gives you:

- a **framework for each of the four interview questions**, built for a 45-second answer
- **fluency and prosody routines** you can run in 15 minutes a day
- an **accuracy routine for Listen and Repeat**, which carries most of the section's raw points
- **twelve original model answers**: each of the four questions answered at a band 4, band 5 and band 6 level, with commentary

The goal is not to sound rehearsed. ETS's scoring guide rewards answers that are on topic and well elaborated, delivered at a good conversational pace, intelligible, with rhythm and intonation that carry meaning, and with accurate, varied language. A framework gives you the structure so that your attention can go to the language.

## What you will be able to do {checklist}

- Recognise which of the four question types you are hearing within the first seconds
- Start speaking quickly with a direct answer, then build a 45-second response
- Elaborate with one reason and one specific example instead of a list of general points
- Control pace, pauses, stress and intonation
- Repeat long sentences exactly in Listen and Repeat
- Judge your own recordings against the ETS descriptors for scores 3, 4 and 5

## The Speaking section in numbers

From ETS's blueprint and overview:

- **Listen and Repeat:** 7 sentences in a campus or academic scenario. The sentences get longer and more complex. You have a maximum of **8 to 12 seconds** to record each repetition, and you repeat **only once**.
- **Take an Interview:** a simulated conversation with a pre-recorded interviewer, in a situation such as a scholarship application or a research study. **4 questions, 45 seconds** to answer each.
- **Scoring:** all **11 items are scored 0–5**, so the section has **55 raw points**. Listen and Repeat accounts for 35 of them and the interview for 20.
- **Timing:** about **8 minutes** for the whole section, which comes last in the test.

According to ETS, the earlier interview questions focus on factual information and personal experience, and later questions ask you to express and support opinions on broader issues. The blueprint adds that prompts move from brief factual statements toward opinion, explanation, prediction and narration, with difficulty increasing across the task.

## The four question types

Schood uses four labels for the four interview questions. They follow the progression ETS describes; the exact wording on test day will vary.

| Question | Type | What it asks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Recall** | Describe a specific past experience: what happened, why, what you liked |
| 2 | **Reaction** | How you usually feel or act in a situation, and why |
| 3 | **Position** | Do you agree with a general claim about people or society? |
| 4 | **Proposal** | Should an institution (a university, a city, a government) do something? |

The first two are about **you**; the last two are about **the world**. Recognising the type in the first two seconds tells you which framework to use.

## The 45-second shape

Every framework below follows the same timing shape:

| Seconds | Move |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Direct answer in one sentence |
| 5–20 | Main reason or main detail, explained |
| 20–38 | One specific example, story or consequence |
| 38–45 | Closing line that links back to the question |

Three rules:

- **Start within about two seconds.** Long silences at the start are expensive. Your first sentence can simply restate the question as an answer.
- **One reason, developed,** is better than three reasons listed. The 4 and 5 descriptors are about elaboration, not number of points.
- **Finish your sentence when time ends.** If the timer stops you mid-sentence, nothing terrible happens, but a closing line at around 38–40 seconds makes the answer feel complete.

## Framework 1: Recall

**Structure:** *When and where → what happened → one vivid detail → why it mattered to you.*

Useful openings:
- *The last time I… was in [month], when I…*
- *One experience that comes to mind is…*

Useful development:
- *What I remember most is…*
- *The reason I decided to… was that…*
- *It mattered to me because…*

**Common trap:** describing a general habit ("I usually travel in summer") when the question asks for one specific occasion. Choose one event and stay with it.

## Framework 2: Reaction

**Structure:** *My usual reaction → the cause → a moment that shows it → a nuance or exception.*

Useful openings:
- *I'd say I'm definitely someone who…*
- *Honestly, my first reaction is usually…*

Useful development:
- *I think that's because…*
- *For example, last month…*
- *That said, it depends on…*

**Common trap:** stating the reaction and stopping. The question almost always has a *why* part; the cause is where your elaboration lives.

## Framework 3: Position

**Structure:** *Verdict → main reason → example or evidence → brief concession → restate.*

Useful openings:
- *I mostly agree with that, because…*
- *I don't think that's true for everyone. In my view…*

Useful development:
- *The main reason is…*
- *You can see this in…*
- *Of course, some people… but even so…*

**Common trap:** answering "it depends" without ever choosing. You can qualify your view, but give one clearly.

## Framework 4: Proposal

**Structure:** *Yes or no → the benefit or harm → how it would work in practice → a condition or cost.*

Useful openings:
- *Yes, I think universities should do that, mainly because…*
- *I'm not convinced that's a good idea, because…*

Useful development:
- *In practice, that would mean…*
- *For example, they could…*
- *The only condition I'd add is…*

**Common trap:** talking only about values ("it is good for society") without a mechanism. Say how the proposal would actually work or fail.

## The model interview

The twelve model answers below respond to one original interview, written for Schood in the style ETS describes.

**Scenario:** *You have agreed to take part in a university research study about how students spend their free time. A researcher will interview you online.*

1. **Recall:** "Tell me about a time recently when you learned a new skill outside of your classes. What was it, and why did you decide to learn it?"
2. **Reaction:** "Some people like to plan their free time carefully, while others prefer to decide at the last minute. Which kind of person are you, and why?"
3. **Position:** "Some people say that hobbies are more valuable when you do them with other people rather than alone. Do you agree? Why or why not?"
4. **Proposal:** "Some universities give students academic credit for activities outside the classroom, such as volunteering or team sports. Should universities do this? Why or why not?"

**How to read the levels:** the labels band 4, band 5 and band 6 are Schood's editorial illustrations of performance consistent with the ETS descriptors for B2, C1 and C2 speaking. They are not ETS-rated samples. Transcripts include natural fillers; ETS's own score-5 samples contain *um* and *uh* too.

## Question 1 at band 4

> Um, recently I learned how to cook some, uh, dishes from my country. I learned it from videos on the internet. I decided to learn because I live in dormitory now and I miss my mother food. So I watched videos and I tried to cook plov, it's rice with meat. First time it was not very good, but second time it was better. I think cooking is useful skill because you can save money and also it's healthy. So, yes, I'm happy I learned it.

**Commentary:** The answer is on topic and has a clear sequence, which is what the scoring guide's 3–4 range rewards. Pausing is noticeable but does not block meaning. What holds it at this level: small but frequent grammar slips (*in dormitory*, *my mother food*, *useful skill*), simple sentence patterns joined with *and* and *so*, and a general final reason. To move up: fix articles and possessives, and replace the general ending with a specific detail.

## Question 1 at band 5

> The most recent one was learning to cook plov, which is a rice dish from Uzbekistan. I moved into a dormitory in September, and after a few weeks of cafeteria food I really started to miss home cooking. So I called my mother, and she talked me through it on a video call while I cooked. The first attempt was, um, a bit of a disaster, because I added the rice too early and it turned into porridge. But by the third try my roommates were actually asking for seconds. What I liked most was that it gave me a reason to call home every weekend, so it turned into more than just a cooking skill.

**Commentary:** Well elaborated and clearly organised: when, why, how, one vivid detail (the rice turning into porridge), and why it mattered. Grammar and vocabulary are accurate and varied (a relative clause, *talked me through it*, *asking for seconds*). The one filler is natural. This is the profile of a 5 on the task guide: on topic, well elaborated, good pace, clear meaning.

## Question 1 at band 6

> Just last month, actually, I taught myself to repair my own bicycle. It started out of pure frustration: my gears kept slipping on the hill up to campus, and the only repair shop nearby had a two-week waiting list. So I found a mechanic's channel online, borrowed a set of tools from a neighbour, and spent a Saturday afternoon taking the derailleur apart on my balcony. What surprised me was how logical it all was once I understood that the problem was just one cable that had stretched. Now I do small repairs for a couple of friends, and, more importantly, I've lost that slightly helpless feeling I used to have whenever something mechanical broke.

**Commentary:** Fluent and precise from the first word. The answer narrates with control (*It started out of pure frustration*), uses less common vocabulary accurately (*derailleur*, *stretched*), and ends with a reflective point that is specific rather than general (*that slightly helpless feeling*). This matches the C2 description of clear, smoothly flowing and memorable descriptions.

## Question 2 at band 4

> I think I am a person who plans. Because I am student, I have many classes and homework, so I need to plan. If I don't plan, I forget things. For example, on weekend I write what I will do on Saturday and Sunday, like study, sport, and meet friends. But sometimes my friends call me and say, let's go somewhere now, and I go, so I am not always planning. I think planning is better for students.

**Commentary:** Answers the question and gives a reason and an example, so the content is relevant. It stays at this level because the sentences are short and similar, articles are missing (*I am student*, *on weekend*), and the nuance at the end is mentioned but not developed. To move up: join ideas with a wider range of connectors and develop the exception into a real point.

## Question 2 at band 5

> I'd say I'm mostly a planner, but not in a strict way. During the week my schedule is so full with classes and my part-time job that if I don't plan my free time, it simply disappears. So on Sunday evenings I block out two or three things I actually want to do, like going to the climbing gym on Wednesday. Having it on the calendar makes me protect that time. That said, I leave Saturdays completely open on purpose, because some of the best evenings I've had were last-minute plans with friends. So I'd call myself a planner with one free day.

**Commentary:** Clear position, a convincing cause (a full schedule), a specific habit (Sunday planning, the climbing gym) and a developed nuance (the open Saturday). Connectors (*so*, *that said*, *because*) carry the logic. A typical 5: elaborated, coherent, accurate.

## Question 2 at band 6

> I'm a planner by necessity rather than by temperament. Left to myself, I'd happily drift through a weekend, but I've noticed that unplanned free time tends to get swallowed by small obligations: laundry, emails, a quick errand that takes two hours. So I schedule my free time almost as seriously as my classes. On Thursday nights, for instance, I book a tennis court for Saturday morning, and once it's paid for, I go. The irony is that planning is what makes me feel spontaneous, because the things I actually care about are already protected, and everything else can be decided on the day.

**Commentary:** The opening contrast (*by necessity rather than by temperament*) is precise and immediately sets up a nuanced answer. Vocabulary is idiomatic and exact (*drift*, *swallowed by small obligations*), and the closing irony is a genuinely interesting idea expressed with ease. This is the C2 profile: precise formulation, no sign of restricting what the speaker wants to say.

## Question 3 at band 4

> Yes, I agree. When you do hobby with other people, it is more fun. For example, I play football with my friends every week. We talk, we laugh, and we become closer. If I play alone, it is boring. Also with other people you can learn from them. But some hobbies, like reading, you do alone, and it is also good. So I think both is OK, but with people is better.

**Commentary:** A clear position and an example, which keeps it on topic. It stays here because of agreement errors (*both is*), missing articles and a closing line that wavers between positions. Rhythm is choppy because almost every sentence is the same length. To move up: develop one reason fully and keep one clear position.

## Question 3 at band 5

> I partly agree. For hobbies that involve skill, like music or sport, doing them with others definitely adds value, because other people push you further than you'd go alone. When I started playing in a small band at my school, I improved faster in three months than I had in a year of practising by myself, simply because I didn't want to let the others down. But I don't think it's true for every hobby. Something like drawing or reading is valuable precisely because it's solitary; it's one of the few times I'm not responding to anyone. So it depends on what the hobby is for.

**Commentary:** A qualified position stated clearly, a reason with a mechanism (*push you further*, *let the others down*), a specific example and a well-developed contrast. The ending gives a reasoned principle rather than a vague *it depends*. Strong control of grammar and vocabulary: a typical 5.

## Question 3 at band 6

> I'd push back on that a little, because it assumes the value of a hobby is social, and often it isn't. Some hobbies are clearly richer in company: it's hard to imagine choir singing or five-a-side football on your own. But for many people, a hobby is the one part of the day where nobody is evaluating them. My grandfather builds model ships, alone, for hours, and he'd tell you the solitude is the whole point; it's the only time his mind is completely quiet. So I'd say the question isn't whether a hobby is shared, but whether it gives you something your working life doesn't.

**Commentary:** The speaker challenges the assumption behind the claim, which is a sophisticated move delivered naturally. The example is concrete and memorable, and the final reframing is precise. Grammar and vocabulary are wide-ranging and controlled throughout, with natural rhythm implied by the contrast structures.

## Question 4 at band 4

> I think yes, universities should give credit. Volunteering and sport are good for students. They learn teamwork and responsibility. Also, some students don't have time for these activities because they study a lot, so if they get credit, they will do it more. For example, my friend volunteers in a hospital and she learned many things. So I think it is good idea.

**Commentary:** A clear answer with a reason and an example. It stays in the middle range because the elaboration is general (*learned many things*), there is no explanation of how credit would work, and some grammar slips remain (*it is good idea*). To move up: add a practical mechanism and one specific detail about the friend's experience.

## Question 4 at band 5

> Yes, I think they should, as long as the credit is connected to something students actually learn. Activities like volunteering or team sports teach skills that classes don't, such as handling responsibility or working with people you didn't choose. If universities gave, say, one course credit for a semester of regular volunteering, more students would be able to fit it into their schedules instead of treating it as an extra. To keep it fair, though, students should have to write a short reflection or have a supervisor confirm their hours. Otherwise, it would just become an easy way to collect credits.

**Commentary:** Answer, benefit, mechanism and condition: the full proposal framework, delivered clearly. The conditional (*If universities gave…, more students would…*) and the concession (*To keep it fair, though…*) show range. A typical 5.

## Question 4 at band 6

> I'd support it, but only if the credit rewards reflection rather than attendance. The case for it is strong: some of the most useful things students learn, like managing a team or dealing with someone in distress, happen outside lectures, and right now students who can't afford to give up paid work are effectively excluded from them. Credit would change that calculation. The risk is that it turns volunteering into box-ticking, where people log hours without engaging. So I'd tie the credit to a short piece of work afterwards, explaining what they learned and what they'd do differently. That keeps the academic value without making it bureaucratic.

**Commentary:** The answer adds a perspective that is easy to miss (students who must work are excluded), names a risk precisely (*box-ticking*), and proposes a concrete safeguard. Vocabulary is exact (*change that calculation*, *bureaucratic*) and the argument flows without visible effort, consistent with the C2 descriptor.

## What separates the three levels

| Feature | Band 4 | Band 5 | Band 6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elaboration | A reason and a general example | A reason with a mechanism and a specific example | A reframed or nuanced point, precise and memorable |
| Grammar | Simple patterns, frequent small slips | Varied structures, accurate | Wide range, controlled, flexible |
| Vocabulary | Common words, some wrong forms | Appropriate and some idiomatic | Precise and idiomatic, with connotation |
| Delivery | Noticeable pauses, choppy rhythm | Good pace, natural pauses | Smooth flow, pauses only to choose words |

Use this table to judge your own recordings. **Change one row at a time.**

## Routine 1: thought groups

Natural English is spoken in **thought groups**: short chunks of meaning separated by tiny pauses, each with one main stressed word.

> *When I moved into the dormitory* / *I really started to miss* / *home cooking.*

Practice:

1. Take a model answer from this guide.
2. Mark thought groups with slashes and underline one stressed word in each.
3. Read it aloud three times, pausing only at the slashes.
4. Record the third reading and listen: are the pauses at the slashes, or in the middle of phrases?

Pauses inside a thought group sound like searching; pauses between thought groups sound like thinking. The scoring guide's "appropriate and natural use of pauses" is exactly this difference.

## Routine 2: stress and intonation

Rhythm and intonation that convey meaning are named in the score-5 description. Practise three patterns:

- **Contrast stress:** "It's not the *money*, it's the *time*." Stress the two contrasting words.
- **New information stress:** in "I learned to cook *plov*", the new word carries the stress.
- **Falling intonation for statements and conclusions,** and a slight rise on items in a list before the last one: "I need *time*↗, *money*↗ and a *plan*↘."

Practise with short sentences until the stress is automatic, then read a model answer and exaggerate the patterns. Exaggeration in practice becomes natural emphasis in the test.

## Routine 3: shadowing

Shadowing means speaking along with a recording, a fraction of a second behind it.

1. Choose 30–60 seconds of clear natural speech: a lecture, a podcast or an interview.
2. Listen once without speaking.
3. Shadow it three times, copying the rhythm and intonation, not just the words.
4. Record your last attempt and compare.

Rotate North American, British and Australian speakers, because the test's audio uses all three. Ten minutes of shadowing a day trains both fluency and the listening speed that Listen and Repeat needs.

## Routine 4: the shrinking timer

This routine trains fluency by repeating the same answer under less time:

1. Answer a practice question in **60 seconds**.
2. Answer the same question again in **45 seconds**, keeping all the content.
3. Answer it a third time in **35 seconds**.
4. Answer it once more at **45 seconds** and record it.

The final 45-second version is usually more fluent and better organised than the first, because the structure and words have become automatic. Use a new question every day.

## Routine 5: Listen and Repeat accuracy

Listen and Repeat is worth more raw points than the interview, and its scoring guide is strict: a 5 is an exact, fully intelligible repetition; a 4 has minor changes such as a missing function word or tense marker; a 3 keeps most content words but changes meaning.

Daily routine (10 minutes):

1. Collect sentences of increasing length from campus or academic contexts: directions, rules, schedules, announcements.
2. Play each sentence once, wait one second, repeat it once, record.
3. Compare word by word with the script. Mark every changed or missing word.
4. Count **function words** (*the, a, to, of, have*) and **endings** (*-ed, -s, -ing*) separately; these are what move a 5 to a 4.
5. Repeat the hardest five sentences the next day.

Hear long sentences as thought groups, not as strings of words: *"For students with a parking permit / the lot behind the gym / is open until ten."* The [Listen and Repeat](/speaking-listen-and-repeat) module covers the task in detail.

## Recovery language

Mistakes happen in every recording. What matters is that you keep going smoothly:

- **Self-correction:** *I went there in June, sorry, in July…* Keep it short and continue.
- **Buying a second:** *That's a good question.* / *Let me think about that for a moment.* Use once, not in every answer.
- **Finding a word:** *It's a kind of… a tool you use for…* Paraphrase rather than stopping.
- **Restarting a sentence:** *What I mean is…*

ETS's score-5 descriptor allows natural pauses; its score-3 descriptor mentions frequent or lengthy pauses and frequent fillers. The difference is frequency and length, not whether a filler ever appears.

## Recording and self-scoring

Record every practice answer. Once a day, score one recording against this checklist:

- **On topic:** did I answer the actual question in my first sentence?
- **Elaboration:** one reason with a mechanism, and one specific example?
- **Pace:** did I keep talking until about 40 seconds without long gaps?
- **Intelligibility:** were any words hard to understand when I listened back?
- **Language:** how many grammar slips did I hear? Which ones repeat?

Write the answers in your error log using the speaking categories from the [Band 4 to 5 Roadmap](/guide-band-4-to-5-roadmap): S-exact, S-flow and S-develop. Use the [simulator](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) for timed sets with the real interface.

## The memorised-answer trap {callout}

**Prepare frameworks, not paragraphs**
A memorised answer that does not match the question is worse than an imperfect real answer: it is off topic, its rhythm sounds recited, and its language often comes from someone else. Prepare your frameworks, your connectors and a few true personal stories that can be adapted, and build each answer fresh.

## Frequently asked questions {faq}

### Do I lose points for saying "um"?
ETS's own score-5 sample answers contain fillers such as "um" and "uh", and the scoring comment says they are natural and do not interfere with meaning. Frequent or lengthy pauses and fillers are mentioned at the score-3 level. Aim to reduce them, not to eliminate them.

### What if I cannot think of a real experience?
A plausible, specific example is better than a general statement. Keep a short list of true stories (a course you liked, a trip, a skill you learned, a problem you solved) that can answer many Recall and Reaction questions.

### How fast should I speak?
Speak at a good conversational pace that you can control. Speed itself is not the goal; ETS describes a good conversational pace with natural pauses. Fast speech with errors and unclear words scores lower than steady, clear speech.

### Should I use advanced vocabulary?
Use words you can pronounce clearly and use correctly. Precise, natural words help; difficult words used wrongly hurt intelligibility and accuracy.

### What happens if the 45 seconds end in the middle of my sentence?
The recording stops. Plan your closing line for around 38–40 seconds so that the answer ends naturally.
