## What this guide adds

The public [Complete the Words](/reading-complete-the-words) module explains the task. This guide is the training book. It gives you:

- a **five-step routine** that turns every blank into a small logic problem instead of a guess
- the **clues** that solve most blanks: the visible letters, the number of missing letters, the grammar slot, the word family and the collocation
- **twelve original paragraphs**, written for Schood in the format ETS describes, graded from B1 to C1+
- an **answer key for every paragraph** with a short explanation for each of the 120 missing words

Work through the drills in order. Each level adds harder vocabulary and longer, more academic sentences. Do not open an answer key until you have written all ten answers for that paragraph.

## What you will be able to do {checklist}

- Use the visible letters and the number of blanks to narrow every gap to a few candidates
- Decide the part of speech a gap needs from its position in the sentence
- Choose the right ending from a word family: -tion, -ment, -ive, -al, -ly, -ed, -ing
- Recognise fixed phrases and collocations that complete themselves
- Avoid the spelling traps that cost points even when you know the word
- Review your misses by error type and build your own drills

## How the task works

ETS's overview of the updated test describes Complete the Words like this, in summary:

- You see an **academic paragraph of about 70–100 words**.
- The **first sentence is complete**.
- After that, **the second half of every second word is removed**, and you type the missing letters.
- **Each paragraph has 10 words with missing letters.**

In the blueprint, Complete the Words sits in the Reading claim "process academic written texts for meaning and form", targets **CEFR B1 to C1+**, and accounts for **30 target items**, each worth a maximum of 1 point. It assesses word-ordering rules, lexical knowledge and reading comprehension together, which is why grammar and vocabulary both matter.

Reading is two-stage adaptive, so the paragraphs you see later depend on how you did earlier. The blueprint estimates the first stage at 18–21 minutes and the second at 9 minutes for the whole Reading section; ETS does not publish a separate time for each paragraph.

## The letter rule

Look closely at ETS's sample paragraph and a pattern appears in every gap: **the visible part is the first half of the word, rounded down, and each missing letter is shown as its own blank.**

| Word length | Visible | Missing | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 | 1 | is → i + s |
| 4 | 2 | 2 | from → fr + om |
| 5 | 2 | 3 | might → mi + ght |
| 6 | 3 | 3 | record → rec + ord |
| 7 | 3 | 4 | however → how + ever |

This gives you two clues before you even think about meaning:

- **The visible letters** tell you how the word starts.
- **The number of blanks** tells you its exact length. If the visible part is *bec* and there are three blanks, the word has six letters: *became*, not *because*.

Schood's drills below follow the same rule, so you can practise counting blanks.

## The five-step routine

Use the same five steps for every blank until they are automatic:

1. **Read the first sentence fully.** It sets the topic and often contains the word family you will need later (*printing* in sentence one, *printed* in a blank).
2. **Read the whole gapped sentence once** before filling anything. Many gaps are decided by a word that comes after them.
3. **Name the slot:** noun, verb, adjective, adverb, preposition, article, pronoun or conjunction.
4. **Generate candidates** from the visible letters and the blank count, then choose the one that fits the slot, the grammar (number, tense) and the meaning.
5. **Check the form:** ending, agreement and spelling. Then move on. Return to doubtful gaps only if you have time.

The most common mistake is jumping to step 4 without steps 2 and 3. A word can start with the right letters, have the right length and still be the wrong part of speech.

## Clue 1: what the slot needs

Position in the sentence tells you the part of speech:

- **After an article or determiner** (*a, the, this, each, their*): a noun, or an adjective followed by a noun.
- **After a subject, before an object:** a verb, and its form must agree with the subject.
- **Between *be* or *have* and something else:** a participle (*been, revised, caused*) or an adjective.
- **Before an adjective or a past participle:** often an adverb ending in *-ly* (*widely documented, relatively abundant*).
- **Before a noun phrase that expresses time, place or means:** a preposition (*during, through, from*).
- **At the start of a clause after a comma:** a conjunction or relative word (*while, which, whether*).

When you are unsure, say the sentence with a placeholder: "the ___ rock". A placeholder that is clearly a describing word tells you the gap is an adjective.

## Clue 2: word families and suffixes

Many Complete the Words gaps at B2 and above test **which form of a word family** fits. Learn endings by the job they do:

| Job | Common endings | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (idea or process) | -tion, -sion, -ment, -ence, -ance, -ity, -ness | assumption, achievement, difference, capacity |
| Noun (person) | -er, -or, -ist, -ant, -ee | worker, merchant, employee |
| Adjective | -al, -ive, -ous, -ful, -able, -ible, -ic | neural, expensive, enormous, typical |
| Adverb | -ly, -ally (after -ic) | widely, relatively, automatically, specifically |
| Verb | -ize, -ify, -en, -ate | reorganize, specify, widen, indicate |
| Participle | -ed, -ing | printed, competing, ruling, underlying |

Two fast checks:

- **-ic adjectives take -ally as adverbs:** *automatic → automatically*, *specific → specifically*, *typical → typically*.
- **Visible letters and blank count work together:** the visible part is always the first half of the whole word, so *difference* (10 letters) appears as *diffe* plus five blanks, while *different* (9 letters) would appear as *diff* plus five blanks. Together they fix the exact form.

## Clue 3: agreement and form

When the slot is a verb or a noun, check the small endings:

- **Third-person -s:** *it returns*, *one worker discovers*, *a glacier advances*.
- **No -s with plural subjects:** *the algae provide*, *experiments indicate*, *humans possess*.
- **Passive needs a participle:** *is caused by*, *be copied by*, *has been revised*.
- **Plural nouns after quantity words and without articles:** *patches of flowers*, *free classes for adults*.
- **Uncountable nouns stay singular:** *sediment*, *information*, *error* in *prone to error*.

These endings are exactly what a fast reader skips. Build the habit of checking the last letters of every completed word.

## Clue 4: collocations and fixed phrases

A large share of gaps sits inside phrases that almost complete themselves once you know them:

- **Fixed expressions:** *as well as*, *such as*, *known as*, *by hand*, *in partnership with*, *in response to*.
- **Verb plus preposition:** *recover from*, *enrolled in*, *prone to*, *carry away*, *return to*.
- **Verb plus noun:** *offer classes*, *perform a dance*, *raise wages*, *provide food*.
- **Adjective plus noun:** *competing explanations*, *underlying rock*, *ruling families*, *default options*.

When you meet a new academic word in your reading, record it with its common partner, not alone: write *prone to error*, not *prone*. Complete the Words rewards partners.

## Clue 5: the small words

Every paragraph has gaps on short, frequent words. They look easy but cause careless misses because several words share the same visible letter:

- **i + 1 blank:** *is, it, in, if*. Decide by grammar: a verb (*is*), a subject (*it*), a preposition (*in*) or a condition (*if*).
- **a + 1 blank:** *an, as, at, am*. *An* before a vowel sound; *as* in *such as, known as, as well as*.
- **o + 1 blank:** *of, on, or*. *Of* after quantities (*many of*) and in *the power of*.
- **t + 2 blanks:** *the, two, too*. Usually *the*.
- **th + 2 blanks:** *that, this, then, than, they*. *That* introduces a clause after *is, argue, predicts, indicate*.
- **wh + 3 blanks:** *which, while, where*. *Which* begins a relative clause about a noun; *while* begins a contrasting or simultaneous clause.

## Spelling traps

You can know the word and still lose the point. Watch for:

- **Doubled letters:** *grammar*, *classes*, *occurred*, *committee*, *recommend*.
- **y to i:** *copied*, *quantities*, *studies*.
- **-ible or -able, -ence or -ance:** *possible, visible* but *available, reliable*; *difference, evidence* but *importance, performance*.
- **Silent letters:** *through*, *scientist*, *knowledge*.
- **American spelling:** ETS materials use American forms such as *recognize* and *organization*. Schood's drills do the same: *reorganize*, *color*, *behavior*.

Type the full word in your head before typing the missing letters; spelling errors often come from typing only the end.

## Pacing

ETS does not publish a time per paragraph, so set your own target from evidence:

- In your first three drills, **time yourself** without pressure and write the time next to the score.
- Then set a target about 20% faster than your average, and keep accuracy at 9 or 10 out of 10.
- If accuracy drops below 8 out of 10 at the faster time, go back to the slower time for a week.

The point of speed is to leave time for the other Reading tasks. Accuracy on Complete the Words always comes first, because every item is worth the same single point.

## How to use the twelve drills

- **Level 1 (B1), drills 1–3:** everyday academic topics, common words, mostly function words and simple forms.
- **Level 2 (B2), drills 4–7:** more word-family and passive forms, fixed academic phrases.
- **Level 3 (C1), drills 8–10:** longer academic words, adverbs, abstract nouns.
- **Level 4 (C1+), drills 11–12:** dense academic prose with long nominalisations.

For each drill: write all ten answers, check against the key, then log every miss with its type (slot, family, agreement, collocation, small word, spelling). One drill a day for twelve days is a good rhythm; repeat the drills you scored below 9 a week later.

## Drill 1 (Level 1, B1): Public libraries {cloze}

Topic: how libraries have changed.

```cloze
Public libraries have changed a great deal over the last fifty years. Many of them now lend laptops as well as books, and some even offer free classes for adults who want to learn new skills. In small towns, the library is often the only place where people can use the internet without paying. Librarians say that visitor numbers have stayed high, although the reasons for visiting are different from the past.
---
of: Quantity pattern "many of + pronoun". With one blank after "o", only "of", "on" and "or" are possible, and only "many of them" is English.
now: An adverb between the subject and the verb "lend". "New" is an adjective and "not" would need "do not", so "now" is the only fit.
laptops: Something a library lends, plural to match "books" in the same "as well as" pair.
well: The fixed expression "as well as", meaning "and also".
books: Plural noun parallel with "laptops"; the traditional thing a library lends.
some: A pronoun subject meaning "some libraries", followed by the verb "offer".
offer: Verb after the adverb "even"; "offer classes" is a common collocation.
classes: Plural noun with no article; "free classes for adults" are lessons.
adults: A person noun, signalled by "who"; plural because there is no article.
want: Plural present verb after "who" (referring to adults), followed by "to learn".
```

## Drill 2 (Level 1, B1): Sleep and memory {cloze}

Topic: why sleep may help learning.

```cloze
Scientists have long wondered why the body needs so much sleep. One idea is that sleep gives the brain time to sort the information it collected during the day. In experiments, students who slept after learning a list of words remembered more of them the next morning than students who stayed awake. The effect was strongest for words that were difficult or unusual. Researchers now think that deep sleep and dreaming sleep may play different roles in this process.
---
idea: A noun after "One" introducing a theory: "One idea is that…".
that: "is that + clause" introduces the content of the idea. "This", "then" and "than" cannot introduce the clause.
gives: The pattern "give someone time to do something"; third-person -s because the subject "sleep" is singular.
brain: The organ that sorts information; "br" plus three letters.
to: "time to + verb", here "time to sort".
the: Definite article: the information is specific, defined by "it collected".
it: The relative clause needs a subject ("the information it collected"). "Is" would make "sort the information is collected", which is ungrammatical.
during: A preposition of time before "the day".
day: "During the day" contrasts with sleep at night.
experiments: A plural noun after "In" that introduces evidence. The word has eleven letters, so five are visible ("exper") and six are missing ("iments").
```

## Drill 3 (Level 1, B1): How bees share directions {cloze}

Topic: animal communication.

```cloze
Honeybees communicate with each other in a surprising way. When one worker discovers rich patches of flowers, it returns to the hive and then performs a short dance. The direction of the dance shows the other bees which way to fly, and the length of the dance tells them how far away the food is. Researchers first described this behavior in the twentieth century. Later studies showed that bees from different regions use slightly different versions of the dance.
---
one: A determiner before the singular noun "worker": a single bee. "Our" does not fit the meaning.
discovers: A present-simple verb with -s after "one worker". Nine letters: "disc" plus five blanks. "Discusses" has the wrong meaning and length.
patches: "Patches of flowers" are small areas; plural with "rich" and no article.
flowers: What bees look for; plural after "of".
returns: "Return to" a place; -s agrees with "it".
the: Definite article before "hive", the bee's home.
and: Joins two actions of the same subject: returns… and then performs.
performs: "Perform a dance" is the collocation; -s agrees with "it". Eight letters: "perf" plus four.
short: An adjective between "a" and "dance".
The: The article that begins the new sentence, with a capital letter.
```

## Drill 4 (Level 2, B2): The urban heat island {cloze}

Topic: why cities are warmer.

```cloze
Cities are often several degrees warmer than the countryside around them. This difference, known as the urban heat island effect, is mainly caused by surfaces such as asphalt and concrete, which absorb sunlight during the day and release it slowly at night. Planting trees and painting roofs in lighter colors can reduce the problem, but these measures work best when they are applied across entire neighborhoods. Some cities now require new buildings to include green roofs for this reason.
---
difference: A noun after "This" that refers back to "warmer". The adjective "different" cannot follow "This" alone, and the blank count (five) gives "-rence".
as: "Known as" means "called".
urban: The fixed term "urban heat island", matching "Cities".
island: Part of the same fixed term.
is: The passive "is caused"; singular to agree with "This difference".
caused: Past participle in the passive "caused by".
surfaces: A plural noun followed by examples ("such as asphalt and concrete").
as: "Such as" introduces examples.
and: Joins the two examples.
which: A non-defining relative clause after a comma takes "which", not "that". The plural verb "absorb" agrees with "surfaces".
```

## Drill 5 (Level 2, B2): Printing and knowledge {cloze}

Topic: early printing in Europe.

```cloze
The spread of printing in fifteenth-century Europe changed how knowledge traveled. Before printed books became common, texts had to be copied by hand, which was slow, expensive and prone to error. Printing allowed identical copies to circulate widely, and this made it easier for scholars in different cities to compare their ideas. Historians still debate, however, how quickly ordinary people benefited from the change. Many people could not read, and books remained costly for decades.
---
printed: A participle used as an adjective, from the family of "printing" in sentence one. "Printing books" would describe the act, not the books.
became: "Become common", past tense for history. The blank count matters: "bec" plus three blanks is "became"; "because" would need four.
texts: A plural subject for "had to be copied".
to: "Had to + verb".
copied: Passive past participle; "y" changes to "i" before -ed.
hand: The fixed phrase "by hand".
was: Singular, because "which" refers to the whole process of copying.
expensive: An adjective in a list of adjectives ("slow, expensive and prone"). The noun "expense" does not fit.
prone: "Prone to" means likely to suffer something.
error: "Prone to error" uses "error" as an uncountable idea, with no article.
```

## Drill 6 (Level 2, B2): Coral partnerships {cloze}

Topic: how coral reefs live.

```cloze
Coral reefs cover only a small fraction of the ocean floor, yet they support an enormous variety of marine life. The reefs are built by tiny animals that live in partnership with algae. The algae provide food through photosynthesis, while the corals offer shelter and nutrients in return. When water temperatures rise too far, this relationship breaks down, and the corals lose their color in a process called bleaching. Reefs can recover if conditions improve quickly enough.
---
reefs: Repeats "coral reefs" from sentence one; plural with "are".
built: The irregular past participle of "build" in the passive "are built by".
tiny: An adjective before "animals"; four letters.
that: Begins a defining relative clause about the animals.
in: The fixed phrase "in partnership with".
with: Completes "in partnership with".
The: The article that begins the next sentence.
provide: A plural verb without -s, because "algae" is plural. Count the blanks: "pro" plus four is "provide"; "provides" would need five.
through: "Through" means "by means of" before "photosynthesis".
while: A conjunction that joins two simultaneous contributions. "Which" cannot start a clause that has its own subject ("the corals").
```

## Drill 7 (Level 2, B2): The power of defaults {cloze}

Topic: behavioral economics.

```cloze
People do not always make choices in the way that traditional economic models predict. One widely documented pattern is the power of default options. When employees are automatically enrolled in workplace savings plans and must actively choose to leave them, participation tends to be higher than when they must actively choose to join. Economists interpret this as evidence that effort and inertia shape decisions alongside careful calculation. Governments have used this insight when designing pension schemes.
---
widely: An adverb before the participle "documented", so it needs -ly.
pattern: A singular noun after "documented" and before "is".
the: Definite article in "the power of".
of: Completes "the power of".
options: A plural noun after "default"; "default options" are choices made for you unless you change them.
employees: Plural with "are". "Employers" has the same length, but people enrolled in workplace savings plans are the staff, so "employees".
automatically: An adverb before "enrolled". "Automatic" is an -ic adjective, so the adverb is -ally.
in: "Enrolled in" a plan or course.
savings: "Savings plans" is a fixed compound; the noun keeps its -s.
and: Joins "are automatically enrolled" and "must actively choose".
```

## Drill 8 (Level 3, C1): How children learn language {cloze}

Topic: theories of first-language acquisition.

```cloze
Children acquire their first language with remarkable speed and without formal instruction. Linguists have proposed competing explanations for this achievement. Some argue that humans possess an innate capacity dedicated specifically to grammar, whereas others maintain that general learning mechanisms, combined with massive exposure to speech, are sufficient. Studies of infants' sensitivity to statistical patterns in sound sequences have strengthened the second position, though they have not settled the debate. Most researchers now accept that both experience and biology contribute.
---
have: Present perfect "have proposed" with the plural subject "Linguists".
competing: A participle adjective: "competing explanations" are rival explanations. "Complex" is too short for the blanks.
for: "An explanation for" something.
achievement: A noun after "this" that refers back to learning a language quickly; the -ment ending.
argue: "Argue that"; no -s after the plural "Some".
humans: A plural subject for "possess".
an: The article "an" before the vowel sound of "innate". "As" and "at" cannot come before a noun phrase here.
capacity: A noun after the adjective "innate"; "capacity" means ability.
specifically: An adverb modifying "dedicated"; "specific" is an -ic adjective, so -ally.
grammar: Spelled with a double "m" and "-ar", not "-er".
```

## Drill 9 (Level 3, C1): Glaciers as sculptors {cloze}

Topic: how ice shapes land.

```cloze
Glaciers shape landscapes far more dramatically than their slow movement might suggest. As each glacier advances, it scrapes the underlying rock and carries away enormous quantities of sediment. When it eventually retreats, it leaves behind distinctive features, including U-shaped valleys, scattered boulders and long ridges of debris. Geologists use these traces to reconstruct the extent of ancient ice sheets, which in turn helps them estimate how climate fluctuated in the distant past.
---
each: A determiner before the singular noun "glacier".
advances: A verb with -s after "each glacier"; its opposite, "retreats", appears later in the paragraph.
scrapes: A verb with -s describing the physical action on rock.
underlying: An adjective meaning "lying underneath", before "rock".
and: Joins two actions of "it": scrapes… and carries.
away: The phrasal verb "carry away".
quantities: "Quantities of" a substance; "y" becomes "ies" in the plural.
sediment: An uncountable noun after "of"; no plural -s.
it: The subject pronoun for the glacier.
retreats: A verb with -s; the opposite of "advances".
```

## Drill 10 (Level 3, C1): Who chose the colors? {cloze}

Topic: Renaissance art patronage.

```cloze
During the Renaissance, most painters worked on commission rather than producing art for an open market. Patrons, whether wealthy merchants, religious institutions or ruling families, typically specified the subject, the dimensions and sometimes even the pigments to be used. Contracts frequently stipulated that expensive blues be applied to particular figures, reflecting the prestige attached to costly materials. Consequently, artistic decisions that modern viewers attribute to individual genius were often negotiated.
---
whether: Introduces a list of alternatives: "whether X, Y or Z".
merchants: A plural person noun after "wealthy".
institutions: A plural -tion noun after "religious".
ruling: A participle adjective: families that ruled.
typically: An adverb before the verb "specified"; the adjective "typical" ends in -al, so the adverb is -ally.
the: The article in "the subject".
the: The article in "the dimensions".
and: Joins the last item of the list.
even: Signals that something is surprising: they chose even the pigments.
pigments: A plural noun: the colored substances used in paint.
```

## Drill 11 (Level 4, C1+): A brain that rewires {cloze}

Topic: neural plasticity.

```cloze
The adult brain was once thought to be largely fixed in its structure. That assumption has been substantially revised. Experiments with musicians and with people recovering from strokes indicate that neural circuits reorganize in response to sustained practice or injury. Nevertheless, plasticity is neither unlimited nor uniformly beneficial; maladaptive changes can entrench chronic pain, and the capacity for reorganization appears to diminish, though not disappear, with age. Rehabilitation programs increasingly try to harness this flexibility.
---
assumption: A noun after "That" referring back to the old belief. The verb is "assume", but the noun has a "p": "assumption".
been: Present perfect passive: "has been revised".
revised: A past participle meaning changed or corrected.
with: "Experiments with" the people studied.
and: Joins the two groups: musicians and people recovering from strokes.
people: The noun after "with", followed by "recovering".
from: "Recover from" an illness.
indicate: A plural verb (the subject is "Experiments") followed by "that".
neural: An adjective meaning "of the nerves", before "circuits".
reorganize: A plural present verb without -s (the subject is "circuits"); American spelling with -ize.
```

## Drill 12 (Level 4, C1+): Trade and inequality {cloze}

Topic: economic theory and evidence.

```cloze
Economists have long debated whether trade between nations narrows or widens inequality within them. Classical theory predicts that opening markets should raise the wages of workers whose skills are relatively abundant, while depressing the earnings of those in scarce supply. Empirical findings, however, have proven considerably messier, partly because technological change and trade liberalization frequently occurred simultaneously, making their respective contributions exceedingly difficult to disentangle. The question remains central to debates about globalization.
---
theory: A singular subject for "predicts". "These" would need a plural noun.
that: "Predicts that + clause".
markets: "Opening markets" means removing barriers to trade.
raise: The transitive verb "raise" takes an object ("the wages"). "Rise" cannot take an object.
wages: "The wages of workers" is the pay they earn.
workers: A person noun, signalled by "whose".
skills: A plural noun with "are".
relatively: An adverb before the adjective "abundant".
while: "While + -ing" sets up a contrast with the first effect.
the: The article in "the earnings of".
```

## Scoring yourself and logging misses

After each drill, write the score out of 10 and the time. Then log **every miss** with one of these labels:

- **SLOT:** wrong part of speech (adjective where a noun was needed)
- **FAMILY:** right word family, wrong ending
- **AGREE:** wrong -s, tense or plural
- **PHRASE:** did not recognise the collocation or fixed phrase
- **SMALL:** wrong short word (*it/is, as/an, that/this*)
- **SPELL:** right word, wrong spelling
- **VOCAB:** did not know the word

After all twelve drills, count the labels. If **SLOT** and **FAMILY** dominate, return to Clues 1 and 2 and make your own drills from academic texts. If **VOCAB** dominates, you need more reading at the level of the drills you failed, with new words recorded in families and with their partners. Copy the counts into your main error log, described in the [Band 4 to 5 Roadmap](/guide-band-4-to-5-roadmap).

## Build your own drills

Once you have used all twelve, make more from any academic text:

1. Choose a paragraph of 70–100 words from a textbook, encyclopaedia or quality news article.
2. Leave the first sentence complete.
3. Starting with the second word of the second sentence, take every second word until you have ten.
4. Keep the first half of each chosen word (rounded down) and replace the rest with one blank per letter.
5. Solve it the next day, so you do not remember the text.

Making drills is itself a useful exercise: choosing the gaps makes you notice word forms. Pair up with a study partner and swap drills for a fair test.

## Never leave a gap empty {callout}

**A blank cannot earn the point**
Each scored Complete the Words item is worth up to one point, and ETS describes no penalty for a wrong answer. If time is short, write your best candidate for every gap from the visible letters and the slot. A reasoned guess often turns out right; an empty gap never does.

## Frequently asked questions {faq}

### Are the drills in this guide real TOEFL texts?
No. All twelve paragraphs were written for Schood in the format ETS describes: about 70–100 words, an intact first sentence, every second word half-removed, ten gaps. They are practice material, not official ETS items.

### Does capitalisation or spelling matter in my answer?
Treat spelling as essential: the task is about form as well as meaning, and the missing letters must make the correct word. Use the spelling the text uses, which in ETS materials is American.

### What if two words fit the same gap?
Use all the clues together. The blank count fixes the length, the slot fixes the part of speech, and agreement fixes the ending. In well-made items only one word satisfies all three and the meaning.

### How many paragraphs will I see on test day?
The blueprint lists 30 target Complete the Words items, and ETS's description says each paragraph has 10 gaps. Because Reading is adaptive, the exact set you see depends on your performance.

### How fast should I be?
ETS does not publish a time per paragraph. Measure your own baseline, then aim to get faster without falling below 9 out of 10. Accuracy is worth more than speed in this task.
