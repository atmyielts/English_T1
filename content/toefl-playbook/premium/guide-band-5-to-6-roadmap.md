## Who this roadmap is for

This guide is for candidates who already score **band 5 or 5.5 overall** on a recent TOEFL iBT or on full mocks in the [Schood simulator](https://toefl.schood.online/full-exam), and who want **band 6**, the top of the 2026 scale and the level ETS aligns to CEFR **C2**.

At this level the usual advice stops working. You already know the format, your grammar is mostly accurate, and you finish sections on time. What separates 5.5 from 6 is **precision and consistency**: fewer misses on the hardest items, fewer small slips in writing and repetition, and speech that is not just fluent but exact and well organised.

Before you start, answer one question honestly: **does your goal actually require 6?** Most institutions publish requirements below the top band, and ETS sets no pass mark. A 6 is a strong signal, but if your target asks for 5 with section minimums, the better use of your time may be protecting those minimums. If you do need 6 (some scholarships, competitive programmes, or personal goals), this roadmap is for you.

## What you will be able to do {checklist}

- Explain exactly what a band 6 overall requires arithmetically
- Locate the task types where C2-level evidence is gathered
- Run a precision audit on two full mocks and measure your variance
- Use advanced drills for stance, style, implication and exact repetition
- Write with qualifying devices that remove ambiguity, as the C2 descriptor describes
- Decide, with evidence, when you are ready to book and when MyBest scores help

## Band 6 on the 2026 scale

On ETS's 1–6 scale, **band 6 corresponds to CEFR C2** in every section and overall, and **5–5.5 corresponds to C1**. During the two-year transition, the score report also shows a comparable 0–120 total. ETS's comparison table gives:

| Band | Reading (0–30) | Listening (0–30) | Writing (0–30) | Speaking (0–30) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 29–30 | 28–30 | 29–30 | 28–30 | 114+ |
| 5.5 | 27–28 | 26–27 | 27–28 | 27 | 107+ |
| 5 | 24–26 | 22–25 | 24–26 | 25–26 | 95+ |

Read the top row carefully. On the old scale, band 6 Reading starts at **29 out of 30**. That tells you something important about the new top band: it represents performance with very little room for error. You are not trying to get "a bit better"; you are trying to remove most of your remaining misses.

## The arithmetic of an overall 6

The overall band is the average of the four sections rounded to the nearest half band, and ETS's published example shows a quarter-band average rounding up (5.25 becomes 5.5). So an overall 6 needs an average of **5.75 or more**.

| Reading | Listening | Writing | Speaking | Average | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 6 | 6 | 6 | 6.00 | 6 |
| 6 | 6 | 6 | 5 | 5.75 | 6 |
| 6 | 6 | 5.5 | 5.5 | 5.75 | 6 |
| 6 | 5.5 | 5.5 | 5.5 | 5.625 | 5.5 |
| 6 | 6 | 6 | 4.5 | 5.625 | 5.5 |

Two practical conclusions:

- **You need at least two sections at 6**, and the other two at 5.5 or above, or three at 6 and one at 5.
- **Any section at 4.5 makes an overall 6 impossible**, even with three perfect sections. If one section is below 5, fix that before anything else.

## Where C2 evidence lives in the test

ETS's blueprint gives a CEFR range for every task type. Not every task is built to reach C2:

| Task type | CEFR range in the blueprint |
|---|---|
| Complete the Words | B1–C1+ |
| Read in Daily Life | A1–C1 |
| Read an Academic Passage | B1–C2 |
| Listen and Choose a Response | A1–B2 |
| Listen to a Conversation | A2–C1 |
| Listen to an Announcement | A2–C1 |
| Listen to an Academic Talk | A2–C2 |
| Build a Sentence | A1–C2 |
| Write an Email | B1–C2 |
| Write for an Academic Discussion | B1–C2 |
| Listen and Repeat | A1–C2 |
| Take an Interview | A1–C2 |

What this means for a band 6 candidate:

- In **Reading and Listening**, the tasks with the highest ceiling are the **academic passage and the academic talk**. You cannot afford misses on the easier task types either, but your extra study hours should go to the hardest academic material.
- In **Writing and Speaking**, every task can show C2, so the gains come from raising the quality of every response, especially Listen and Repeat and Build a Sentence, which together carry a large share of raw points (35 of 55 in Speaking and 10 of 20 in Writing, per the blueprint).

## Step 1: the precision audit

At band 5.5, one mock is not enough evidence. Take **two full mocks** in the [simulator](https://toefl.schood.online/full-exam), at least five days apart, under test conditions. Then build an audit table:

| Measure | Mock A | Mock B |
|---|---|---|
| Section bands | | |
| Reading misses by task type | | |
| Listening misses by task type | | |
| Build a Sentence score (of 10) | | |
| Listen and Repeat sentences with any change | | |
| Email and discussion feedback, top issue | | |
| Interview: answers under 35 seconds of content | | |

Then ask three questions:

1. **Where are the misses?** At this level, misses usually concentrate in two or three task types.
2. **Are they the same misses?** If Mock A and Mock B fail in the same places, you have a skill gap. If they fail in different places, you have a **consistency** problem.
3. **What is the variance?** If a section moves a half band between two mocks, that section is not secure, even if one result was a 6.

## Reading: C1 versus C2

ETS's descriptors describe a **C1 reader** as handling lengthy, complex texts and identifying finer points of detail, including attitudes and implied or stated opinions. A **C2 reader** understands a broad range of long and complex texts and appreciates **subtle distinctions of style**, identifying both implicit and explicit meaning.

In practice the C1 to C2 difference in Reading appears in questions that ask:

- why the author includes a particular example or sentence
- how two paragraphs relate (contrast, concession, elaboration, evidence for a claim)
- what a word means in this specific context, when its everyday meaning would mislead
- what the author implies about a position without stating it

C1 readers are usually right about **what** a text says and sometimes wrong about **why** it says it. C2 readers are reliable on both.

## Reading: advanced drills

- **Stance mapping.** For every academic passage you practise, mark each claim as the author's view, a reported view, or evidence. Then answer the questions. Many advanced misses come from choosing a reported view as the author's.
- **Function labels.** Write a one-word function next to each sentence: *claim, example, concession, contrast, consequence, definition*. When a question asks why something is mentioned, your label is usually the answer.
- **Context-meaning drill.** Collect words whose usual meaning differs from their academic meaning (*account for, qualify, arrest, sound, treat, figure*). Write one sentence for each sense.
- **Complete the Words at speed and 100% accuracy.** At band 6, Complete the Words misses should be close to zero. Use the hardest paragraphs in the [Complete the Words Drills](/guide-complete-the-words-drills) guide under a strict time limit and treat any miss as a priority log entry.
- **Read above the test.** Add 20 minutes a day of long-form academic or high-quality journalistic reading. The test's academic passages are around 200 words; reading much longer, denser texts makes them feel short.

## Listening: C1 versus C2

ETS describes a **C1 listener** as skilled at using contextual, grammatical and lexical cues to infer attitude, mood and intentions and to anticipate what comes next, while possibly needing to confirm occasional details with an unfamiliar accent. A **C2 listener** makes appropriate inferences even when the links or implications are not made explicit.

So the C2 step is about **implicit connections**:

- the speaker never says "therefore", but the second point is a consequence of the first
- the speaker mentions an example without saying what it illustrates
- the tone (hedging, mild irony, enthusiasm) changes the meaning of a literal statement
- an announcement implies an action the listener must take without stating it as an instruction

## Listening: advanced drills

- **Silent-link drill.** Listen to an academic talk once, then write the logical relationship between each pair of points (cause, contrast, example, exception) even where no signal word was used. Check against the transcript.
- **Tone labelling.** For short dialogues, label the speaker's attitude in one word (*sceptical, reluctant, relieved, apologetic*). Choose a Response items often hinge on this.
- **Unfamiliar-accent block.** Identify your weakest of North American, British and Australian English and give it two extra sessions a week for a month.
- **Note economy.** At this level heavy notes can cost attention. Practise with notes limited to six words per talk: topic, purpose, two key terms, structure, conclusion.
- **No-replay discipline.** In practice, never replay audio before answering. The test plays each recording once.

## Writing: C1 versus C2

ETS describes **C1 writing** as using a broad range of complex structures flexibly, with consistent layout and punctuation, precisely qualifying opinions by degree of certainty, and expressing strong disagreement diplomatically. **C2 writing** uses comprehensive mastery to formulate thoughts precisely, give emphasis, differentiate and eliminate ambiguity, conveys finer shades of meaning with qualifying devices (adverbs of degree, clauses that limit a claim), and shows awareness of connotation.

On the task scoring guides, a 5 on the email or the discussion already requires precise, idiomatic word choice and almost no errors beyond typical timed slips. The band 6 writer gets that 5 **consistently**, on both tasks, every time, and adds near-perfect Build a Sentence accuracy.

## Writing: the precision toolkit

These are the tools the C2 descriptor points to. Practise them until they appear naturally:

- **Degree adverbs:** *largely, partly, marginally, considerably, scarcely* instead of *very* and *really*.
- **Limiting clauses:** *at least in the short term*, *provided that funding continues*, *except where students already work*.
- **Precise contrast:** *whereas* (two parallel facts), *although* (a concession), *yet* (an unexpected result), *by contrast* (two separate cases).
- **Emphasis by structure:** *What matters most is…*, *It is the cost, not the idea, that worries me.*
- **Connotation choices:** *frugal* versus *stingy*, *persistent* versus *stubborn*, *modest* versus *small*. Choose the word whose attitude you intend.

Each of these makes a sentence say exactly one thing. That is what "eliminate ambiguity" means in practice.

## Writing: a before-and-after rewrite

A discussion sentence at C1:

> Online classes are good for students because they are flexible, but they also have some problems with motivation.

The same idea at C2:

> Online classes suit students who already manage their time well; for those who rely on the structure of a timetable, the same flexibility can quietly erode motivation.

What changed:

- **"good for students"** became a claim limited to a specific group of students.
- **"some problems"** became a named mechanism: flexibility eroding motivation.
- The semicolon and **"the same flexibility"** link the two halves logically instead of with a vague *but*.
- **"quietly erode"** adds a precise shade of meaning (gradual, unnoticed) that *some problems* did not.

Run this rewrite on one sentence from every practice response for four weeks. Keep the before-and-after pairs in your log.

## Speaking: C1 versus C2

ETS describes **C1 speaking** as fluent and spontaneous, almost effortless, using complex structures and less common vocabulary appropriately, with the full range of phonological features controlled well enough for intelligibility. **C2 speaking** flows naturally and smoothly at length, pauses only to find precisely the right word or an example, formulates thoughts precisely without restricting what the speaker wants to say, and gives clear, elaborate and often memorable descriptions.

On the tasks:

- **Listen and Repeat:** the C2 descriptor in the blueprint describes repetition that is fluent and intelligible with clear segmentation of meaning. A score of 5 on each sentence requires an exact repetition, so at band 6 you need **all seven sentences exact**, including the longest ones.
- **Take an Interview:** a 5 is on topic, well elaborated, at a good conversational pace, easily intelligible, with rhythm and intonation that convey meaning and accurate, varied language. ETS's own 5-level samples contain natural fillers; band 6 is not about removing every *um*, it is about precise, organised content delivered with control.

## Speaking: advanced drills

- **Longest-sentence drill.** Collect long academic or campus sentences of 15–20 words with relative clauses and compound verbs. Hear once, repeat once, record, check word by word. Target: zero changes across ten sentences in a row.
- **Precision interview answers.** For each practice question, give a 45-second answer that includes one limiting clause and one precise example (a real place, time or number you know). The [Interview Answer Frameworks](/guide-interview-answer-frameworks) guide shows band 6 model answers with commentary.
- **Memorable detail.** End descriptive answers with one concrete image ("the bus that never arrives before 8:10"). The C2 descriptor mentions often memorable descriptions; one specific detail does more than three general adjectives.
- **Intonation for contrast.** Practise stressing the contrasting words in sentences like "It's not the *cost*, it's the *time*." Rhythm and intonation that carry meaning are explicitly in the scoring guide.

## Vocabulary depth for C2

At band 6, vocabulary is less about knowing more words and more about knowing each word **deeply**: its family, its partners, its register and its attitude. ETS's C2 descriptors mention a broad lexical repertoire including idiomatic expressions and awareness of connotation, and the C1 to C2 reading step is about subtle distinctions of style.

Build depth with four questions for every new academic word:

1. **Family:** what are its noun, verb, adjective and adverb forms? (*viable, viability*; *contend, contention, contentious*)
2. **Partners:** which words does it usually appear with? (*a contentious issue*, *raise a concern*, *draw a distinction*)
3. **Register:** is it formal, neutral or informal? (*commence* versus *start*; *a lot of* versus *a substantial number of*)
4. **Attitude:** does it carry a positive or negative shade? (*ambitious* versus *overambitious*; *assertive* versus *pushy*)

Keep a depth notebook with one line per word answering all four. Review it in Complete the Words drills (family), in your writing (partners and register) and in interview answers (attitude). Twenty deep entries a week do more at this level than a hundred translations.

## Listen and Repeat at full length

The final Listen and Repeat sentences are the longest and most complex: the blueprint describes progression toward compound verbs, dependent and relative clauses, and complex noun phrases. Band 5.5 speakers often repeat the first five sentences exactly and lose a function word or an ending in the last two.

- **Practise the end of the task, not the start.** Build a bank of sentences of 15–22 words and spend most of your repetition time there.
- **Hold the structure, not the words.** Before you speak, identify the main clause and the attached clause: *The form, which you can find at the front desk, / must be signed by your advisor.* Structure is easier to remember than a string of words.
- **Keep the endings.** Final -s, -ed and -ing disappear under pressure. Check them first when you compare your recording with the script.
- **Measure in sets of seven.** Score each set as the number of exact repetitions out of seven, and track the number weekly.

## Reducing variance

Many band 5.5 candidates already produce band 6 performances on some days. The goal is to produce them **on test day**. Variance comes from three sources:

- **Fatigue:** the test runs for roughly two hours with no scheduled breaks, and Speaking comes last. Practise full mocks at your test time of day.
- **Early errors:** in the adaptive sections, the first stage decides the difficulty of the second. Build a habit of extra care in the first items of Reading and Listening.
- **Unchecked writing:** most C1 writers can produce near-error-free text when they proofread. The ones who do not reach 6 skip the proofread when time is short. Make the final two minutes of each writing task non-negotiable.

Measure variance directly: keep the results of every full mock and every section mock in one table and look at the range, not just the best result.

| Week | Main work | Hours | Checkpoint |

## Advanced error-log fields

Add three fields to the standard log described in the [Band 4 to 5 Roadmap](/guide-band-4-to-5-roadmap):

- **Confidence (1–3):** how sure you were when you answered. A wrong answer at confidence 3 is a misconception; at confidence 1 it is a guess. They need different fixes.
- **Near-miss:** questions you answered correctly but only after changing your answer or spending too long. These are tomorrow's errors.
- **Level of the item:** easy, medium or hard as you judged it. At band 6, misses on easy items are the most expensive and the most fixable.

Review weekly: count confident-wrong answers separately. If they are more than a handful, you have specific misconceptions to correct, usually in vocabulary meaning or in how you interpret author stance.

## Decision rules

- **Book the test** when your last two full mocks both average 5.75 or above, and no section in either mock is below 5.5.
- **Keep preparing** if one mock reaches 6 and the other is 5.5: that is variance, not readiness. Spend two weeks on the source of the variance and test again.
- **Change approach** if a section has not moved across three weeks of targeted drills. At this level, that usually means the drill is too easy. Raise the difficulty of the material, not the number of hours.
- **Consider MyBest** if you already have an official result with strong sections. ETS's score report includes MyBest scores, the highest section scores from valid tests in the last two years. Whether an institution accepts them is the institution's decision, so check first; the [Retake and MyBest Strategy](/retake-and-mybest-strategy) module explains the trade-offs.
- **Respect the retake rule.** ETS allows unlimited attempts but not more than one test in any three-day period.

## Protecting the score on test day

At band 6, test day is mostly about not losing points you already own:

- Take the first items of Reading and Listening a little slower than feels necessary.
- Do not change a Reading answer unless you can name the exact words that prove the new one.
- In Writing, start the proofread with two minutes left, whatever happens.
- In Listen and Repeat, wait for the whole sentence before you start speaking, then speak it in meaning groups.
- If the score summary at the end offers you the choice to report or cancel, decide by your plan, not by your mood. ETS shows unofficial Reading and Listening scores at that point; Writing and Speaking are not shown yet.

## The wrong target {callout}

**Perfection is not the requirement**
ETS's own score-5 sample responses contain small errors and natural fillers. Band 6 rewards precise, controlled, well-organised language with errors only of the kind a capable writer or speaker makes under time pressure. Chasing zero imperfections makes you slower and more hesitant, which costs more than the errors you are trying to avoid.

## Frequently asked questions {faq}

### Is band 6 required by many universities?
ETS publishes no pass mark, and requirements vary by institution and programme. Many requirements sit below the top band. Check your exact programme's page and any section minimums before committing months to a 6.

### Can I get an overall 6 with Speaking at 5?
Yes, if the other three sections are all 6: the average is 5.75, which rounds to 6. With Speaking at 5 and any other section at 5.5, the average falls to 5.625 and the overall is 5.5.

### Do Reading and Listening at band 6 mean zero mistakes?
ETS does not publish raw-to-band tables for the 2026 sections, so nobody can tell you an exact number. The old-scale comparison (29–30 of 30 for Reading) suggests the margin is very small. Plan for near-perfect accuracy.

### Should I request a score review if I miss band 6 by half a band?
ETS allows a review of Speaking and/or Writing within 30 days of the test, for a fee, and only if you have not already sent scores to an institution. A review can raise or lower the score. Reading and Listening cannot be reviewed.

### How do I know if my mock result is reliable?
Treat any single mock as one data point. Two or more full mocks, taken under the same conditions, are much better evidence, and the spread between them tells you how secure the result is.
