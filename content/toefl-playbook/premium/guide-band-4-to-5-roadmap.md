## Who this roadmap is for

This roadmap is for learners whose recent TOEFL iBT result, or whose full mock in the [Schood TOEFL simulator](https://toefl.schood.online/full-exam), sits at **band 4 or 4.5 overall** and who need **band 5 or higher**. On the 2026 scale that is the step from CEFR **B2** to CEFR **C1**, which ETS maps to the same number in every section and in the overall score.

It assumes you have already read the public modules [TOEFL Essentials](/toefl-essentials) and [Score Scale 2026](/score-scale-2026). Those explain the format. This guide is about the harder question: **which specific weaknesses keep a B2 learner at 4 or 4.5, and what to do about each one, in what order, week by week.**

The method has four parts:

1. **Diagnose by section** with a full timed mock, not with a feeling.
2. **Compare your evidence to the ETS descriptors** for B2 and C1 so you know what "better" looks like.
3. **Run weekly targets** that put most of your hours on the section with the best return.
4. **Keep an error log** and use it every week to decide what to practise next.

A realistic expectation: the B2 to C1 step is a genuine change in language ability, not a trick. Many learners need several months of steady work for it. This guide cannot promise a band, and nothing here is a score prediction. What it can do is stop you spending hours on things that do not move the descriptors.

## What you will be able to do {checklist}

- Read your section bands and work out exactly how the overall band was rounded
- Name the descriptor difference between B2 and C1 in each of the four sections
- Choose one priority section and one maintenance section for the next four weeks
- Run a weekly cycle of drills, timed sets and review in the Schood simulator
- Keep a structured error log and turn it into next week's drills
- Use checkpoints and decision rules instead of guessing when a plan is not working

## Band 4 and band 5 on the 2026 scale

Since 21 January 2026, ETS reports each section and the overall score on a **1–6 scale in half-band steps**. The scale is aligned to the CEFR so that the same number means the same level everywhere: **4–4.5 is B2** and **5–5.5 is C1** in Reading, Listening, Writing, Speaking and overall.

During a two-year transition, the score report also shows a comparable 0–120 total. ETS's comparison table gives these minimums:

| Band | CEFR | Comparable 0–120 total |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | B2 | 72+ |
| 4.5 | B2 | 86+ |
| 5 | C1 | 95+ |
| 5.5 | C1 | 107+ |

Section by section, the same table puts band 5 at roughly **24–26 for Reading, 22–25 for Listening, 24–26 for Writing and 25–26 for Speaking** on the old 0–30 scale, and band 4 at **18–21, 17–19, 17–20 and 20–22**. If your target school still publishes an old-style requirement such as "95 total", this is why it lines up with band 5.

**Practical point:** ETS sets no pass mark. Each institution sets its own requirement and some also set section minimums. Before you plan, write your exact target down: overall band, any section minimums, and the deadline for your application.

## How the overall band is calculated

The overall band is the **average of the four section bands, rounded to the nearest half band**. ETS gives two worked examples on its own pages: an average of **5.125 is reported as 5**, and an average of **5.25 is reported as 5.5**. So a quarter-band average rounds up, and anything below it rounds down.

That arithmetic matters when you are near the line:

| Reading | Listening | Writing | Speaking | Average | Overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5.00 | 5 |
| 5.5 | 5 | 4.5 | 4.5 | 4.875 | 5 |
| 5 | 5 | 4.5 | 4.5 | 4.75 | 5 |
| 5 | 4.5 | 4.5 | 4.5 | 4.625 | 4.5 |
| 5.5 | 5.5 | 4 | 4 | 4.75 | 5 |

Three lessons follow from the table:

- **One section at 4.5 does not stop an overall 5**, as long as the others hold. Two sections at 4.5 are also survivable if the other two are at 5 or above.
- **A strong section can carry a weak one**, but only if the institution has no section minimums. Check this before you rely on it.
- **Every half band counts the same.** Moving Speaking from 4 to 4.5 changes the average exactly as much as moving Reading from 5 to 5.5. The cheapest half band is the one to chase first.

## Step 1: get a clean baseline

Take one **full timed mock in the [Schood simulator](https://toefl.schood.online/full-exam)** in the real order: Reading, Listening, Writing, Speaking, with no pauses between sections, because the real test has no scheduled breaks. Use the same desk, the same time of day and the same note-taking material you will use on test day.

Then record, in your error log (format later in this guide):

- the band for each section
- for Reading and Listening, which **task types** produced the misses
- for Writing, the task score pattern: Build a Sentence accuracy, email and discussion feedback
- for Speaking, Listen and Repeat accuracy and the interview feedback
- how you felt about **time** in each section: rushed, comfortable, or with time left over

**Do not average two old mocks with one new one.** Your baseline is the most recent full mock taken under real conditions. If you have not taken one in the last two weeks, take one now before you read further.

## Step 2: read your profile

Most band 4–4.5 learners fall into one of four profiles. Find yours, because each one has a different first move.

- **Profile A: receptive strong, productive weak.** Reading and Listening at 5 or above, Writing or Speaking at 4 or below. Your fastest gains are almost always in Writing and Speaking, where specific, learnable habits are scored.
- **Profile B: productive strong, receptive weak.** Writing and Speaking at 4.5 or above, Reading or Listening at 4. Your ceiling is vocabulary range and inference speed. Gains come slower but last.
- **Profile C: flat 4.** All four sections at 4 or 4.5. You need broad C1 input (more reading and listening at C1 difficulty) plus accuracy work in Writing. Choose one productive skill to push first.
- **Profile D: one collapse.** Three sections at 4.5 or 5, one at 3.5 or below. Fix the collapse first; the averaging table shows why a single low section is the most expensive problem you have.

Write your profile at the top of your error log. You will check it again at every checkpoint.

## Reading: what separates B2 from C1

ETS's performance descriptors describe a **B2 reader** as someone who uses several strategies, such as finding main points and using context clues, adjusts reading speed to the text, and can tell when a text gives facts and when it argues. A **C1 reader** handles long, complex texts and picks up finer points of detail, including attitudes and opinions that are implied rather than stated. C1 readers use contextual, grammatical and lexical cues to infer attitude and anticipate what comes next.

Translated into the three 2026 Reading tasks:

- **Complete the Words** rewards exactly those grammatical and lexical cues. At B2 you complete common words; at C1 you complete academic words whose form depends on grammar (*-tion* or *-tive*, *-ed* or *-ing*, singular or plural) without hesitating.
- **Read in Daily Life** rewards the purpose of a message, informal and telegraphic language, and scanning. The blueprint spans A1 to C1 here; for many B2 learners the remaining misses are about implied meaning ("what does the writer suggest…").
- **Read an Academic Passage** has questions on detail, vocabulary in context, inference, the relationship between ideas and the purpose of part of the text. The B2 ceiling is often the **purpose and relationship** questions, not detail.

Reading is two-stage adaptive: your performance in the first part decides the difficulty of the second part. ETS does not publish the routing rule, so treat the first stage as the part where careless errors cost the most.

## Reading: the moves that work at this level

- **Daily Complete the Words sets.** Two or three paragraphs a day, timed, then a full review of every miss by error type. The [Complete the Words Drills](/guide-complete-the-words-drills) guide has twelve graded paragraphs and the review method.
- **Purpose-and-relationship drill.** After every academic passage you practise, write one line per paragraph: "This paragraph exists to…". Then compare with the questions. C1 readers see structure; this drill trains it.
- **Implied-meaning drill.** For each Daily Life text, write what the writer wants the reader to do or believe, in your own words, before you look at the options.
- **Academic vocabulary in families.** Learn words with their family: *analyse, analysis, analytical, analytically*. Complete the Words punishes knowing only one form.
- **Do not read slower to be safe.** If your mock showed time left over in Reading, you are probably fine on speed. If you rushed, the fix is fewer re-reads, not faster eyes: answer, mark doubts, move on.

## Listening: what separates B2 from C1

ETS describes a **B2 listener** as able to follow extended speech and complex arguments when the topic is reasonably familiar and the argument is signposted, and to separate main themes from asides. A **C1 listener** uses contextual, grammatical and lexical cues to infer attitude, mood and intention, recognises a wide range of idioms, and follows most lectures and discussions with ease.

In the 2026 Listening section that difference shows up in four places:

- **Listen and Choose a Response:** the question or statement is heard, not shown. B2 listeners miss replies that depend on tone or implied meaning (a polite refusal, a surprised question).
- **Listen to a Conversation:** two questions per conversation, often about what a speaker implies or will do next.
- **Listen to an Announcement:** short campus messages about schedules, rules or events; misses are usually the detail that changed or the action the listener must take.
- **Listen to an Academic Talk:** about 100–250 words with four questions; C1 questions ask why the speaker mentions something and how the talk is organised.

Audio is played once, and voices include North American, British and Australian English.

## Listening: the moves that work at this level

- **Function-first listening.** For every talk, write two words while listening: the speaker's purpose ("compare", "warn", "define") and the structure ("cause → effect"). Questions about why something is mentioned become easier when you were tracking purpose all along.
- **Reply-type drill for Choose a Response.** After each item, label the correct reply: agreeing, declining, correcting, clarifying, reassuring. Patterns appear quickly, and patterns are what you need when the audio is gone.
- **Accent rotation.** Spend each listening session on one variety (North American, British or Australian) and rotate. B2 listeners often have one comfortable accent.
- **Post-listening transcript check.** When practice material has a transcript, read it only after answering, and underline the exact words that carried the answer. Misses usually cluster around signal phrases ("which is why", "that said", "not to mention").
- **Prediction habit.** Before each conversation's questions appear, say to yourself what the speakers will probably do next. ETS lists prediction as a tested skill.

## Writing: what separates 4 from 5

Writing has three tasks: **Build a Sentence** (10 items, 1 point each), **Write an Email** (7 minutes, scored 0–5) and **Write for an Academic Discussion** (10 minutes, scored 0–5). The blueprint gives Writing 20 raw points, so Build a Sentence carries half of them. ETS does not publish how raw points convert to bands, so treat every point as worth fighting for.

The ETS scoring guides draw a clear line between a **4** ("generally successful") and a **5** ("fully successful") on both written tasks:

- a 4 has adequate elaboration, some syntactic variety, appropriate word choice and few errors
- a 5 has elaboration that effectively supports the purpose, effective variety, precise and idiomatic word choice, and almost no errors beyond typical timed-writing slips

ETS's own email sample shows how thin that line is: a response that did everything asked was held at 4 mainly because of a consequential verb-form error. At this level, **accuracy and precision move scores more than length.**

## Writing: the moves that work at this level

- **Build a Sentence to 10 out of 10.** These are machine-scored and reward grammar you can drill: question word order, relative clauses, embedded questions, passive forms. Track every miss by structure in your log.
- **One precise verb per sentence.** Replace *get, make, do, have* with the verb that names the action: *obtain, arrange, submit, postpone*. This is the cheapest route to "precise, idiomatic word choice".
- **Social conventions in the email.** Score 5 emails handle requests, apologies and refusals with the right politeness for the reader. Practise the same request written to a professor, an office and a classmate.
- **Discussion: add, do not repeat.** Your post must contribute something new to what the two classmates said. A specific example from your experience or a consequence nobody mentioned is worth more than a restatement.
- **Two-minute proofread, every time.** Verb forms, articles, subject–verb agreement, plural -s. Practise it as a fixed final step in every timed response.

The [Email and Discussion Templates](/guide-email-and-discussion-templates) guide gives adaptable structures and annotated model answers.

## Speaking: what separates 4 from 5

Speaking has **Listen and Repeat** (7 sentences) and **Take an Interview** (4 questions, 45 seconds each). Every one of the 11 items is scored 0–5, so the section has 55 raw points and **Listen and Repeat carries 35 of them**. The descriptors:

- In Listen and Repeat, a 5 is an exact, fully intelligible repetition; a 4 keeps the meaning but changes or drops small words, tense or number markers.
- In the interview, a 5 is on topic and well elaborated, at a good conversational pace with natural pauses, easily intelligible, with rhythm and intonation that carry meaning. A 4 is elaborated but may lack connectors, has some pausing that affects flow, and grammar adequate for general meanings.

At section level, ETS describes B2 speakers as producing stretches of speech at an even tempo but hesitating while searching for patterns, and C1 speakers as fluent and spontaneous, almost effortless, with broader grammar and less common vocabulary used appropriately.

## Speaking: the moves that work at this level

- **Exactness drill for Listen and Repeat.** Repeat, record, then compare word by word with the script. Count function words you dropped (*the, a, of, to, have*) and endings you lost (*-ed, -s*). Those are the difference between a 4 and a 5 on each sentence.
- **Chunk the long sentences.** The sentences grow longer through the task. Hear them as two or three meaning groups, not as a string of words; the [Listen and Repeat](/speaking-listen-and-repeat) module explains thought groups.
- **45-second answer shape.** Direct answer first, then a reason, then a specific example, then a closing line. The [Interview Answer Frameworks](/guide-interview-answer-frameworks) guide gives frameworks for all four interview questions and model answers at three levels.
- **Connectors out loud.** The 4 descriptor mentions missing connectors. Rehearse a small set until they are automatic: *the main reason is*, *for example*, *on the other hand*, *which means that*.
- **Record and listen back daily.** Five minutes. You will hear the pauses and false starts that you cannot hear while speaking.

## Weekly targets for an eight-week push

This is a template. Shift hours toward your priority section, but keep every section in contact every week. Schood's own [Eight-Week Standard plan](/plans/eight-week-standard) turns it into daily tasks.

| Week | Main work | Hours | Checkpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Baseline mock, profile, error log set up, first drills | 8–10 | Log has 30+ entries |
| 2 | Priority section drills; daily Complete the Words | 8–10 | Priority drill accuracy measured |
| 3 | Priority section timed sets; productive-skill feedback | 9–11 | Two timed writing tasks reviewed |
| 4 | Section mock of priority section; review | 9–11 | Section band vs baseline |
| 5 | Second priority; maintain the first | 9–11 | Error categories shrinking |
| 6 | Full mock; re-profile | 10–12 | Full mock bands recorded |
| 7 | Target the two weakest error categories | 9–11 | Repeat-error rate down |
| 8 | Final full mock and test-day rehearsal | 8–10 | Decision: test, or extend |

Hours are guidance for a learner studying alongside school or work. They are not a promise of results; the right number is the one you can sustain every week.

## The error log

The error log is the most useful tool in this guide. Keep it as a spreadsheet or a notebook with these columns:

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| Date and source | "Mock 2, Reading" or "Drill, Email 4" |
| Task type | Complete the Words, Daily Life, Academic Passage, Choose a Response, and so on |
| What happened | The question, your answer, the correct answer, or the sentence you said |
| Category | One label from the category list below |
| Why | One sentence: what you thought, and what you should have noticed |
| Fix | The drill that targets it, with a date to repeat it |

Rules that make the log work:

- **One miss, one line.** Do not summarise five misses as "vocabulary problems".
- **Write the why in your own words.** "Did not see *however* changed the direction" is useful; "careless" is not.
- **Every entry gets a fix with a date.** An entry without a fix is a diary, not a log.

## Error categories that matter at this level

Use a small fixed set of labels so patterns show up when you count them:

- **R-form:** Complete the Words word form (suffix, ending, part of speech)
- **R-vocab:** did not know the word or its meaning in context
- **R-purpose:** missed why a paragraph or sentence exists
- **R-infer:** chose a stated detail when the question asked for implication
- **L-reply:** wrong reply type in Choose a Response
- **L-detail:** missed a changed detail, time or action
- **L-structure:** lost the organisation of a talk
- **W-grammar:** verb form, agreement, article, plural
- **W-word:** vague or wrong word choice, wrong collocation
- **W-task:** missed a bullet in the email or did not add to the discussion
- **S-exact:** dropped or changed words in Listen and Repeat
- **S-flow:** long pauses, restarts, missing connectors
- **S-develop:** answer too short or too general
- **T-time:** ran out of time or rushed the end

At the end of each week, count entries per label. **Your two biggest labels are next week's drill focus.**

## The weekly review session

Block one 60-minute session at the end of every week. Do it in this order:

1. **Count the log.** Totals per category this week and last week.
2. **Pick two categories.** The two largest, unless one is already shrinking fast.
3. **Re-do five old misses** from those categories without looking at the answers.
4. **Plan next week:** which drills, which days, which timed sets.
5. **Write one sentence of evidence** about progress, using numbers you actually measured (for example "Build a Sentence: 7/10 → 9/10 across two sets").

Never write a progress note you cannot back with a measured number. If you did not measure, write "not measured yet" and fix that next week.

## Checkpoints and decision rules

Plans fail quietly. These rules make you notice:

- **After two weeks on a priority section with no change in drill accuracy:** change the drill, not the hours. The drill is not targeting the real error.
- **If a section band does not move across two full mocks four weeks apart:** go back to the error log and re-label the last 30 entries. Mislabelled errors are the usual cause.
- **If the overall average is within a quarter band of 5:** spend the next fortnight on the section with the cheapest next half band, which is usually Writing or Speaking for Profile A and C learners.
- **If one section drops while you push another:** add two maintenance sessions a week for the dropping section before continuing.
- **If a test date is fixed and your last full mock is below 4.5 overall two weeks before it:** decide calmly whether to keep the date or reschedule. ETS's bulletin requires reschedules at least four full days before the test.

## Common plateaus at 4.5

- **The "almost" Writing plateau.** Emails and posts are on task but full of small errors. Fix: the two-minute proofread and a personal list of your five most frequent grammar errors, checked every time.
- **The fast-but-vague Speaking plateau.** Answers are fluent but general ("it is very important for many people"). Fix: one specific example per answer: a name, a place, a time, a number you actually know.
- **The safe-vocabulary plateau.** Writing uses only words you are sure of, so it never shows precise word choice. Fix: one upgrade per paragraph, chosen during the proofread.
- **The router-error plateau.** Reading and Listening misses cluster early in the section. Fix: slow down very slightly at the start; accuracy in the first stage matters more than speed.
- **The comfort-accent plateau.** Listening misses cluster on one accent. Fix: accent rotation for three weeks.

## Mistakes that waste weeks {callout}

**Practising what you are already good at**
The most common reason for a stuck band is that practice time goes to the comfortable section. If your error log says Speaking, but your week was mostly reading passages, you will feel busy and stay at 4.5. Let the counts in the log, not your mood, decide the week.

## Frequently asked questions {faq}

### Can I reach band 5 overall with one section at 4?
Yes, arithmetically. For example 5.5, 5.5, 4 and 4 average 4.75, which rounds to 5. But check whether your institution sets section minimums; many do, and a section at 4 may fail them even when the overall passes.

### Is band 5 the same as the old 95?
ETS's comparison table lists 95+ as the 0–120 total comparable to an overall band 5. During the two-year transition your score report shows both, so you can check against either requirement without converting anything.

### Should I work on all four sections every week?
Yes, but not equally. Put most hours on the priority section and keep the others in short maintenance sessions so they do not slip.

### How many full mocks should I take in eight weeks?
Three is a good number: a baseline, one in the middle, and one near the end. More mocks without review do not help; the review is where the learning happens.

### My Speaking band is the lowest of my four. Is that unusual?
No. In ETS's figures for 2024 test takers converted to the new scale, the average Speaking band (3.96) was lower than the averages for Reading (4.59), Listening (4.77) and Writing (4.37). It is a common profile, and Speaking is a section where specific, practisable habits (exact repetition, answer structure, connectors) are what the scoring guide describes.
