## Why structures, not scripts

The public modules [Write an Email](/writing-email) and [Write for an Academic Discussion](/writing-academic-discussion) explain what each task asks. This guide gives you **structures you can adapt in seconds** and the language that moves a response from a 4 to a 5 on ETS's 0–5 scoring guides.

A structure is not a memorised script. ETS's scoring guides reward elaboration that supports the communicative purpose, appropriate social conventions, syntactic variety and precise word choice. A pasted script does the opposite: it ignores the reader, repeats the same sentences as thousands of other candidates and usually misses one of the required points. The lowest score descriptions even mention responses that mostly borrow language from the prompt.

What you will learn instead is a **flexible frame**: a fixed order of moves, and a bank of ways to perform each move, so that every response is new but you never have to plan from nothing.

## What you will be able to do {checklist}

- Plan and write a complete email in 7 minutes, covering every bullet in the prompt
- Choose the right register for a professor, an office, a manager or a classmate
- Write a discussion post that takes a clear position and adds something new
- Use hedging, qualifying and concession language naturally
- Follow a timing plan that leaves two minutes to proofread
- Recognise the mistakes that hold a response at 3 or 4

## The two tasks in numbers

From **ETS's overview and blueprint**:

| | Write an Email | Write for an Academic Discussion |
|---|---|---|
| Time | 7 minutes | 10 minutes |
| Prompt | A scenario plus usually three things to do | A professor's question plus two student posts |
| Purpose | A specific communicative goal: request, recommend, invite, propose a solution | State and support an opinion, contribute to the discussion |
| Score | 0–5 | 0–5 |
| Length guidance | "Write as much as you can and in complete sentences" | "An effective response will contain at least 100 words" |

Both tasks are AI scored and sit in the Writing section with ten Build a Sentence items. The blueprint gives the section 20 raw points: 10 from Build a Sentence and 5 from each of these two tasks.

## What a 5 looks like on the scoring guides

ETS's guides describe a **5 email** as effective and clearly expressed, with elaboration that effectively supports the purpose, effective syntactic variety, precise and idiomatic word choice, consistent appropriate social conventions (politeness, register, organisation, how requests or refusals are phrased) and almost no errors beyond typical timed slips.

A **5 discussion post** is a relevant and very clearly expressed contribution, with relevant, well-elaborated explanations, examples or details, a variety of structures, precise and idiomatic word choice and almost no errors.

The **4** in both guides is "generally successful": adequate elaboration, some variety, appropriate (not precise) word choice, few errors. ETS's own sample email was held at 4 by a single kind of consequential error in verb form.

So the move from 4 to 5 has three parts: **elaborate with purpose, choose precise words, and remove errors.** Everything in this guide serves one of those three.

## The email frame

Use these five moves, in this order:

1. **Greeting and context (1 sentence).** Who you are, or why you are writing, in relation to the reader.
2. **Bullet one, developed (2–3 sentences).** State it, then add one detail or reason.
3. **Bullet two, developed (2–3 sentences).** The same pattern.
4. **Bullet three, developed (2–3 sentences).** Often the request or the proposal; make the action clear.
5. **Close (1–2 sentences).** A polite next step and a sign-off appropriate to the reader.

Two rules matter more than anything else:

- **Every bullet gets its own developed paragraph or sentence group.** Missing or thinly treating a bullet is the fastest way to lose the "achieves the communicative goal" part of the score.
- **The reader decides the register.** The frame stays the same; the language changes with who will read it.

## Register: one request, three readers

The same request, adjusted for three readers. Notice how the moves stay, while the tone and the phrasing change.

**To a professor (formal, respectful):**
> Dear Professor Okafor, I am writing to ask whether it would be possible to submit my lab report two days after the deadline. I would be grateful for any flexibility you can offer.

**To a campus office (polite, efficient):**
> Dear Housing Office team, I would like to request a later move-in date for my room. Could you let me know whether this is possible and what I need to do?

**To a classmate (friendly, direct):**
> Hi Sam, could you send me your notes from Tuesday's lecture? I had to leave early and I missed the last part.

Register checklist:

- **Formal:** full forms (*I would*), modal requests (*Would it be possible…*), titles (*Professor*, *Dr.*), thanks without exaggeration.
- **Polite and efficient:** clear requests (*Could you let me know…*), no slang, short paragraphs.
- **Friendly:** contractions, direct questions, a warm close. Still complete sentences and correct grammar.

## The functions bank

Most email bullets ask you to perform one of a small set of functions. Have **two ways** to do each:

| Function | Neutral to formal | Friendly |
|---|---|---|
| Request | Would it be possible to…? / I would be grateful if you could… | Could you…? / Any chance you could…? |
| Apologise | I apologise for… and I will make sure that… | Sorry about… I'll… |
| Explain a problem | Unfortunately, when I tried to…, the system… | The thing is, when I tried to… |
| Recommend | I would recommend… because… | You should definitely… it's… |
| Invite | I would like to invite you to… which will take place on… | Do you want to come to…? |
| Propose a solution | One option might be to…, which would… | What if we…? That way… |
| Decline politely | I am afraid I will not be able to…; however, I could… | I can't make it, but how about…? |
| Thank | Thank you for taking the time to… | Thanks so much for… |

Use the frame to place each function, then develop it with one concrete detail: a date, a reason, a consequence.

## Model email 1: missed registration (formal)

**Prompt (original, for practice):** Your environmental science course is organising a visit to a water treatment plant. Registration closed while you were away for a family event, and you did not see the reminder. Write to your professor, Dr. Lindqvist. In your email: explain why you did not register; ask whether a place is still available; suggest what you could do if the visit is full.

> **Subject:** Water treatment plant visit
>
> Dear Dr. Lindqvist,
>
> I am a student in your Tuesday environmental science section, and I am writing about the visit to the water treatment plant. I was away last week for my sister's wedding, and I only saw the registration reminder after the form had closed. I should have checked the course page before I left, and I am sorry for the inconvenience.
>
> I would be grateful if you could tell me whether a place is still available. The visit is directly related to the filtration unit we are studying, so I would very much like to see the process in person.
>
> If the group is already full, I would be happy to join a waiting list in case someone cancels. Alternatively, I could complete an extra reading assignment on the plant's treatment stages so that I do not miss the content.
>
> Thank you for considering my request.
>
> Kind regards,
> Aziz Karimov

**Annotation:**

- **All three bullets are covered**, each in its own paragraph: why (paragraph 1), availability (paragraph 2), alternatives (paragraph 3).
- **Elaboration supports the purpose:** the reason for wanting a place ("directly related to the filtration unit") makes the request stronger, not just longer.
- **Social conventions:** a brief, sincere apology that accepts responsibility; a modal request (*I would be grateful if…*); two concrete alternatives instead of pressure.
- **Precise words:** *filtration unit*, *treatment stages*, *in case someone cancels*.
- **Variety:** a relative clause, a conditional (*If the group is already full…*), a purpose clause (*so that I do not miss…*).

## Model email 2: recommending a course (friendly)

**Prompt (original):** Your friend Leila is choosing between two summer courses you have both heard about: Public Speaking and Introduction to Data Analysis. You took one of them last year. Write to Leila. In your email: say which course you recommend; explain why; give her one piece of advice for doing well.

> **Subject:** Summer course advice
>
> Hi Leila,
>
> Great to hear you're planning a summer course! If I were you, I'd go for Introduction to Data Analysis, the one I took last year.
>
> The main reason is that it's surprisingly practical. Instead of just learning formulas, we spent most classes working with real data sets, like bus timetables and weather records, and by the end I could build simple charts that I've since used in two other courses. Public Speaking is useful too, but you already present well, so I think the data course would teach you more.
>
> My one piece of advice: don't skip the first two weeks of spreadsheet exercises, even if they seem easy. Everything later builds on them, and the people who fell behind early found it hard to catch up.
>
> Let me know what you decide, and good luck!
>
> Best,
> Dilnoza

**Annotation:**

- **Register fits a friend:** contractions, an exclamation, *If I were you, I'd…*, a warm close. Still fully grammatical.
- **The recommendation is justified by a specific, personal detail** (bus timetables, charts used in two other courses), which is what "elaboration that effectively supports the communicative purpose" looks like.
- **A comparison with the other option** shows the recommendation is reasoned, not random.
- **The advice is concrete and explained** (why the first two weeks matter).

## Model email 3: proposing a solution (polite, efficient)

**Prompt (original):** The library's new online system for booking group study rooms has been double-booking rooms. Last week your group arrived to find another group in the room you had reserved. Write to the library services manager. In your email: describe the problem; explain how it affected your group; propose a solution.

> **Subject:** Double bookings in the study room system
>
> Dear Library Services Manager,
>
> I am writing to report a problem with the new group study room booking system. Last Thursday my group reserved Room 4 from 3 to 5 p.m. and received a confirmation email, but when we arrived another group was already there with a confirmation for the same time.
>
> Because every other room was occupied, we had to hold our project meeting in the café, where it was too noisy to rehearse our presentation. It was the last meeting we had planned before the deadline, so the timing was particularly unfortunate.
>
> One possible solution would be to show each room's bookings on a screen at the door, so that groups could spot a conflict before a session starts. In the meantime, it might help if staff at the front desk could check a printed list when two groups claim the same room.
>
> Thank you for looking into this. I would be happy to share my confirmation email if it would help.
>
> Yours sincerely,
> Javohir Tursunov

**Annotation:**

- **Facts first, then impact, then solution**, matching the bullets in order.
- **Specific details** (Room 4, 3 to 5 p.m., the confirmation email) make the problem credible and clear.
- **The solution is constructive and hedged** (*One possible solution would be…*, *it might help if…*): firm content, polite form.
- **An offer of help at the end** is a small social convention that strong emails often include.

## The discussion frame

Your post joins a conversation, so it must do two things: **take a position** and **add something the other posts did not say.** Use these moves:

1. **Position (1–2 sentences).** Answer the professor's question directly. You may respond to one classmate by name.
2. **Main reason (2–3 sentences).** One reason, explained, not three reasons listed.
3. **Specific support (2–3 sentences).** An example, a consequence or a brief scenario: something concrete.
4. **Engage the other side (1–2 sentences).** Acknowledge the strongest opposing point and answer it, or refine your position.
5. **Close (1 sentence).** A sentence that states the takeaway, not a summary of everything.

**What counts as "new":** a different reason, a specific example, a practical consequence, a condition under which your view changes, or a reply to a classmate's point. Restating what Priya or Tomás said in different words is not a contribution.

## Model discussion 1: cars in historic centres

**Prompt (original):** *Professor Ramos:* Many cities with historic centres are debating whether to ban private cars from those areas. Supporters say it protects old buildings and makes streets safer; critics say it hurts local businesses and makes access harder. Should cities ban private cars from their historic centres? Why or why not?

*Priya:* Yes. Car pollution damages old stone buildings, and narrow streets were never designed for traffic. A ban protects the heritage that attracts visitors in the first place.

*Tomás:* I disagree. Shops in these areas depend on customers who drive in, and older residents may struggle without a car. A ban could empty the centre instead of saving it.

> I agree with Priya that a ban makes sense, but Tomás's concern about access is the real test of whether it works. In my view, the policy succeeds only when the space taken from cars is given to something better than cars. When my city closed its old market street to traffic, the first year was difficult, exactly as Tomás predicts: two shops near the entrance closed. However, the council then added a frequent electric shuttle from the car parks and allowed delivery vans in the early morning. Within a couple of years, the street had more people on it than before, because walking there became pleasant rather than merely possible. So I would support a ban, but only as part of a package that includes reliable public transport and exemptions for residents with limited mobility. Banning cars without replacing what they provided would simply move the problem somewhere else.

**Annotation:**

- **Position plus engagement in the first sentence:** it names both classmates and sets up a refined view.
- **The contribution is new:** neither classmate mentioned the conditions that make a ban work (shuttle, deliveries, exemptions).
- **The example is specific and personal**, and it concedes the opposing point honestly (shops closed) before answering it.
- **Precise language:** *pleasant rather than merely possible*, *as part of a package*, *limited mobility*.
- **About 150 words**, comfortably above ETS's guidance of at least 100 words, written in 10 minutes with time to check.

## Model discussion 2: recorded lectures

**Prompt (original):** *Dr. Haddad:* Some universities now record every lecture and post the videos online for students. Should universities record all lectures? Why or why not?

*Mei:* Yes. Recordings let students review difficult material and help anyone who is ill or has to work.

*Daniel:* No. If every lecture is online, fewer students will attend, and the live discussion that makes lectures valuable will disappear.

> Daniel is right that attendance might fall, but I think that tells us something about lectures rather than about recordings. If a session is nothing more than a speaker reading slides, students lose very little by watching it later at double speed. Recording would therefore push lecturers to make the live session worth attending, for example by using part of the time for questions, short problems or debates that a video cannot reproduce. Mei's point about reviewing material is also stronger than it first appears: in my statistics course, I rewatched one explanation of sampling error three times before it finally made sense, which would have been impossible from my notes alone. For these reasons, I believe universities should record lectures, as long as they also redesign the in-person part of the course so that attending still offers something extra.

**Annotation:**

- **Reframes the debate** (attendance falling as a signal about lecture quality), which is a genuine contribution.
- **Uses one classmate's point to strengthen the position** and supports it with a precise personal example.
- **Logical connectors** (*therefore*, *for example*, *for these reasons*, *as long as*) make the argument easy to follow without sounding mechanical.

## Band 5–6 language that sounds natural

Use these sparingly: one or two per paragraph, where they make the meaning more exact.

- **Hedging certainty:** *is likely to*, *tends to*, *in most cases*, *it seems reasonable to assume that*.
- **Limiting a claim:** *at least in the short term*, *as long as*, *provided that*, *except where*.
- **Conceding then answering:** *Admittedly, … ; even so, …* / *While it is true that …, this overlooks …*
- **Precise contrast:** *whereas*, *by contrast*, *rather than*, *not so much X as Y*.
- **Emphasis by structure:** *What concerns me most is…*, *It is not the cost but the time that…*
- **Consequences:** *which means that*, *as a result*, *this would in turn*.

ETS's C1 descriptor for writing mentions precisely qualifying opinions by degree of certainty and expressing disagreement diplomatically. These phrases are how you do both in a short response.

## Timing plans

**Write an Email (7 minutes):**

| Minute | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–1 | Read the scenario and bullets; note the reader and one detail for each bullet |
| 1–5:30 | Write greeting, three developed bullets and close |
| 5:30–7 | Proofread: verbs, articles, agreement, spelling; check every bullet is covered |

**Write for an Academic Discussion (10 minutes):**

| Minute | Action |
|---|---|
| 0–1.5 | Read the professor and both students; choose a position and your new point |
| 1.5–8 | Write position, reason, specific support, engagement and close |
| 8–10 | Proofread and sharpen one vague word per paragraph |

Practise both timings in the [simulator](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) until the proofread happens automatically.

## The two-minute proofread

In this order, because this order catches the errors that cost the most:

1. **Verb forms and tense:** *can be sent*, not *can sent*; *I have taken*, not *I have took*.
2. **Subject–verb agreement:** *the system doesn't*, *students who live*.
3. **Articles:** *a deadline*, *the course page*, no article with general plurals.
4. **Plural -s and word forms:** *two poems*, *successfully*, *responsible*.
5. **Spelling of key words:** especially the words copied from the prompt.
6. **Bullet check (email) or contribution check (discussion):** is every required point there?

Write your own top five recurring errors on a card and check those first. Your personal list beats any general list.

## Mistakes that hold scores at 3 or 4

- **Missing a bullet**, or mentioning it in a few words at the end.
- **The wrong register:** *Hey prof* to a professor, or stiff formality to a friend.
- **Generic elaboration:** "This is very important for everyone" instead of a concrete detail.
- **Repeating a classmate** in the discussion instead of adding to the conversation.
- **Listing three reasons** with one sentence each, instead of developing one reason well.
- **Memorised openings** that do not fit the prompt ("In today's modern world…").
- **Skipping the proofread** because the response "feels finished".
- **Copying long phrases from the prompt**: ETS's low-score descriptors mention language mostly borrowed from the stimulus.

## Practice prompts

Write each one under time, then compare with the frame and the annotations above. All prompts are original.

**Emails (7 minutes):**

1. Your student club wants to invite a local engineer to speak. Write to her: introduce the club, explain why you chose her, and suggest two possible dates.
2. You bought a used textbook from the campus shop but it is the wrong edition. Write to the shop: describe the problem, explain why the edition matters, and ask what they can do.
3. A classmate missed the group meeting where tasks were divided. Write to him: summarise the decisions, tell him his task, and ask him to confirm.

**Discussions (10 minutes):**

1. Should first-year university students be required to live on campus?
2. Should museums be free for everyone, even if this means less funding for exhibitions?
3. Is it better for students to specialise early or to study a broad range of subjects first?

## Feedback loop

For each practice response, record in your error log:

- **Task score** if you used the simulator's feedback, or your own score against the ETS descriptors.
- **Bullets covered** (email) or **new contribution made** (discussion): yes or no.
- **Errors found in proofread** and **errors found later** (the second number is what the proofread missed).
- **One upgraded word or sentence** you would change now.

Review the log weekly. If "errors found later" stays high, your proofread is too fast or in the wrong order. The [Band 4 to 5 Roadmap](/guide-band-4-to-5-roadmap) shows how to fold this into a full weekly plan.

## Templates are a starting point {callout}

**Make the frame invisible**
The best responses use a clear structure that the reader never notices. If a sentence could be pasted into any response on any topic, replace it with one that only makes sense for this prompt. Specific beats impressive.

## Frequently asked questions {faq}

### How long should my email be?
ETS asks you to write as much as you can in complete sentences, and the scoring guide rewards elaboration that supports the purpose. As a starting point, aim for about 100–150 words that you can finish and proofread in seven minutes, then adjust after timing yourself. The model emails in this guide run to about 140–170 words; treat them as something to work toward, not a minimum.

### Is 100 words enough for the discussion?
ETS's prompt states that an effective response will contain at least 100 words. Treat 100 as a floor. A developed post with one reason, one specific example and one engagement point usually runs longer.

### Can I invent an example from "my experience"?
The task asks you to support your opinion with your own reasoning, experiences or knowledge. A plausible, specific scenario is a normal way to support a point; what matters for the score is relevance, clarity and language quality.

### Should I name the classmates in my post?
It is a natural way to engage with the discussion and makes your contribution clearly connected. It is not required; what is required is that you add something of your own.

### British or American spelling?
Either is correct English. Be consistent within a response. ETS's own materials use American spelling.
