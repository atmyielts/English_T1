<!-- rich_text: Why this task matters -->
Write for an Academic Discussion is the last task in the Writing section of the TOEFL iBT as updated on 21 January 2026. You join an online class discussion: a professor posts a question, two students give different answers, and you write your own post that states and supports your opinion. You have **10 minutes**.

The task is worth up to **5 raw points** of the section's 20, the same as the email. It is also the one Writing task that did not change in the 2026 update: ETS's score-scale report notes that the discussion task remained as it was, so it remains well researched and predictable.

What makes the task interesting is that it is not an essay. Nobody wants an introduction about how important the topic is in modern society. The professor wants a clear position, real reasons, and a contribution that moves the conversation forward. If you can write one focused, well-supported paragraph of around 120 to 160 words that does this, you are writing what the top of the scoring guide describes.

<!-- checklist: What you will be able to do -->
- Describe the format, timing and AI scoring of the discussion task accurately.
- Read the professor's question and both student posts in about 90 seconds.
- Choose a position quickly and defend it with your own reasons.
- Build a post with five moves: position, link, reason, example, wrap.
- Refer to classmates' ideas without copying their words.
- Recognise what separates 3-, 4- and 5-level posts.
- Manage 10 minutes so that you finish with time to proofread.

| Feature | Academic Discussion |
|---|---|
| Position | Task 3 of 3 in Writing |
| Time | 10 minutes |
| Input | Professor post + 2 student posts |
| Your job | State and support an opinion |
| Score | 0 to 5, AI scored |
| Length guidance | At least 100 words |

The on-screen directions in ETS's sample ask you to express and support your opinion and to make a contribution in your own words, and they say that an effective response will contain at least 100 words. That sentence is the only length guidance ETS gives; it is a floor for an effective response, not a target to stop at.

The professor's post frames a topic from a field such as social studies, business, education, environmental science or psychology, and ends with a direct question. The two students usually take different positions, each with a short reason.

<!-- rich_text: What ETS says the task measures -->
The 2026 blueprint places the discussion post in the Writing claim about effective responses to common situations in academic contexts, with the subskill of writing academic paragraph text that presents a clear, well-supported argument using varied grammar and vocabulary. It is set in an **academic** language-use context, at CEFR B1 to C2.

ETS's overview says the post should, in our words:

1. **Argue for a position with clear elaboration**, which may involve responding to the other posts or using information from them.
2. **Be adequately supported, clear and cohesive.**
3. **Use a range of grammar and vocabulary accurately and appropriately.**
4. **Follow the mechanics** of English: spelling, punctuation and capitalisation.

In other words: an opinion, reasons that actually support it, connected sentences, and controlled language. Nothing in the description rewards long introductions, memorised phrases or rare vocabulary for its own sake.

<!-- rich_text: How the post is scored -->
The blueprint lists the discussion post as **AI scored** (natural language processing and machine learning evaluating features such as fluency, coherence and grammar), with a maximum of **5 points**. ETS publishes a six-level scoring guide. Summarised in our own words:

- **5, fully successful.** A relevant, very clearly expressed contribution. Explanations, examples or details are relevant and well developed. Sentence structures vary effectively, word choice is precise and idiomatic, and errors are almost absent apart from typical fast-typing slips.
- **4, generally successful.** A relevant contribution that is easy to understand. Elaboration is adequate, there is variety in structures, word choice is appropriate, and there are few errors.
- **3, partially successful.** Mostly relevant and mostly understandable. Part of an explanation or example is missing, unclear or off the point, and there are noticeable errors in structure, word forms or idiom.
- **2, mostly unsuccessful.** An attempt to contribute, but ideas are poorly developed or only partly relevant, with limited range and many errors.
- **1, unsuccessful.** Words and phrases that show an attempt but few coherent ideas, or language mostly borrowed from the prompt.
- **0.** Blank, rejects the topic, not in English, entirely copied or unrelated, or random keystrokes.

ETS's 5-level sample post contains a missing article, and the commentary treats it as the kind of slip a capable writer makes in a first draft. The guide rewards clarity and development first; a tiny error does not sink a strong post.

ETS converts the 0 to 20 raw Writing total into the 1 to 6 Writing band, but it does not publish a conversion from one discussion score to a band. Treat the levels as performance targets.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: pick a side in the first sentence -->
State your position in sentence one
The scoring guide rewards a clear contribution. Open with your answer to the professor's question, not with background. If you genuinely see both sides, choose the side you can support best and acknowledge the other in one clause.

<!-- rich_text: Anatomy of the prompt -->
Every prompt has the same three parts.

**The professor's post.** Two to five sentences introducing a topic, sometimes defining a term, then a question such as *Should...? Why or why not?* or *Which is more important, X or Y?* Read the final question twice. That question, not the general topic, is what you must answer.

**Student post 1 and student post 2.** Each student, identified by name, gives a position and one or two reasons. They usually disagree, but sometimes both agree with a different reason each, or one proposes a middle position.

**Your task.** Add your own post. You may agree with one student, disagree with both, or take a third position, but your reasons must be your own.

A useful habit: after reading, summarise each student in five words or fewer. *Priya: cars out, cleaner and safer. Tomas: shops lose driving customers.* These summaries are your raw material for referring to them later without copying.

<!-- rich_text: Reading the posts strategically -->
You have limited time, so read with a purpose.

1. **Read the professor's question first, then the whole professor post.** The question tells you what to look for in the rest.
2. **Read each student post looking for the reason, not the opinion.** The opinion is usually obvious; the reason is what you will either build on or answer.
3. **Look for what is missing.** Neither student mentions cost? Older people? Long-term effects? Fairness? A missing angle is the easiest source of an original contribution.
4. **Decide in under 30 seconds.** Spending three minutes choosing a side is a common way to run out of time. Your position does not need to be your deepest personal belief; it needs to be the one you can explain best in English in eight minutes.

<!-- rich_text: A five-move structure -->
This structure produces a focused paragraph that matches what the scoring guide rewards. It works for almost every prompt.

1. **Position (1 sentence).** Answer the question directly. *I believe city centres should be closed to private cars.*
2. **Link to the discussion (1 sentence).** Connect to a classmate briefly, in your words. *Tomas is right that shop owners worry about losing drivers, but...*
3. **Reason (2 to 3 sentences).** Your main new reason, explained: why it is true and why it matters.
4. **Example (2 to 3 sentences).** A specific case, personal experience, or a realistic scenario that shows the reason in action.
5. **Wrap (1 sentence).** A conclusion that adds a condition, a solution or a consequence, rather than repeating sentence one.

That is roughly 7 to 10 sentences and 120 to 170 words. If you have time for more, add a second short reason, not more introduction.

<!-- rich_text: Generating ideas fast: six lenses -->
When your mind goes blank, run the question through these six lenses and stop at the first one that gives you a clear reason and an example.

| Lens | Ask yourself |
|---|---|
| People | Who is helped or harmed? Students, families, workers, older people? |
| Money | Who pays? Is it efficient? |
| Time | What changes in the short term versus the long term? |
| Health and safety | Is anyone safer, healthier, more stressed? |
| Fairness | Is it fair to everyone, including people without resources? |
| Practicality | Can it actually be done? What would make it work? |

The *practicality* lens is especially powerful because it creates a contribution neither student made: *the idea only works if...* Conditions and compromises show nuanced thinking and naturally demand more complex grammar (conditionals, concessions), which the scoring guide also rewards.

<!-- rich_text: Engaging classmates without copying them -->
The directions ask for a contribution *in your own words*, and the scoring guide places language borrowed from the stimulus at the bottom levels. Referring to classmates is good; reusing their sentences is not. Three safe techniques:

- **Name plus short paraphrase.** *Amara's point about accountability is important, but...* Five words that show you read her post, then your own idea.
- **Concede and turn.** *While Kenji is right that recordings help sick students, I still think...* This shows balance and gives you a natural complex sentence.
- **Extend.** *I agree with Priya, and I would add a reason she didn't mention:...* Agreement is fine as long as your reason is new.

Avoid restating a student's reason as your main support. If Priya says cars cause pollution, and your post's main reason is that cars cause pollution, you have contributed nothing new, even if your sentences are correct.

<!-- rich_text: How long should the post be? -->
ETS's directions say that an effective response will contain **at least 100 words**. Practically:

- **Under 100 words**, it is hard to include a position, a developed reason and an example. Short posts tend to lose points for elaboration, not for word count itself.
- **About 120 to 170 words** is the range most strong writers reach in 10 minutes while leaving time to check. ETS's 5-level sample post is in this range.
- **Over 200 words** is fine if it stays focused, but longer posts give more room for errors and often contain filler.

Quality of development matters more than length. A 140-word post with one clear reason and one vivid example will usually beat a 220-word post that lists four reasons without explaining any of them.

<!-- rich_text: Useful language for the discussion -->
**Giving your position**
- *In my view,...* / *I'm convinced that...* / *I would argue that...*
- *I lean towards Amara's side, mainly because...*

**Referring to classmates**
- *As Tomas points out,...* / *Priya raises a fair point about...*
- *I see why Kenji believes..., but...*

**Adding and explaining**
- *What matters most here is...* / *The main reason is that...*
- *This is important because...* / *As a result,...*

**Giving examples**
- *For instance,...* / *A good example is...* / *In my own experience,...*
- *Imagine a student who...*

**Conceding and contrasting**
- *Admittedly,...* / *Even so,...* / *That said,...* / *On the other hand,...*

**Wrapping up**
- *For these reasons,...* / *Ultimately,...* / *This would only work if...*

Use a handful, not all. A post stuffed with linking phrases reads as mechanical; the scoring guide values precise, idiomatic word choice, which usually means fewer and better connectors.

<!-- rich_text: Prompt 1: car-free city centres -->
This is an original Schood prompt in the style of the task.

> **Dr. Haddad (Urban Planning):** Several cities have closed their central streets to private cars and turned them into pedestrian zones. Supporters say this makes city centres healthier and more pleasant; critics argue that it hurts local businesses and makes the centre harder to reach. Should cities ban private cars from their central districts? Why or why not?
>
> **Priya:** Yes. Without cars, the air is cleaner and the streets are much safer for children and cyclists. A car-free centre also gives people space to walk and relax, which is something most cities are missing.
>
> **Tomas:** I disagree. Many small shops in city centres depend on customers who drive in, especially for large purchases. Older people and people with disabilities may also find it much harder to get to the centre if they can't drive there.

Five-word summaries: *Priya: cleaner, safer, more space. Tomas: shops and access suffer.* Missing angle: what happens to shops in practice, and what the city can do to protect access.

<!-- rich_text: Model A: a 3-level post -->
> I agree with Priya that cities should ban cars in the center. Cars make a lot of pollution and it is not healthy for people who live there. Also it is dangerous for children because drivers drive very fast. In my hometown there is one street without cars and many people walking there in the evening, it is very nice. Tomas say that shops will lose customers but I think people will come by bus. So I think banning cars is good idea for city and people will be more happy.

(About 95 words.)

**Why this is about a 3.** The post is mostly relevant and understandable, and it takes a position. But:

- **Elaboration is weak.** Its two main reasons, pollution and children's safety, are Priya's reasons in slightly different words, so the contribution is small. The answer to Tomas (*people will come by bus*) is a good start but is not explained.
- **Noticeable errors:** *many people walking there* (missing *are*), a comma splice before *it is very nice*, *Tomas say* (agreement), *is good idea* (missing *a*), *more happy* (should be *happier*).
- **Limited range:** mostly simple sentences joined by *and*, *also* and *so*.
- It falls just under the 100-word guidance, which makes full development harder.

<!-- rich_text: Model B: a 4-level post -->
> I understand Tomas's concern about small businesses, but I still believe city centres should be closed to private cars. In my opinion, the biggest advantage is not only cleaner air but also how people use the space. When a street is full of cars, people just pass through it. However, when it becomes pedestrian, people stay longer, sit in cafés and visit more shops, so businesses can actually earn more. For example, in my hometown the main shopping street became car-free three years ago, and now it is crowded with families every weekend. Of course, the city have to provide good public transport and parking outside the centre, otherwise older people would have difficulty to reach it. Overall, I think the benefits are bigger than the problems.

(About 130 words.)

**Why this is about a 4.** It is a relevant contribution that is easy to follow. It answers Tomas with a new reason (people linger and spend more in pedestrian streets) instead of repeating Priya, supports it with an example, and adds a practical condition. Structures vary: a contrast with *not only... but also*, time clauses with *when*, a conditional idea with *otherwise*.

Few errors remain: *the city have to* should be *has to*; *difficulty to reach it* should be *difficulty reaching it*. Word choice is appropriate but sometimes general (*the benefits are bigger than the problems*). The example is helpful but brief, and the final sentence repeats rather than extends.

<!-- rich_text: Model C: a 5-level post -->
> Tomas is right that shop owners worry about losing customers who drive, but I think that worry assumes people shop the same way on a quiet street as on a busy road, and they don't. A street packed with traffic is somewhere you hurry through; a pedestrian street is somewhere you linger. When my hometown turned its main avenue into a walking zone, the cafés that had complained the loudest were soon putting tables outside and staying open later. The key, in my view, is what the city offers in return: frequent buses, parking garages at the edge of the centre, and delivery hours for shops. With those in place, a car-free centre is not a loss for businesses or for older residents but a gain in safety, cleaner air and public space that everyone can use.

(About 145 words.)

<!-- rich_text: What makes Model C a 5-level post -->
- **A very clear contribution.** It answers the professor's question and directly engages Tomas, but its central idea, that people behave differently in pedestrian streets, is the writer's own.
- **Well-elaborated support.** One reason, explained (*hurry through* versus *linger*), illustrated with a specific example (the cafés that complained became the ones that benefited), and then strengthened with concrete conditions that answer Tomas's access concern.
- **Effective syntactic variety.** A concession with *but*, a parallel contrast joined by a semicolon, a time clause, a relative clause (*the cafés that had complained the loudest*), a colon introducing a list, and a *not... but* structure in the conclusion.
- **Precise, idiomatic word choice.** *Packed with traffic*, *linger*, *in return*, *at the edge of the centre*.
- **A wrap that adds something.** The final sentence brings in Priya's benefits only after the conditions are set, so it concludes rather than repeats.
- **Almost no errors.**

Notice what Model C does *not* do: no general introduction, no list of five reasons, no memorised phrase like *This is a controversial issue in today's society*.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: add something new -->
Your main reason must not belong to a classmate
Before you write your reason, check it against both student posts. If either student already said it, find a different reason with the six lenses, or take their reason further by adding a condition, a consequence or a counter-example. A new angle is what makes the contribution yours.

<!-- rich_text: Prompt 2: required lecture attendance -->
> **Dr. Lindqvist (Education):** Many universities now record lectures and post them online the same day. This has started a debate about attendance. Should attendance at lectures still be required when recordings are available? Why or why not?
>
> **Kenji:** No. Recordings let students learn at their own pace. I can pause, rewatch difficult parts and study at the time of day when I concentrate best. Forcing everyone into the room wastes time for students who learn better alone.
>
> **Amara:** Yes. Being in the room keeps students accountable, and live lectures allow questions and discussion that a video can't replace.

A 5-level response:

> Kenji makes a good case for flexibility, and recordings are a great safety net when someone is sick. Still, I don't think they should replace required attendance, mainly because a recording removes the part of a lecture that is hardest to find anywhere else: the chance to interrupt. In a live class, a confused student can ask a question the moment the confusion happens, and a professor who sees twenty puzzled faces can try a different explanation. With a video, you can pause, but you can't ask. There is also a practical problem that Amara only touches on. Many students, me included, intend to watch the recordings and then quietly fall behind, because nothing forces us to start. A compromise could work, though: attendance required for most sessions, with a set number of recorded absences allowed each term.

(About 150 words.)

**Notes.** The writer concedes to Kenji, develops Amara's brief idea into a precise new reason (*the chance to interrupt*), adds a second, personal reason that goes beyond what Amara said, and closes with a specific compromise. This is what the guide means by relevant, well-elaborated support.

<!-- rich_text: Common traps -->
- **The essay introduction.** *Nowadays, technology plays a very important role in education...* uses time and words without contributing anything.
- **Restating a classmate.** Agreeing is fine; using their reason as your main support is not a contribution.
- **Copying phrases from the posts.** Paraphrase or summarise in a few words of your own.
- **Answering the topic instead of the question.** The professor asks *Should attendance be required?*, not *Are recordings good?*
- **Listing many reasons.** Three undeveloped reasons are weaker than one reason with an explanation and an example.
- **Invented statistics.** A made-up figure such as *studies show 73% of students...* adds little and can sound unconvincing. A concrete personal example or realistic scenario does the job better.
- **Sitting on the fence.** *Both sides have good points* with no decision is not a clear position. A balanced post still chooses.
- **No time to proofread.** Agreement, articles and verb forms are the errors that pull a 5-level idea down to a 4.

<!-- rich_text: The 10-minute plan -->
| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| 0:00 to 1:30 | Read the question, the professor post, both students; 5-word summaries |
| 1:30 to 2:30 | Choose a side; find your new reason and one example |
| 2:30 to 8:30 | Write the five moves |
| 8:30 to 10:00 | Proofread and improve one or two word choices |

The discussion is the last thing you write on the test, after Build a Sentence and the email. Fatigue is real at this point, which is another reason to rely on a fixed structure rather than on inspiration. If you finish writing early, use the spare time to add one sentence of example or condition, then proofread again.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: draft first, polish last -->
Do not edit while drafting
Write the whole five-move paragraph first, even if a sentence feels clumsy. Fix wording only in the final 90 seconds. Learners who stop to perfect each sentence often run out of time before the example, which is the part the scoring guide rewards most.

<!-- checklist: Proofreading checklist for the post -->
- The first sentence answers the professor's exact question.
- At least one classmate is mentioned by name and paraphrased, not copied.
- The main reason is new, not a classmate's reason.
- The reason is explained and followed by a specific example.
- The final sentence adds a condition, consequence or solution.
- The post is over 100 words.
- Subjects and verbs agree, especially after names and plural nouns.
- Articles are present before singular countable nouns.
- No comma joins two complete sentences.

<!-- rich_text: Drills -->
**Drill 1: new reason or not?** Prompt 1 (car-free centres). For each proposed main reason, say whether it is a new contribution or a restatement.
1. *Cars produce pollution that harms residents.*
2. *Delivery vans would still need access, so a total ban is unrealistic.*
3. *Without traffic noise, the centre could host outdoor concerts and markets.*

**Drill 2: paraphrase a classmate in eight words or fewer.**
4. Kenji's post (Prompt 2).
5. Amara's post (Prompt 2).

**Drill 3: correct the errors** in this extract from a 4-level post.
6. *I agree with Amara because students who attends live lectures can asks questions. When I was in first year, I have missed many classes and it was difficult to catch up.*

**Drill 4: plan in 60 seconds.** Write a position, a new reason and an example for this original prompt.
7. *Dr. Osei (Economics): Some governments give large subsidies to space programmes, while others argue the money should go to problems at home, such as housing. Should governments fund space exploration? Student A says yes, because space research produces useful technologies. Student B says no, because people need affordable homes first.*

<!-- rich_text: Drill answers -->
1. **Restatement.** Priya already argued that the air would be cleaner. You could extend it, but as a main reason it is not new.
2. **New.** Neither student mentions deliveries; this practicality angle supports a partial ban or a compromise.
3. **New.** It develops the idea of public space in a specific direction neither student took.
4. Example: *Kenji values learning at his own pace.*
5. Example: *Amara thinks live classes keep students engaged.*
6. Corrected: *students who attend live lectures can ask questions* (plural subject *who* refers to *students*; *can* is followed by the base form). *When I was in first year, I missed many classes, and it was difficult to catch up* (simple past with a finished past time; a comma before *and* joining two clauses is optional but helpful).
7. Sample plan. Position: *Fund space exploration, but at a capped level.* New reason: *space programmes are long-term investments that create skilled jobs at home* (people/money lenses, different from Student A's technology point). Example: *a regional engineering university that trains satellite technicians who stay and work locally.* Wrap: *a fixed share of the budget lets governments do both.* Any plan works if the reason is not one of the students' reasons and the example is specific.

<!-- rich_text: Priorities by level -->
**From B1 toward B2.** Get the structure right: position in sentence one, one reason, one example, over 100 words. Practise five-word summaries of student posts until you can do them in 30 seconds.

**From B2 toward C1.** Make your contribution genuinely new and develop it. Use the six lenses, add a concession clause and a condition, and replace general words (*good*, *bad*, *a lot of problems*) with precise ones.

**From C1 toward C2.** Aim for control and economy: one sharp idea, vividly illustrated, in varied but natural sentences. Use the final minute to remove filler and fix the single error most likely to distract a reader.

These priorities follow the CEFR writing descriptors ETS publishes for its bands; ETS does not publish a score conversion for this one task.

<!-- rich_text: A weekly practice routine -->
- **Three times a week:** one timed post (10 minutes) on a new prompt, then compare it with the five-move structure and the proofreading checklist.
- **Once a week:** take an old post and rewrite it to replace the main reason with a new lens; this builds idea flexibility.
- **Daily (10 minutes):** read two short opinion pieces or student forum threads in English and write a five-word summary of each writer's reason.
- **Weekly:** a full Writing section in the [TOEFL simulator practice area](https://toefl.schood.online/practice), so you write the discussion post after Build a Sentence and the email, tired, exactly as on test day.

Build a personal bank of prompts across subjects: education, technology, environment, business, health, cities, media and culture. Professors' questions on the test come from many fields, but they never require specialist knowledge.

<!-- rich_text: Frequently asked questions -->
**Do I have to agree with one of the students?**
No. You can agree with one, disagree with both, or take a middle position. You must state your own opinion and support it.

**Is first person allowed?**
Yes. *I think*, *in my view* and personal examples are normal in an online class discussion. ETS's task description says test takers support their opinion with their own reasoning, experiences or knowledge.

**Can I use a template?**
A structure (the five moves) is helpful. A pre-written paragraph that could fit any topic is not, because the post must be a relevant contribution to this particular discussion.

**Is 100 words enough?**
ETS says an effective response contains at least 100 words. In practice, posts in the 120 to 170 range give you room for a developed reason and an example.

**Does the task differ from the old TOEFL discussion task?**
ETS's 2026 score-scale report states that the academic discussion task remained unchanged in the update. What changed around it is the rest of the Writing section and the 1 to 6 reporting scale.
