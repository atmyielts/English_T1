<!-- rich_text: Why the interview matters -->
Take an Interview is the second and final Speaking task, and the last thing you do on the updated TOEFL iBT. A prerecorded interviewer asks you four questions inside a realistic situation, such as applying for a scholarship or taking part in a research study, and you have 45 seconds to answer each one. Each answer is scored from 0 to 5, so the interview can contribute up to 20 of the 55 raw Speaking points in the ETS blueprint.

This is where the test hears your own English: your ideas, your grammar under pressure, your vocabulary and your delivery. Many learners find it the most stressful task because there is no preparation time shown in the ETS description and no text to lean on. The fix is not to memorise answers. It is to recognise what kind of question you are hearing in the first few seconds and to have a simple, reliable shape for each kind.

This module shows you the four-question progression, the qualities ETS looks for at each score level, four answer frameworks, two complete original interviews with model answers, a weaker answer with a step-by-step upgrade, and drills you can practise with a timer.

<!-- checklist: What you will be able to do -->
- Recognise each question type within the first seconds of hearing it
- Answer the question directly in your first sentence
- Use a matching framework to develop each answer for about 45 seconds
- Control your pace so you finish your point before time runs out
- Show a range of grammar, especially past, present and conditional forms
- Connect ideas with natural spoken signposts
- Review your recordings against the ETS level descriptions

<!-- rich_text: The task at a glance -->
From the ETS overview and the 2026 blueprint:

- **Position:** after Listen and Repeat, at the end of the Speaking section and the test.
- **Items:** 4 questions in one interview scenario.
- **Setting:** a simulated conversation with a prerecorded interviewer. ETS mentions situations like scholarship applications and research studies. A short scenario introduction is given in audio and print, with visuals such as a photo of the interviewer.
- **Response time:** 45 seconds for each answer.
- **Progression:** early questions ask about facts and personal experience; later questions ask you to express and support opinions on broader issues.
- **Scoring:** each answer 0–5; the blueprint lists Speaking as AI scored.
- **Section:** the full Speaking section (Listen and Repeat plus the interview) has an approximate base time of 8 minutes and is linear.

Because this is the final task, you will do it after well over an hour of testing with no scheduled breaks. Part of your preparation is simply practising speaking well when you are tired.

<!-- rich_text: What ETS says the task measures -->
The blueprint describes the interview as speaking spontaneously and meaningfully in an interview format: responding with clear, coherent elaboration using accurate grammar, varied vocabulary and intelligible prosody. The ETS overview lists four qualities of a good response:

1. It **answers the question** with appropriate and coherent elaboration.
2. It **keeps a good conversational pace.**
3. It is **intelligible** and uses rhythm and intonation to convey meaning.
4. It uses **a range of vocabulary and grammar** effectively and accurately.

The blueprint also says the prompts are designed to draw out different speech functions: description, explanation, opinion, suggestion, prediction and narration, with difficulty increasing across the task. Topics are chosen to be accessible to a general audience and can touch areas like life sciences, history and business, without needing specialist knowledge.

In other words, the interview is not testing whether your opinion is correct or impressive. It is testing whether you can communicate an answer clearly, develop it naturally and control your English while you do.

<!-- rich_text: How the interview is scored -->
ETS publishes a 0–5 scoring guide for this task. Summarised in our own words:

| Score | Typical features |
|---|---|
| 5 | Fully answers; well developed; natural pace; easy to understand; accurate range of language |
| 4 | Answers and develops, but links may be weak; some pauses; occasional effort to understand; adequate language |
| 3 | Answers with limited development; choppy, many fillers; some unclear words; limited range |
| 2 | Barely connected to the question; mostly repeats question words; often hard to understand |
| 1 | Vaguely related; isolated words or phrases; mostly unintelligible |
| 0 | No response, no English, unrelated, or only "I don't know" |

Two details in the ETS materials are worth noticing. First, the sample answers that ETS scores at 5 contain natural fillers such as "um" and "uh". The scoring comment explains that these pauses are natural and do not interfere with meaning. You do not need to sound like a news reader. Second, the step from 5 to 4 is often about connection and development: a 4-level answer may be on topic and elaborated but lack effective connectors between sentences, or pause in ways that slightly affect the flow.

So the target is clear, connected, developed speech at a comfortable conversational pace. Perfection is not required; clarity and development are.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: answer in the first sentence -->
Answer first, explain second
Your first sentence should directly answer the question in the interviewer's terms: "Yes, I think cities should build more bike lanes" or "The last trip I took was to Bukhara." Development comes after the answer, never instead of it.

<!-- rich_text: How the four questions progress -->
ETS describes the progression in general terms, and the sample interview follows it closely. You can plan for this pattern:

| Question | Typical focus | Main tense or function |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A personal fact or a recent experience | Past or present; description |
| 2 | Your reaction, habit or experience related to the topic | Present or past; explanation |
| 3 | Your opinion on a claim about the topic | Opinion with reasons |
| 4 | A broader issue, policy or prediction | Opinion, suggestion, conditionals |

The interviewer often bridges between questions with a short comment and a new idea ("Good. Now I'd like to ask..."). Each question can contain two parts, such as "Why did you go there? What did you like about it?" Answer both parts. Missing half of a two-part question is one of the most common ways to lose development.

The questions also get conceptually harder. Question 1 asks about your own life, which you know. Question 4 asks about society, a government or an institution, which requires you to reason. Plan your energy accordingly: answers 1 and 2 should feel easy and confident, which frees attention for 3 and 4.

<!-- rich_text: Framework 1: personal fact or experience -->
Use **Answer – Detail – Detail – Feeling** for questions about what you did, where you went or what you study.

1. **Answer** the question in one sentence.
2. **Detail 1:** a specific fact (where, when, who, what).
3. **Detail 2:** a second specific fact, an example or a short story moment.
4. **Feeling or result:** what you liked, learned or noticed.

Example question: *"Tell me about a place you enjoy visiting in your free time."*

> "A place I really enjoy is the botanical garden near my university. I usually go there on Sunday mornings when it's quiet. There's a greenhouse with tropical plants, and I like walking through it because it's warm even in winter. Last month I went with my younger sister and she spent half an hour just looking at the cactus collection. For me it's the best way to reset before a busy week."

Notice the past tense for the specific story ("Last month I went...") and the present simple for habits ("I usually go..."). Switching tenses correctly is one of the easiest ways to show grammatical range.

<!-- rich_text: Framework 2: reaction, habit or explanation -->
Use **React – Reason – Example – Wrap** for questions like "How do you feel about...?", "Why do you think you react that way?" or "How do you usually...?"

1. **React:** state your feeling or habit directly.
2. **Reason:** explain why with "because" or "the main reason is".
3. **Example:** give one concrete situation that shows it.
4. **Wrap:** add a nuance or a short conclusion ("That said, ..." or "So overall...").

Example question: *"Some people feel nervous when speaking in front of a group. How do you usually feel, and why?"*

> "Honestly, I used to feel very nervous, but now it's mostly fine. I think the reason is practice. In my second year we had to present a project every two weeks, so I got used to people looking at me. I remember my first presentation, my hands were shaking and I spoke way too fast. By the end of the year, I was actually enjoying the questions. That said, I still get a little nervous when the audience is people I don't know."

The "That said" ending adds nuance without starting a new topic, which is exactly the kind of natural elaboration the rubric rewards.

<!-- rich_text: Framework 3: opinion on a claim -->
Use **Position – Reason – Example – Concession – Close** when the interviewer presents a claim and asks if you agree.

1. **Position:** agree, disagree or partly agree, using the words of the question.
2. **Reason:** your strongest reason.
3. **Example:** a short example from your experience or from something you know.
4. **Concession (optional):** briefly admit the other side.
5. **Close:** repeat your position in new words.

Example question: *"Some people say students should choose a career before they start university. Do you agree?"*

> "I partly disagree. I think it's good to have a general direction, but choosing an exact career at seventeen is really difficult. The main reason is that you discover what you're good at by trying things. For example, my cousin started in economics and then took one programming course and completely changed direction, and now she loves her work. Of course, students who know early can save time. But for most people, I'd say the first year should be for exploring."

If time is short, skip the concession; never skip the reason and the example.

<!-- rich_text: Framework 4: broader issue, policy or prediction -->
Use **Position – Who benefits – How or condition – Close** for questions about what governments, schools or companies should do, or what will happen in the future.

1. **Position:** a clear yes, no or "yes, if...".
2. **Who benefits (or who is harmed):** name a group and explain the effect.
3. **How or condition:** explain how it would work, or the condition that makes it a good idea. Conditionals ("If cities did this, ...") belong here.
4. **Close:** a short concluding sentence, possibly with a prediction.

Example question: *"Should companies allow employees to work from home two days a week?"*

> "Yes, I think two days a week is a good balance. The people who benefit most are employees with long commutes, because they'd get back hours every week and probably feel less tired. If companies set clear rules, like which days the whole team comes in, they wouldn't lose the face-to-face meetings that help people work together. Some managers worry about productivity, but that can be measured by results rather than hours at a desk. I'd expect more companies to move to this model in the next few years."

Question 4 is the best place to show advanced grammar naturally: second conditionals, modal verbs like "would" and "might", and predictions.

<!-- rich_text: What 45 seconds sounds like -->
Forty-five seconds is longer than most learners expect when they practise, and shorter than they expect on the test when they are nervous. At an unhurried conversational pace, many speakers produce roughly 90 to 120 words in that time. That is our estimate for planning, not an ETS figure; your natural pace may be different.

A simple timing map for a 45-second answer:

- **0–5 seconds:** direct answer.
- **5–25 seconds:** main reason or first detail, with explanation.
- **25–40 seconds:** example or second detail.
- **40–45 seconds:** a short close.

If you finish your full answer with ten seconds left, add one more specific detail about your example rather than starting a new idea. If the timer is about to end and you are mid-example, finish the sentence you are in. A complete final sentence leaves a better impression than a cut-off list.

Use a timer in all practice. ETS itself recommends practising Speaking tasks with a timer so you learn how much speaking fills the time.

<!-- rich_text: Model interview 1: scholarship committee -->
**Scenario (original):** You are applying for a scholarship that pays for one semester of study at a partner university abroad. A member of the scholarship committee is interviewing you online.

**Question 1:** "Thanks for joining us. To start, tell me about a subject you're studying at the moment. What do you find most interesting about it?"

> "Right now I'm studying environmental engineering, and this semester we have a course on water treatment. What I find most interesting is how simple ideas can solve big problems. For example, last week we built a small sand filter in the lab, and the water that came out was, um, surprisingly clean. In my region water quality is a real issue, so it feels very practical. It's also the reason I want to study abroad, because the partner university has a research center on exactly this topic."

**Question 2:** "Studying abroad can be challenging. Think about a time you had to adapt to something unfamiliar. How did you handle it?"

> "When I moved from my hometown to Tashkent for university, everything was new: the size of the city, the transport, living with roommates. At first I felt a bit lost, honestly. What helped was making a routine. I learned two metro routes, I found a library where I could study every evening, and I joined a volunteer group, which is where I met most of my friends. After about a month the city felt like mine. So I think I adapt by making small, familiar habits in a new place."

**Question 3:** "Some people say students learn more from living in another country than from their classes there. Do you agree?"

> "I'd say I mostly agree. Classes are important, of course, but you can take a similar course online from almost anywhere. What you can't get online is the daily experience of solving problems in another language and culture, like opening a bank account or working on a team with people who think differently. Those situations force you to communicate and to be flexible. A friend of mine studied in Korea for a semester, and he says the group projects taught him more than any lecture. So for me, living there is the bigger lesson."

**Question 4:** "Last question. Many universities now offer virtual exchange programs, where students take online classes with a partner university without traveling. Should scholarship money go to virtual programs instead of traditional study abroad?"

> "I don't think it should replace study abroad, but some of the money could go to virtual programs. The main advantage is access: a virtual exchange costs much less, so the same budget could reach many more students, including those who can't leave their families or jobs. But if all the money went online, students would lose the experience of actually living somewhere new, which, as I said, is a big part of the learning. So I'd suggest a mix, maybe virtual programs for first-year students and full scholarships for the strongest applicants later."

**Commentary.** Each answer starts with a direct response and develops it with specific detail. The tenses shift naturally: present for current study, past for the move, present for opinions, conditionals for the policy question. The answer to question 4 refers back to the answer to question 3 ("as I said"), which makes the conversation feel connected. The "um" in the first answer is the kind of natural filler that ETS does not treat as a problem.

<!-- rich_text: Model interview 2: research study on free time -->
**Scenario (original):** You have agreed to take part in a research study about how young adults spend their free time. A researcher will ask you some questions.

**Question 1:** "Thank you for your time. First, tell me what you usually do on a free afternoon. Where do you go, and who are you with?"

> "On a free afternoon I usually meet two or three friends from my course. We often go to a café near the river, because it's quiet and the tea is cheap. Sometimes we play board games there; last Saturday we played a strategy game for almost three hours. If the weather is good, we walk along the embankment instead. It's nothing special, but it's a nice break from studying, and it's when we really talk about things other than exams."

**Question 2:** "Think about a hobby or activity you started recently or would like to start. What attracted you to it?"

> "A few months ago I started learning to cook properly, not just simple things. What attracted me was, uh, two things really. First, I moved into a shared flat and got tired of eating fast food. Second, my grandmother is an amazing cook, and I wanted to learn her recipes before I leave for my studies. So now every weekend she teaches me one dish. Last week it was plov, and it was much harder than I thought, especially getting the rice right. But I love that it connects me to my family."

**Question 3:** "Some people believe free time is more valuable when it's planned, while others prefer to keep it unplanned. Which do you prefer, and why?"

> "I prefer a mix, but if I have to choose, I'd say planned. The reason is that when I don't plan, my free time just disappears into my phone, and at the end of the day I feel like I didn't really rest. When I plan something, even something small like a walk or a film with friends, I actually look forward to it and I remember it later. That said, I don't plan every minute. I think one unplanned evening a week is healthy, so I can be spontaneous."

**Question 4:** "Some cities are considering keeping public libraries and sports centers open until midnight, so that young people have more places to spend their evenings. Is this a good use of public money?"

> "I think it's a good idea, at least in big cities. The people who'd benefit most are students and young workers who are busy during the day. Right now, after eight or nine in the evening, the only places to go are cafés or shopping malls, which cost money. If libraries and sports centers stayed open, young people would have safe, free places to study or exercise. Of course, it would cost more for staff and electricity, so maybe cities could start with a few centers and see how many people use them."

**Commentary.** The researcher's questions move from routine, to personal story, to preference, to public policy. The models answer every part of each question (where and who; what attracted you; which and why). Question 4 uses a second conditional ("If libraries... stayed open, young people would have...") and ends with a practical suggestion, one of the speech functions the blueprint lists.

<!-- rich_text: A 3-level answer and how to upgrade it -->
Here is a weaker answer to question 3 from the second interview. It is the kind of response that fits the ETS description of a partially successful answer.

> "Um... I think planned. Planned is... more good. Because, uh, when you plan, you... you can do more things. Unplanned is also good sometimes. Yes. I like planned free time. Because, um, it is good for... for life. I think planned is better. Yes."

**What is happening.** The speaker answers the question, so it is on topic. But development is very limited: the only reason ("you can do more things") is never explained or illustrated. The same idea repeats three times. Pauses are frequent and break up phrases. The grammar range is narrow ("more good" instead of "better" at first), and the vocabulary mostly repeats the question.

**Upgrade in four moves:**

1. **Answer with the question's words, once:** "I prefer planned free time."
2. **Add a real reason with "because" and a consequence:** "...because if I don't plan, I usually waste the time on my phone."
3. **Add one concrete example:** "Last weekend, for example, I planned a bike ride with my brother, and it was the best part of my week."
4. **Close with a nuance:** "But I also like one free evening with no plans."

Result: "I prefer planned free time, because if I don't plan, I usually waste the time on my phone. Last weekend, for example, I planned a bike ride with my brother, and it was the best part of my week. We went along the canal and stopped for lunch, and I felt really rested afterwards. But I also like keeping one evening a week free, with no plans at all."

The upgrade uses simple English. The gain comes from structure, specific detail and connection, not from rare vocabulary.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: when your mind goes blank -->
Narrow the question, then use one example
If you have no opinion ready, choose the side that gives you the easiest example, and talk about that example in detail. A specific, well-explained small story develops an answer better than a big idea you cannot explain.

<!-- rich_text: Connectors and signposts that carry elaboration -->
The 4-level description mentions answers that are elaborated but lack effective connectors. Signposts are what make spoken ideas feel linked. You do not need many; you need a few that you can use without thinking.

- **Giving the answer:** "I'd say...", "Honestly, ...", "For me, ...", "I mostly agree..."
- **Adding a reason:** "The main reason is...", "That's because...", "Part of it is that..."
- **Giving an example:** "For example, ...", "I remember when...", "Last year, ..."
- **Adding a point:** "On top of that, ...", "Another thing is..."
- **Conceding:** "Of course, ...", "That said, ...", "I understand why some people think..."
- **Contrasting:** "But...", "On the other hand, ...", "Even so, ..."
- **Closing:** "So overall, ...", "That's why I think...", "In the end, ..."

Avoid sounding scripted. Long written-style openings such as "In my humble opinion, there are numerous factors to consider" waste seconds and sound unnatural in a conversation. Short, conversational signposts serve you better.

<!-- rich_text: Delivery: pace, pauses and intonation -->
The rubric treats delivery as a central part of the score: conversational pace, natural pauses, intelligible pronunciation and rhythm and intonation that convey meaning.

- **Pace.** Aim for steady and unhurried. Speaking fast to fit more words in usually increases errors and blurs pronunciation.
- **Pauses.** Pause between ideas, not in the middle of phrases. "I think (pause) the main reason is cost" sounds natural; "I think the main (pause) reason (pause) is cost" sounds choppy.
- **Fillers.** Occasional "um" is normal. Constant fillers, especially after every few words, are a sign of searching for language and are mentioned in the 3-level description. A short silent pause is often better than a filler.
- **Intonation.** Let your voice rise and fall. Stress the words that matter: "I *really* disagree", "the *main* reason". Flat, monotone delivery makes good content harder to follow.
- **Volume and microphone.** Speak at normal conversational volume, facing the microphone. Home Edition test takers must use a microphone that is not part of a headset, so check your setup in advance.

For sound-level problems such as th, w/v, final consonants and word stress, use the pronunciation section in [Speaking: Listen and Repeat](/speaking-listen-and-repeat). The same fixes help here.

<!-- rich_text: Grammar and vocabulary range on demand -->
"Range" does not mean rare words. It means using the right forms for what you want to say. The four-question progression gives you natural places to show range:

- **Question 1:** past simple for completed events ("I visited", "we went"), present simple for habits, and maybe present perfect for experience ("I've been there three times").
- **Question 2:** "used to" for past habits ("I used to feel nervous"), present perfect for change ("I've become more confident"), and cause-and-effect language.
- **Question 3:** opinion language and comparatives ("more useful than", "not as important as").
- **Question 4:** modal verbs and conditionals ("Cities should...", "If schools offered..., students would..."), and predictions ("I expect...", "It will probably...").

Vocabulary range grows the same way. Instead of "good" five times, reach for one or two precise words: "convenient", "affordable", "stressful", "rewarding", "practical". Precision is more valuable than difficulty. A word you are not sure how to pronounce or use is a risk; choose the word you can say clearly.

<!-- rich_text: Common traps -->
- **Repeating the question as your answer.** Echoing the interviewer's sentence uses time without adding meaning; the 2-level description refers to answers made mainly of the question's language.
- **Answering only half.** Two-part questions need two parts in your answer.
- **Memorised speeches.** A prepared paragraph that does not fit the question sounds off topic. Prepare frameworks and examples, not scripts.
- **General statements only.** "Technology is very important in modern life" says almost nothing. Specific details carry development.
- **Too many reasons.** Three thin reasons are weaker than one reason developed with an example.
- **Speaking too fast.** Speed reduces clarity and accuracy.
- **Stopping early.** Finishing in 20 seconds leaves development points unused. Add specifics until near the end.
- **Getting cut off mid-sentence after a slow start.** Get to the answer in the first sentence so time is spent on content.
- **Changing your position halfway.** If you start "yes", finish "yes". Nuance is fine; contradiction is confusing.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: the last ten seconds -->
Finish the sentence you are in
When you sense time running out, do not start a new idea. Complete the current sentence and, if possible, add a three- or four-word close such as "So that's why I agree." Your response ends cleanly and the listener hears a finished thought.

<!-- rich_text: Drill 1: classify the question -->
For each question, decide which framework fits (1 personal fact or experience, 2 reaction or explanation, 3 opinion on a claim, 4 broader issue or policy) and write the first sentence of your answer.

1. "Tell me about the last book or article you read that you found interesting."
2. "Some people believe that university exams should be replaced by projects. Do you agree?"
3. "How do you usually prepare when you have an important deadline?"
4. "Should public transport be free for students? Why or why not?"
5. "Think about a teacher who influenced you. What did they do?"
6. "Do you think online shopping will completely replace small local shops?"

<!-- rich_text: Drill 2: repair the answer plan -->
Each answer plan below has one main problem. Identify it and fix it.

1. Question: "Where did you go on your last holiday, and what did you do there?" Plan: "I like holidays because they are relaxing. Holidays are important for everyone."
2. Question: "Should schools teach financial skills, like budgeting?" Plan: "Yes. Reason 1: useful. Reason 2: important. Reason 3: modern world. Reason 4: parents are busy."
3. Question: "Some people prefer studying alone; others prefer groups. Which do you prefer?" Plan: "I prefer alone... but groups are good... but alone is quiet... but groups help."
4. Question: "Tell me about a skill you'd like to learn." Plan: "In my opinion, in today's globalised world, skills are a very significant factor for success in numerous areas."

<!-- rich_text: Drill 3: 45-second sprints -->
Set a timer for 45 seconds and record an answer to each prompt. Do one round per day. After each answer, check: Did I answer in the first sentence? Did I give at least one specific example? Did I finish before the timer?

1. "Describe a place in your city where people like to spend time outdoors."
2. "How has the way you study changed since you started school?"
3. "Some people think teenagers should have part-time jobs. Do you agree?"
4. "Should museums be free for everyone? Why or why not?"
5. "Tell me about a time you helped someone solve a problem."
6. "Do you think people will travel more or less in the future? Why?"
7. "What kind of news do you follow, and how do you usually get it?"
8. "Should universities require all students to take one course outside their major?"

There are no fixed answers for sprints. Use the frameworks and the self-check above, and compare with the models in this module.

<!-- rich_text: Priorities by level -->
**Around B1 (bands 3–3.5).** Focus on answering directly and on speaking for most of the 45 seconds. Use frameworks 1 and 2 until they are automatic. Build a bank of personal examples: your studies, your city, a trip, a hobby, a challenge, a person who influenced you. Reduce mid-phrase pauses.

**Around B2 (bands 4–4.5).** You can talk, but development or connection may be uneven. Make every answer contain one specific example. Add signposts between ideas. Practise questions 3 and 4 the most, with conditionals and comparatives. Record and listen for choppy pauses.

**Around C1 (bands 5–5.5) and aiming higher.** Polish: precise word choice, natural intonation, concise nuance ("That said..."), and smooth links between answers. Practise unfamiliar policy topics (science funding, history education, local business) so that question 4 never surprises you.

The CEFR alignment (B1 = 3–3.5, B2 = 4–4.5, C1 = 5–5.5, C2 = 6) is the ETS scale mapping; the descriptions above are typical patterns, not exact score predictions.

<!-- rich_text: Practice and review routine -->
Twenty to thirty minutes a day, four or five days a week, is a realistic routine.

1. **Warm up (3 minutes):** one Listen and Repeat set or shadowing, to get your mouth moving.
2. **Framework practice (10 minutes):** three questions of one type, 45 seconds each, recorded.
3. **Review (10 minutes):** listen to each recording. Write down your first sentence and your example. Mark long pauses and repeated words. Re-record your weakest answer once.
4. **Example bank (5 minutes):** add one new personal story or fact you can use in several topics.

Once a week, run a full four-question interview from start to finish without stopping, ideally at the end of a long study session to simulate fatigue. Every two to three weeks, take the Speaking section in the [TOEFL simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) or a full mock at [toefl.schood.online/full-exam](https://toefl.schood.online/full-exam).

- My first sentence answered the question directly
- I answered every part of the question
- I gave at least one specific detail or example
- My ideas were linked with signposts

- I used the right tenses for the question

<!-- rich_text: FAQ -->
**Is there preparation time before each answer?** The ETS description gives 45 seconds to answer each question and does not describe a separate preparation period. Practise starting to answer as soon as recording begins, with your first sentence as your thinking time.

**Do fillers like "um" lower my score?** Occasional, natural fillers do not. ETS scored sample answers containing "um" and "uh" at 5 and explained that they did not interfere with meaning. Frequent fillers that break up phrases are a sign of limited fluency.

**Should I memorise answers?** No. Memorise frameworks, signposts and a bank of personal examples. Scripts rarely fit the exact question and tend to sound off topic.

**Can I invent details?** Your answer is assessed on language and development, not on truth. A simple, believable example you can describe clearly is fine.

**What if I don't understand the question?** Use the scenario and key words you did catch, and give a reasonable answer about the topic. Answering a slightly narrower version of the question is better than saying nothing.

**Will the interviewer react to my answers?** The interviewer is prerecorded, so the questions are fixed in advance. Short bridging comments are part of the recording, not responses to what you said.
