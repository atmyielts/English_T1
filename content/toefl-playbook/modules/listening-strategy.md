<!-- rich_text: Why Listening strategy matters -->
The 2026 TOEFL iBT Listening section looks nothing like the old one. Long lectures with six questions are gone. In their place you get many short, focused audio clips: single spoken questions you must answer appropriately, quick conversations, campus announcements and short academic talks. ETS describes the change directly: instead of long listening sets, the section uses concise clips that let you show how well you understand meaning, organisation and speaker intent.

That design rewards different habits from the ones many older prep books teach. Heavy note-taking matters less. Fast recognition of what a speaker *means*, not just what they *say*, matters more. And because every clip is played only once and the section is adaptive, concentration from the first second is essential.

This module walks through all four task types with original transcripts, explains every answer and every wrong option, and gives you a pacing plan, a note-taking system, drills and a weekly routine. It assumes you know the overall test structure; if not, read [TOEFL Essentials](/toefl-essentials) first.

<!-- checklist: What you will be able to do -->
- Describe the Listening section's structure, including the router and the lower and upper modules.
- Choose socially appropriate replies to spoken questions and statements, including indirect ones.
- Follow short conversations and predict what a speaker will do next.
- Extract schedules, changes and instructions from announcements on one hearing.
- Track the main idea, examples and organisation of a short academic talk.
- Take light, useful notes without missing the audio.
- Recognise the main distractor types and eliminate them quickly.

<!-- rich_text: The Listening section at a glance -->

|---|---|
| Position | Second section, after Reading |
| Items | 47, including some unscored items |
| Approximate base time | 29 minutes |
| Design | Two-stage adaptive |
| Router stage | about 18 minutes |
| Second module | lower about 7 minutes, upper about 11 minutes |
| Scoring | Machine scored, 1 point per scored item |

The four task types and the blueprint's target item counts:

| Task type | Target items | CEFR range |
|---|---|---|
| Listen and Choose a Response | 15–19 | A1–B2 |
| Listen to a Conversation | 10 | A2–C1 |
| Listen to an Announcement | 6–10 | A2–C1 |
| Listen to an Academic Talk | 8–16 | A2–C2 |

Choose a Response items make up the largest single group, so even though each one is short, together they carry real weight.

<!-- rich_text: How the audio is delivered -->
Knowing the delivery conditions removes surprises on test day.

- **Each recording is played once.** The blueprint is explicit about this. There is no replay button.
- **Each clip has a picture of the speaker or speakers**, giving you context such as a classroom, a campus office or two students talking.
- **Questions are printed on screen with multiple-choice options**, and the blueprint says everything you need to respond remains accessible while you answer.
- **You may take notes** in every section. At a test centre you get scratch paper and pencils; at home you use a small whiteboard or paper inside a transparent sheet protector with an erasable marker.
- **Accents vary.** The ETS overview names North American, British and Australian accents; the ETS Listening page also mentions New Zealand. Expect a balanced mix of voices.
- **Some audio is AI-generated and some uses voice actors**, according to the blueprint, delivered at a natural pace.
- **Length varies a lot.** The blueprint says inputs range from very brief utterances (no longer than six stressed syllables) to extended talks of up to 250 words, with many in the 35–100 word range.
- **Language is natural.** Expect idioms, colloquial phrases, academic vocabulary and rhetorical patterns such as comparison, contrast and cause and effect.

<!-- rich_text: How the two-stage adaptive design works -->
Like Reading, Listening is multistage adaptive.

1. **Router.** Everyone begins with a first stage of mixed listening tasks, estimated at about 18 minutes in the blueprint.
2. **Second module.** Your router performance selects a lower or an upper module. The blueprint estimates the lower module at about 7 minutes and the upper at about 11 minutes, which suggests the upper module includes more extended listening.
3. **Score.** Your Listening score reflects the whole section. ETS explains that matching difficulty to ability gives accurate scores in less time.

Strategic consequences:

- **Concentrate fully from the first clip.** Router answers count twice: as points and as the basis for routing.
- **Do not try to read the routing.** Whether your second module feels long or short, hard or easy, keep using the same method. Speculating wastes attention.
- **Expect longer talks if you are doing well.** Prepare your stamina for 250-word talks even if your current level feels lower.

See [The 2026 score scale](/score-scale-2026) for how section performance becomes a band.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: one hearing -->
You hear it once, so listen first and write second
Never let note-taking cause you to miss the next sentence. If you must choose between writing and listening, listen. A short clip is easier to remember than you think.

<!-- rich_text: What ETS says Listening measures -->
The ETS claim for this section is that you can understand spoken English in academic and social contexts, including single exchanges, short conversations, announcements and academic talks, in order to identify meaning, purpose and appropriate responses.

The blueprint's evidence statements describe a progression:

- **Basic (A1–A2):** identify the main idea and context of a short message, understand important details, predict what someone will do next, recognise the purpose of a short message.
- **Independent (B1–B2):** infer meaning that is not stated, recognise rhetorical structures such as compare and contrast or cause and effect, and understand a wide range of vocabulary including idiomatic and colloquial expressions.
- **Proficient (C1–C2):** interpret implied and nuanced meaning, understand the full range of grammar used by proficient speakers, and follow colloquial and idiomatic speech with ease.

Notice how often *purpose*, *implication* and *prediction* appear. The test cares less about catching every word and more about understanding why someone is speaking and what they mean.

<!-- rich_text: How Listening is scored -->
Listening is **machine scored**: each scored item is a selected-response question worth up to 1 point. The ETS overview gives a raw score range of 0–30 for Listening, which is then reported on the 1–6 band scale. Some items are unscored, used to try out material for future tests, and you cannot tell which.

At the end of the test you see **unofficial Reading and Listening scores**, and you then choose whether to report or cancel your scores. Official scores appear in your ETS account three days after the test; ETS notes that it is rare but possible for the unofficial scores to change after quality checks.

Two practical points follow:

- **Answer every item.** Nothing in the ETS materials suggests a penalty for wrong answers.
- **Every item counts equally.** A difficult academic-talk question is worth no more than a quick Choose a Response item.

<!-- rich_text: Listen and Choose a Response: format and skills -->
You hear one short question or statement, the first half of an exchange. It is **only spoken, never printed**. Then you read four written replies and choose the most appropriate one. Topics relate to campus life.

ETS says the task measures your understanding of common vocabulary and formulaic phrases, simple grammatical structures including question patterns, socially appropriate responses, English sounds and the use of stress and intonation, and implied meaning, speaker role or context.

The hardest part is that the right reply is often **indirect**. In real conversation, people answer "Do you know where the lab is?" with "It's next to the café", not "Yes, I know". They answer "Want to get coffee?" with "I've got a class in five minutes", which means *no* without saying no. The task tests whether you hear the function of the sentence: request, offer, invitation, complaint, suggestion, surprise or question.

A three-step method:

1. **Classify the prompt as you hear it.** Is it a question, a request, an offer, an invitation, a complaint or news?
2. **Hold its key idea in mind.** Who, what, when or whether?
3. **Read the four replies and choose the one a sensible person would actually say next.**

<!-- rich_text: Worked examples: Choose a Response -->
Transcripts are shown here; on the test you would only hear the prompt.

**1. Prompt:** "You haven't seen my lab notebook anywhere, have you?"
- A. Yes, I've seen that film twice.
- B. Try the shelf by the sink. Someone tidied up earlier.
- C. I'll take notes for you tomorrow.
- D. No, the lab opens at nine.

**Answer: B.** The prompt is an indirect request for help finding something. B helps without saying yes or no. A plays on "seen"; C plays on "notebook"; D repeats "lab" and starts with a direct "No" that does not lead anywhere useful.

**2. Prompt:** "Would you mind if I borrowed your calculator for the quiz?"
- A. Not at all. Just give it back afterwards.
- B. Yes, the quiz is on Thursday.
- C. I minded my sister's shop yesterday.
- D. It's a scientific one.

**Answer: A.** "Would you mind ...?" is answered "Not at all" to give permission. Learners often fall for "Yes", which literally means "Yes, I mind". C repeats a sound-alike use of "mind". D is related but does not answer.

**3. Prompt:** "The seminar's been moved to Room 4, hasn't it?"
- A. That's what the email said.
- B. Four people, I think.
- C. I moved flats last month.
- D. It wasn't my idea to start a seminar.

**Answer: A.** A tag question asks for confirmation; A confirms indirectly. B and C borrow "four" and "moved" with different meanings.

**4. Prompt (said with frustration):** "I can't believe the library's closing early again."
- A. I know, it's the third time this month.
- B. Yes, I believe you can.
- C. It opens at eight.
- D. Please close the door.

**Answer: A.** The prompt is a complaint, so the natural reply shares the feeling. Intonation matters here: a falling, annoyed tone signals complaint, not a real question about belief.

**5. Prompt:** "How about grabbing lunch after the lecture?"
- A. I'd love to, but I've got a tutorial at one.
- B. About an hour.
- C. I grabbed it already.
- D. The lecture was about climate.

**Answer: A.** "How about ...?" is an invitation; A declines politely with a reason. B treats "how about" as "how long"; C misuses "grab".

<!-- rich_text: Response patterns worth memorising -->
Many prompts follow fixed patterns with fixed kinds of replies.

| Prompt pattern | Function | Natural reply type |
|---|---|---|
| Would you mind ...? | Request | Not at all / Sorry, I need it |
| Why don't we ...? | Suggestion | Good idea / I'd rather ... |
| Do you happen to know ...? | Question | Information, not "yes" |
| You haven't ..., have you? | Check | Information or correction |
| I can't believe ... | Complaint or surprise | Sympathy or explanation |
| Congratulations on ...! | Praise | Thanks |
| Do you want me to ...? | Offer | Thanks, that would help / I'm fine |

Three listening cues carry extra meaning:

- **Stress:** "I *said* Tuesday" corrects; "I said *Tuesday*" emphasises the day.
- **Rising intonation on a statement** turns it into a question: "You're leaving already?"
- **Hesitation words** such as "well" or "actually" at the start of a reply often signal disagreement or a problem.

<!-- rich_text: Listen to a Conversation: format -->
You hear a short conversation between two speakers and answer two questions. ETS says topics come from areas such as dining, social activities, education, entertainment, services, health, hobbies, home, shopping, communications and travel, and the ETS section page notes that conversations relate primarily to campus life.

Questions ask you to identify the main idea and context, understand important details, understand idiomatic and colloquial language, infer meaning that is not stated, recognise the purpose of what a speaker says, predict what a speaker will do next, and follow the connection between ideas across turns.

What to track while listening:

- **The problem or purpose.** Most conversations have one: something is broken, late, confusing or needed.
- **Each speaker's position.** Who suggests what, and who agrees?
- **The final decision.** Prediction questions are usually answered in the last two turns.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example: a conversation -->
> **Narrator:** Listen to a conversation between two students.
> **Man:** Hey, are you heading to the print room?
> **Woman:** I was, but the machines on the second floor are out of order again.
> **Man:** Seriously? My lab report's due at five, and I promised the professor a paper copy.
> **Woman:** The engineering building has printers, but you need a department card for those.
> **Man:** Which I don't have. Great.
> **Woman:** Actually, the copy shop on Mill Street prints straight from email. It's about a ten-minute walk.
> **Man:** Ten minutes there, ten back. That's cutting it close, but it beats turning up empty-handed. Thanks.

**Question 1. What is the man's main problem?**
- A. He has lost his department card.
- B. He needs to print a report before a deadline.
- C. He does not know where the engineering building is.
- D. His professor has rejected his lab report.

**Answer: B.** The report is due at five and he promised a paper copy, but the usual printers are broken. A distorts "Which I don't have": he never had a card, so he did not lose one.

**Question 2. What will the man probably do next?**
- A. Wait for the second-floor printers to be repaired
- B. Go to the engineering building
- C. Walk to the copy shop on Mill Street
- D. Email his professor to ask for more time

**Answer: C.** "It beats turning up empty-handed" accepts the copy-shop idea despite the time. "Cutting it close" means there is only just enough time, a common idiom. D uses "email" from the woman's line in a different role.

<!-- rich_text: Listen to an Announcement: format -->
An announcement simulates a message you might hear in a classroom or at a school event, in person or broadcast. ETS describes them as about 40–85 words long, covering schedules, directions, rules and regulations, or student achievements.

Questions ask about the main idea and context, important details, implied meaning, the speaker's purpose and what listeners will probably do next.

Announcements have a predictable shape, and knowing it lets you take minimal, targeted notes:

1. **Who is speaking to whom** ("Attention, students in ...").
2. **What has changed or is happening.**
3. **The reason** (often after "because" or "due to").
4. **The action required** ("please bring", "make sure you").
5. **An extra detail or exception** at the end, often the source of one question.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example: an announcement -->
> **Narrator:** Listen to an announcement in a classroom.
> **Woman:** Before you go, a quick note for everyone in Introduction to Statistics. Tomorrow's lab session won't be in the usual computer room, because the network is being upgraded. Instead, we'll meet in the main lecture hall and work on paper, so please bring a calculator and some graph paper. The worksheet is still due at the end of the session, but the online quiz deadline has been pushed back to Sunday night.

**Question 1. What is the main purpose of the announcement?**
- A. To introduce a new statistics course
- B. To explain a change to a lab session
- C. To cancel tomorrow's worksheet
- D. To describe a network problem

**Answer: B.** The change of room and method is the heart of the message. D is the reason, not the purpose.

**Question 2. What should students bring?**
- A. A laptop and a charger
- B. A calculator and graph paper
- C. Their completed worksheet
- D. Their quiz results

**Answer: B.** Stated directly. A is what students might expect for a computer lab, which is exactly why it is tempting.

**Question 3. What does the speaker say about the online quiz?**
- A. It has been cancelled.
- B. It must be finished during the lab.
- C. Students have more time to complete it.
- D. It will be done on paper.

**Answer: C.** "Pushed back to Sunday night" means the deadline is later. B mixes the quiz with the worksheet, which is still due at the end of the session.

**Note-taking model for this clip:** *Stats lab: tmrw -> lecture hall (network). Bring calc + graph paper. Worksheet due end. Quiz -> Sun night.* That is all you need.

<!-- rich_text: Listen to an Academic Talk: format -->
An academic talk simulates a short talk by an educator, such as part of a lecture or an educational podcast. ETS describes them as about 100–250 words, followed by four questions, designed so that no background knowledge is needed. Topics come from history, art and music, life science, physical science, business and economics, and social science.

Questions ask about main and supporting ideas, grammatical structures, inferences, the organisation of the talk, and vocabulary that may be uncommon, colloquial or idiomatic.

Talks usually follow one of a few structures. Identify the structure early and your notes almost write themselves:

| Structure | Signal phrases |
|---|---|
| Concept and examples | Take X, for example; consider |
| Problem and explanation | The question is why; the usual explanation |
| Comparison | In contrast; whereas; on the other hand |
| Process | First; then; once that happens |
| Claim and qualification | But not every case fits; that said |

The opening usually announces the topic, often through an example or a question. The signal "but" or "now, here's the thing" usually introduces the point the questions care about most.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example: an academic talk -->
> **Narrator:** Listen to part of a talk in a biology class.
> **Professor:** OK, picture a penguin, or a shark, or even a deer. What do they have in common? They're darker on top and lighter underneath. Biologists call this countershading, and the usual explanation has to do with light. Light generally comes from above, right? So the top of an animal is brightly lit and its belly is in shadow. That contrast makes a body look rounded and solid. It's exactly how painters create the illusion of depth. Countershading cancels the effect. The dark back absorbs some of the light, the pale belly brightens the shadow, and the animal looks flatter and harder to pick out. Now, there's a second benefit, at least in water. A predator looking down at a shark sees a dark back against the dark depths. Look up from below, and the pale belly blends into the bright surface. But, and this is worth stressing, not every case fits neatly. Some animals that live in near-total darkness are still countershaded, so researchers suspect the pattern may have other functions too, like protection from the sun.

**Question 1. What is the talk mainly about?**
- A. How painters use light and shadow
- B. A colour pattern in animals and explanations of its function
- C. How sharks hunt in deep water
- D. Why some animals live in darkness

**Answer: B.** The whole talk explains countershading and its possible purposes. A and C are supporting details.

**Question 2. Why does the professor mention painters?**
- A. To compare animal colours with famous paintings
- B. To explain how shading makes an object look three-dimensional
- C. To suggest that artists studied animals
- D. To introduce a new topic

**Answer: B.** Painters are an analogy for how light-and-shadow contrast creates depth. This is a purpose question about a detail.

**Question 3. According to the professor, how does countershading help a sea animal seen from below?**
- A. Its dark back matches the deep water.
- B. Its pale belly blends with the bright surface.
- C. It absorbs sunlight to stay warm.
- D. It reflects light to frighten predators.

**Answer: B.** A is the view from *above*, a reversal trap. C and D are not mentioned.

**Question 4. What does the professor imply about the usual explanation?**
- A. It has been completely disproved.
- B. It may not explain every case of countershading.
- C. It applies only to penguins.
- D. It was first proposed by painters.

**Answer: B.** "Not every case fits neatly" and "may have other functions too" qualify the explanation without rejecting it. A is too strong.

<!-- rich_text: Note-taking that works -->
In the 2026 format, clips are short, so note-taking should be light and purposeful.

- **Choose a Response:** usually no notes. Keep the prompt in your head and read the options immediately.
- **Conversations:** note the problem and the final decision only: *printers broken -> copy shop Mill St*.
- **Announcements:** use the five-part shape: who, change, reason, action, extra.
- **Academic talks:** a two-column layout works well. Left: the main idea and each new point. Right: examples and signals such as "but" or "second benefit".

Useful symbols: *->* leads to or changes to, *=* means, *x* not or problem, *+* and or advantage, *?* uncertain, *!* speaker stresses this.

Practise writing without looking at the paper. On test day your eyes should stay on the screen and your attention on the voice.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: predict first -->
Predict the answer before you read the options
After each clip, take one second to say to yourself what the answer is likely to be. Then read the options. A prediction protects you from attractive distractors that reuse words you heard.

<!-- rich_text: Distractor types to recognise -->
Wrong options in Listening follow the same few designs again and again.

| Distractor | How it works |
|---|---|
| Sound-alike | Reuses a word you heard with a different meaning (mind, grab, moved) |
| Literal reading | Answers the words, not the function ("Yes" to "Would you mind") |
| Right idea, wrong person | Assigns one speaker's suggestion to the other |
| Reason as purpose | Offers the cause when the question asks the purpose |
| Reversal | Swaps above and below, before and after, cause and effect |
| Too strong | Turns "may" into "always" or "disproved" |
| Reasonable but unheard | Sounds sensible but was never said |

When you review mistakes, write the distractor type next to each one. Patterns in your own errors tell you exactly what to practise.

<!-- rich_text: Common traps -->
1. **Choosing the option that repeats words from the audio.** Repetition is a distractor technique, not a clue.
2. **Answering "yes" or "no" literally.** Many prompts are requests or invitations in question form.
3. **Missing the turn in the last line of a conversation.** Decisions often arrive at the very end.
4. **Writing too much.** Heavy notes cause you to miss the next sentence, and the clip will not be repeated.
5. **Losing focus between clips.** Short tasks come quickly. Reset your attention at the start of each one.
6. **Being thrown by an accent.** Expect a mix of accents. Focus on stressed words, which carry the meaning in every accent.
7. **Overreacting to one missed clip.** One item is one point. Let it go and listen fully to the next.

<!-- rich_text: Timing, pacing and recovery -->
Listening is paced by the recordings, so your job is to keep up rather than to save time. The ETS approximate base time is 29 minutes; the blueprint estimates about 18 minutes for the router and about 7 or 11 minutes for the second module.

Practical pacing rules:

- **Answer as soon as you are reasonably sure.** Lingering on one question while the next clip is waiting does not help.
- **For Choose a Response, decide within a few seconds.** Your memory of the prompt fades quickly.
- **For talks, read the question carefully.** It is printed; spend your time on the question wording, not on rereading notes.

**If you miss part of a clip:** use what you did hear plus the logic of the options. Eliminate the options that contradict anything you heard, choose the most general remaining option for main-idea questions, and move on with full attention. Recovering quickly is a skill; practise it deliberately by occasionally looking away during practice audio and then answering anyway.

Practise full-length Listening in the [TOEFL simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) so that 29 minutes of concentration feels normal.

<!-- rich_text: Drills -->
Ask a study partner to read these transcripts aloud once at natural speed, or use a text-to-speech tool. Do not read them yourself first.

**Choose a Response**

1. "Do you happen to know if the gym's open on public holidays?"
   A. Yes, I happen to. B. I think it's closed, but check the website. C. I go three times a week. D. It was a public event.
2. "Why don't we split the reading list between us?"
   A. Because the list is long. B. Good idea. You take the first half? C. I didn't split it. D. The library has a copy.
3. "Congratulations on getting the internship!"
   A. Thanks, I still can't quite believe it. B. Congratulations to you too. C. It starts in June, I think. D. I'd like to apply.
4. "Do you want me to save you a seat?"
   A. Yes, I saved some money. B. That would be great, I'm running late. C. The seats are blue. D. I'm sitting down.

**Conversation**

> **Woman:** Are you still selling your bike?
> **Man:** I was going to, but my brother's starting at the university in September, so I think I'll give it to him.
> **Woman:** Oh, fair enough. Any idea where I could find a cheap one?
> **Man:** The student union runs a repair workshop on Thursdays. They fix up abandoned bikes and sell them for next to nothing.

5. Why has the man changed his plans?
6. What does the man suggest the woman do?

<!-- rich_text: Drill answers -->
1. **B.** "Do you happen to know ...?" asks for information. A answers literally and sounds rude; C and D reuse "gym" ideas and "public".
2. **B.** "Why don't we ...?" is a suggestion; B accepts it and continues the plan. A treats it as a real "why" question.
3. **A.** Congratulations are answered with thanks. B would only make sense if both had good news. C gives information nobody asked for.
4. **B.** An offer is accepted with thanks and a reason. A plays on "save"; D sounds related but does not respond to the offer.
5. He has decided to give the bike to his brother, who is starting at the university in September (detail and reason).
6. Go to the student union's Thursday repair workshop, which sells repaired bikes cheaply. "For next to nothing" means very cheaply (idiom).

<!-- rich_text: Priorities by level -->
#### Band 3 to band 4 (B1 to B2)
- Learn the response patterns table until reactions are automatic.
- Practise with short clips daily so that one-time listening feels normal.
- Focus on main idea and purpose questions before fine details.

#### Band 4 to band 5 (B2 to C1)
- Train inference: what does the speaker mean, imply or plan?
- Learn common idioms and colloquial phrases (cutting it close, next to nothing, pushed back, fair enough).
- Identify talk structures in the first 20 seconds and take two-column notes.

#### Band 5 to band 6 (C1 to C2)
- Handle the longest talks in the upper module without losing the thread.
- Catch qualifications and attitude: "that said", "worth stressing", "may have other functions".
- Stay accurate across all accents and faster speech, including reduced forms such as "gonna" and "wanna".

The bands are on the 1–6 TOEFL scale explained in [The 2026 score scale](/score-scale-2026).

<!-- rich_text: A weekly practice routine -->
This routine takes about 30 minutes a day. Consistency matters more than long sessions, so adjust the time to what you can keep up every week.

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| Mon | 20 Choose a Response prompts from a partner or recordings; classify each prompt's function |
| Tue | Three short campus conversations; note problem and decision only |
| Wed | Two announcements and one academic talk; five-part and two-column notes |
| Thu | A podcast or lecture of 5–10 minutes; summarise the main idea in two sentences |
| Fri | Mixed timed set, then a distractor-type review of every error |
| Sat | Listening section in the [TOEFL simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) |
| Sun | Rest, or relaxed listening to English podcasts or series |

Vary your sources across North American, British and Australian speakers through the week.

<!-- checklist: Review checklist after every listening set -->
- Did I classify each Choose a Response prompt by function before reading the options?
- Did I predict an answer before looking at the options?
- For each error, did I name the distractor type?
- Did my notes capture the problem, decision, change or main idea, and nothing more?
- Did I lose focus after a hard clip, and how quickly did I recover?
- Did I record new idioms with the sentence I heard them in?
- Did I practise with more than one accent this week?

<!-- rich_text: Frequently asked questions -->
**Can I replay a recording?**
No. The blueprint states that recordings are played once.

**Are the Choose a Response prompts printed on screen?**
No. ETS says the question or statement is only heard. The four replies are printed.

**Can I take notes?**
Yes, in every section. Keep them light so you do not miss audio.

**Which accents will I hear?**
ETS names North American, British and Australian accents in its overview, and its Listening page also mentions New Zealand.

**Why did my second module feel longer than my friend's?**
The blueprint gives different estimated times for the lower and upper modules, and ETS notes that test time and items may vary as the test adapts. Differences between test takers are expected.

**Will my unofficial Listening score change?**
ETS says it is rare but possible after quality checks. Official scores arrive in your ETS account three days after the test.
