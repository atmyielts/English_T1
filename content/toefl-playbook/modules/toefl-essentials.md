<!-- rich_text: Why start here -->
Most TOEFL preparation goes wrong for a simple reason: people prepare for a test that no longer exists. Books, videos and forum posts written before 2026 describe long reading passages, long lectures, integrated speaking tasks and a 0–120 score. The TOEFL iBT that ETS has delivered since **21 January 2026** looks very different. It has new task types, two adaptive sections, a much shorter Speaking section and a new 1–6 score scale.

This module is the map for the rest of the Playbook. It gives you an accurate picture of the updated test, built only from ETS's own documents: the *Updated TOEFL iBT Test Overview*, the *2026 Test Blueprint and Specifications* and the official ets.org pages. Where ETS documents give slightly different numbers, we say so instead of hiding it.

Read this module once from top to bottom before you do anything else. Then use the links at the end to go to the skill modules in the order that fits your weakest area.

<!-- rich_text: The TOEFL iBT in one minute -->
The TOEFL iBT is ETS's test of academic English. It measures reading, listening, writing and speaking in the kind of English you meet at university: textbooks and lectures, but also campus notices, emails to professors, conversations with classmates and online class discussions. ETS states that TOEFL iBT scores are accepted by more than 13,500 institutions in more than 160 countries.

There is no pass or fail. Each university, scholarship programme or agency sets its own minimum score, and you need to meet the minimum of the places you are applying to. That is why the most useful question is not "what is a good TOEFL score?" but "what score do my target institutions require, section by section?" The module [Score goals and study plans](/score-goals-and-study-plans) shows you how to answer it.

The test is taken on a computer, either at an authorised test centre or at home with a live online proctor. ETS describes the updated test as taking just under two hours, and official scores arrive in your ETS account **3 days** after the test.

<!-- rich_text: What changed on 21 January 2026 -->
ETS describes the 2026 update as keeping the purpose of the test, academic English for university admission, while adding new task types and more, shorter opportunities to show what you can do. In practice, the main changes are:

1. **New task types in every section.** Reading now opens with *Complete the Words*, a paragraph in which you type the missing halves of words. Listening includes short single-exchange items. Writing starts with *Build a Sentence*. Speaking is entirely new: *Listen and Repeat* and *Take an Interview*.
2. **Shorter texts and recordings.** Instead of a few long passages and lectures, you get many short texts (about 15 to 200 words) and recordings (from a single question up to about 250 words).
3. **Two-stage adaptive Reading and Listening.** Your performance on the first part of each section decides whether the second part is easier or harder.
4. **A new score scale.** Every section and the overall score are reported from 1 to 6 in half-band steps, aligned to the CEFR. A comparable 0–120 total also appears on score reports for a two-year transition period.
5. **Faster results.** Official scores are posted 3 days after the test.

One task survived almost unchanged: *Write for an Academic Discussion*. ETS's 2026 score-scale report says this task remained the same.

<!-- rich_text: Section order and timing at a glance -->
You always take the sections in this order. The approximate base times come from the ETS timing table; they do not include directions, which add time.

| Section | Items | Approx. time | Design |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reading | 50 | 30 min | Two-stage adaptive |
| Listening | 47 | 29 min | Two-stage adaptive |
| Writing | 12 | 23 min | Linear |
| Speaking | 11 | 8 min | Linear |

The item counts for Reading and Listening include some unscored items. ETS uses them to try out questions for future tests, and you cannot tell which ones they are, so treat every item as if it counts.

ETS's own pages describe the whole test as taking just under 2 hours and advise planning about 2.5 hours, including about 30 minutes for check-in. ETS also notes that because the test adapts, the exact test time and number of items may vary. The blueprint gives a slightly finer breakdown: a total base test time of about 1 hour 23 minutes to 1 hour 29 minutes, depending on which Reading and Listening modules you receive.

<!-- rich_text: Inside the Reading section -->
Reading measures whether you can understand written English in both academic and everyday contexts. It has three task types.

**Complete the Words.** You see a paragraph of about 70–100 words from an academic text. The first sentence is complete. After that, the second half of every second word is missing, and you type the missing letters. Each paragraph has 10 incomplete words. The task tests vocabulary, spelling, grammar and your ability to use context. The blueprint targets 30 Complete the Words items in the section, which works out to about three paragraphs. Full strategy: [Complete the Words](/reading-complete-the-words).

**Read in Daily Life.** Short non-academic texts of about 15–150 words: notices, menus, emails, text-message chains, schedules, advertisements, forms, receipts, social media posts. Each text has two or three multiple-choice questions about purpose, details, informal language and what you can infer.

**Read an Academic Passage.** A passage of about 200 words from a subject such as history, biology, economics or art, written so that no background knowledge is needed. Each passage has five questions: facts, vocabulary in context, inference, how ideas relate, and why the author includes a part of the text.

Both of the last two task types are covered in [Read in Daily Life and Read an Academic Passage](/reading-daily-life-and-academic). All Reading items are machine scored, one point each.

<!-- rich_text: Inside the Listening section -->
Listening measures whether you can understand spoken English in social and academic settings. Every recording is played **once**. A picture of the speaker or speakers appears on screen, and the questions are printed with multiple-choice options. You may take notes.

- **Listen and Choose a Response.** You hear one short question or statement, which is not printed, and choose the best reply from four written options. These items test everyday spoken English, intonation and implied meaning. The blueprint targets 15–19 of them.
- **Listen to a Conversation.** A short conversation between two people on a campus or everyday topic, followed by two questions. About 10 items in total.
- **Listen to an Announcement.** A short announcement of about 40–85 words, such as a schedule change, directions or a reminder about rules, made in a classroom or at a campus event. About 6–10 items.
- **Listen to an Academic Talk.** A short talk of about 100–250 words by a lecturer or expert, followed by four questions. About 8–16 items.

The ETS overview says the voices have North American, British and Australian accents; ETS's section page also mentions New Zealand. Details and practice: [Listening strategy](/listening-strategy). All Listening items are machine scored, one point each.

<!-- rich_text: Inside the Writing section -->
Writing has three task types and a total time of 23 minutes. It is linear: everyone taking the same test form gets the same tasks, regardless of how they did earlier.

- **Build a Sentence (10 items).** You see one sentence from a conversation, then a reply whose words and phrases are jumbled. You move the pieces into a grammatical sentence that answers the first speaker. Machine scored, one point each. Strategy: [Build a Sentence](/writing-build-a-sentence).
- **Write an Email (1 task, 7 minutes).** A short scenario asks you to write an email that does three things, for example explain a problem, ask for help and suggest a time to meet. AI scored from 0 to 5. Strategy: [Write an Email](/writing-email).
- **Write for an Academic Discussion (1 task, 10 minutes).** A professor posts a question on an online class forum and two students reply with different views. You add your own supported opinion. ETS's directions say an effective response contains at least 100 words. AI scored from 0 to 5. Strategy: [Academic Discussion](/writing-academic-discussion).

Subtracting the two timed tasks from 23 minutes leaves roughly 6 minutes for the ten Build a Sentence items. That is our arithmetic, not an ETS rule, but it is a useful planning figure.

<!-- rich_text: Inside the Speaking section -->
Speaking is the shortest section, about 8 minutes, and also linear. You speak into a microphone and your responses are recorded.

- **Listen and Repeat (7 items).** A scenario sets the scene, for example a new library assistant learning what to tell visitors. You hear seven sentences, one at a time, and repeat each exactly. The sentences get longer and more complex as you go. ETS gives a maximum of 8 to 12 seconds to record each repetition. Each item is scored 0–5. Strategy: [Listen and Repeat](/speaking-listen-and-repeat).
- **Take an Interview (4 questions).** A prerecorded interviewer asks four questions within a scenario, such as a scholarship interview or a research study. Early questions are about facts and your experience; later ones ask for opinions on broader issues. You have 45 seconds per answer. Each answer is scored 0–5. Strategy: [Take an Interview](/speaking-take-an-interview).

ETS's blueprint lists both Speaking tasks as AI scored.

<!-- rich_text: How two-stage adaptive testing works -->
Reading and Listening are *multistage adaptive*. ETS's description is simple: performance on the first part of the section is used to choose the content of the second part, so that the difficulty matches your level.

Think of each of these sections as two stages:

1. **The router.** Everyone starts with a first stage of mixed difficulty. In the blueprint this takes about 18–21 minutes in Reading and about 18 minutes in Listening.
2. **The second module.** Based on how you did in the router, you receive either a *lower* or an *upper* second module. In Reading the blueprint estimates about 9 minutes for it. In Listening the estimate is about 7 minutes for the lower module and about 11 minutes for the upper module.

Writing and Speaking do not adapt. Everyone on a given form gets the same tasks.

Why does ETS do this? An adaptive test can measure your level accurately with fewer questions, because it does not waste your time on items that are far too easy or far too hard for you. That is part of why the whole test is now under two hours.

<!-- callout: Decision rule -->
Treat the first stage as the most important part of Reading and Listening
Your router performance decides which second module you see, and the upper module is where the higher bands become reachable. Give the router full concentration from the first item, never rush it to save time for later, and do not slow down at the end of a section to "rest" before the second module.

<!-- rich_text: What adaptivity means for your strategy -->
A few practical consequences follow from the adaptive design.

- **You cannot reliably tell which module you are in.** Items in either module can feel easy or hard. Don't try to guess from the difficulty, and don't change your approach based on that guess.
- **Hard items are not a bad sign.** If the second part feels harder, that may simply mean you did well in the router. Keep working calmly.
- **Every item still matters.** Your score depends on the items you answer correctly and on which module you reached. Unanswered items cannot earn points, and nothing in the ETS materials suggests that a wrong answer costs more than a blank one, so always answer.
- **Time management is per stage.** Each stage has its own timer in practice tests, so learn the rhythm of each stage instead of one long section clock. The skill modules give stage-by-stage timing plans.
- **Do not game the test.** Some learners think about deliberately getting early items wrong to receive an easier second module. That only lowers the score ceiling. The best strategy is always to answer as accurately as you can.

<!-- rich_text: Machine scoring and AI scoring -->
The blueprint divides the 120 items of the test into two kinds of scoring. Counting task by task gives 107 machine-scored and 13 AI-scored items; the blueprint's summary row prints 108 and 12. The difference does not change anything you do.

**Machine scored.** All Reading items, all Listening items and the ten Build a Sentence items. These are scored by rules: your answer either matches the key or it does not. Each scored item is worth one point.

**AI scored.** Write an Email, Write for an Academic Discussion, the seven Listen and Repeat items and the four interview answers. ETS describes these as evaluated using natural language processing and machine learning that look at features such as fluency, coherence and grammar. Each is scored from 0 to 5 using published scoring guides. ETS also states that TOEFL tests are scored only by a centralised scoring team, never at the test centre.

The practical lesson: for the machine-scored tasks, accuracy is everything, including spelling in Complete the Words and exact word order in Build a Sentence. For the AI-scored tasks, the scoring guides reward completeness, clear organisation, range and accuracy of language, and intelligible delivery. The skill modules explain each guide in plain words.

<!-- rich_text: Scores in brief -->
You receive five scores, each from 1 to 6 in steps of 0.5: Reading, Listening, Writing, Speaking and overall. The overall score is the average of the four section scores, rounded to the nearest half band. ETS's examples: an average of 5.125 becomes 5, and an average of 5.25 becomes 5.5.

Each band lines up with the same CEFR level in every section:

| Band | CEFR level |
|---|---|
| 6 | C2 |
| 5–5.5 | C1 |
| 4–4.5 | B2 |
| 3–3.5 | B1 |
| 2–2.5 | A2 |
| 1–1.5 | A1 |

During the transition, which ETS says runs for two years after January 2026, score reports also show a comparable total on the old 0–120 scale. Many universities still publish requirements like "TOEFL 90". The full conversion tables and how universities use them are in [The 2026 score scale](/score-scale-2026).

At the end of the test you see unofficial Reading and Listening scores immediately and choose whether to report or cancel your scores. Official scores, including Writing and Speaking, arrive 3 days later.

<!-- rich_text: Test centre or Home Edition -->
ETS offers the same test in two ways: at an authorised test centre, or as the **TOEFL iBT Home Edition** on your own computer with a live human proctor watching online. ETS states that the Home Edition is the same test as at a centre. Check that your target institutions accept the format you choose.

| Factor | Test centre | Home Edition |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment | Provided | Your desktop or laptop |
| Audio | Centre headset | Speaker and microphone, no headset |
| Notes | Paper and pencils provided | Small whiteboard or sheet in a clear sleeve, erasable marker |
| Noise | Other test takers speaking | Your own room, alone |
| Dates | Fixed sessions | Frequent appointments |
| Main risk | Travel, arrival, noise | Technical or room-rule problems |

ETS says test centre administrations are offered more than 170 times a year, and Home Edition appointments are available 24 hours a day, 4 days a week, booked at least 24 hours in advance.

Home Edition has strict requirements. You must use a desktop or laptop (no tablet), one monitor and one keyboard, an internal or external speaker and a microphone that is not part of a headset, and a camera that can show the proctor the whole room. You must be alone with the doors closed, with no food or drink and with your ears and face visible. If your equipment fails the requirements on test day, ETS says you cannot test and will not get a refund or free reschedule. The [Test day guide](/test-day-guide) covers both formats in detail.

<!-- callout: Choosing where to test -->
Choose the format you can control best, not the one that sounds easier
If your home internet is unstable, you share a small flat, or your laptop is old, a test centre is usually the safer choice. If the nearest centre is far away, the dates do not suit your deadline, or you are distracted by other people speaking aloud, the Home Edition may be better. Run the official equipment check on your own computer before deciding.

<!-- rich_text: Registering step by step -->
Registration happens through your ETS account. The steps are:

1. **Create your ETS account** with your name *exactly* as it appears on the ID you will bring. ETS is clear that if the name on your ID does not match the name in your account, you will not be allowed to test and your fee will not be refunded.
2. **Check the ID rules for your country** on the ETS ID requirements page. They vary by country and testing location.
3. **Choose test centre or Home Edition**, then a date and time. Leave enough time before your application deadline for scores to arrive and, if needed, for a retake.
4. **Pay the test fee.** The fee depends on your country. ETS lists extra fees for some services. At the time of writing (checked 24 October 2026), the ETS fees page listed US$49 for registering within 7 days of the test date and US$69 for rescheduling. Always check the current fees page.
5. **Add free score recipients.** Your fee includes up to four free score reports to institutions. Choose them in advance. One ETS page says you can do this until 10 p.m. on the day of the test and another says until 10 p.m. local test-centre time on the day *before* the test. Finish by the day before to be safe.
6. **Home Edition only:** verify your identity in the ETS ID verification app as soon as you can after registering, install the TOEFL Test App and run the equipment check.

The *TOEFL iBT Information Bulletin* (PDF on ets.org) contains the full rules for registration, fees, ID and score reporting. It is worth reading once.

<!-- rich_text: Accommodations for disability or health needs -->
ETS offers testing accommodations for test takers with documented disabilities or health-related needs. Depending on the need, the blueprint mentions options such as:

- extended time or extra breaks;
- screen readers such as JAWS, magnification, braille, large print or a human reader;
- captioned audio or transcripts of spoken content for test takers who are deaf or hard of hearing.

Accommodations are not automatic. You request them through a formal process with documentation, and you must be approved **before** you register for the test. The process takes time, so start early if it applies to you. The ETS *Bulletin Supplement for Test Takers with Disabilities or Health-related Needs* explains the procedure and forms.

<!-- rich_text: Rules that surprise people -->
These official rules are easy to miss and can cost you a test.

- **No scheduled breaks.** The updated test has no scheduled break. Plan food, water and the bathroom before you start.
- **You must attempt all four sections.** ETS states that if you do not answer at least one Reading question, one Listening question, one Writing task and one Speaking task, *none* of your sections are scored. You cannot take the test only to improve one section.
- **Notes are allowed, with rules.** At a test centre you get scratch paper and pencils and cannot bring your own. At home you may only use a small whiteboard, or a blank sheet inside a transparent sheet protector, with an erasable marker. You erase it on camera at the end.
- **No writing assistance.** Spelling and grammar tools are not permitted. For Home Edition, ETS names tools such as Grammarly and spell check among the software you must disable.
- **No phones or smartwatches.** At a centre they are stored with your belongings; at home your phone is put away after check-in unless it is being used as the second camera.
- **The report-or-cancel decision comes immediately.** After the test you see unofficial Reading and Listening scores and must decide whether to report or cancel all scores. Cancelled scores can be reinstated within 60 days for a fee.

<!-- rich_text: From booking to score report: a timeline -->
Here is how the process looks in time, using the ETS figures.

1. **Weeks or months before:** book your date, check ID, choose the format, and request any accommodations.
2. **By the day before the test:** finalise your four free score recipients.
3. **Test day:** about 30 minutes for check-in, then just under 2 hours of testing. You see unofficial Reading and Listening scores at the end and choose to report or cancel.
4. **3 days after:** official scores appear in your ETS account and you receive an email.
5. **About 1–2 days later:** a PDF score report is available to download. ETS pages give this as 1 day and as 24–48 hours after scores arrive.
6. **Institutions receive scores** according to how they collect them. ETS gives approximate delivery times of 4–8 business days through ETS Data Manager and 8–10 business days through server feeds, with postal mail much slower.
7. **For 2 years:** your scores remain valid and you can send more reports, for a fee.

Count backwards from your earliest application deadline. If a retake might be needed, you want at least one more realistic test date before that deadline.

<!-- rich_text: What the 2026 test rewards -->
ETS describes the updated test as measuring two layers of ability.

**Foundational skills:** vocabulary, grammar, spelling and the ability to process language quickly. Complete the Words, Build a Sentence and Listen and Repeat measure these most directly. You cannot fake them with templates. They grow through reading and listening volume, careful attention to word forms and regular accuracy practice.

**Communicative skills:** understanding purpose, inference and organisation, and producing clear, appropriate messages. The academic passages and talks, the email, the discussion post and the interview measure these. Strategy and structure help a lot here.

A balanced plan trains both. Learners who only practise test strategy tend to stall on the foundational tasks, while learners who only "improve their English" often lose points to unfamiliar formats and timing. The Playbook modules are written to combine the two.

<!-- rich_text: Your first diagnostic on the Schood simulator -->
Before you plan your preparation, find out where you are. The Schood TOEFL simulator follows the 2026 structure, so you can practise the new task types in the right order and under time pressure.

1. Take a **full mock** under real conditions at [toefl.schood.online/full-exam](https://toefl.schood.online/full-exam): one sitting, no pauses, notes only on one sheet, phone in another room.
2. Write down your section results and, just as important, *where* you lost points: which task types, and whether the problem was understanding, speed, spelling or nerves.
3. Compare your section results with the requirements of your target institutions, using the tables in [The 2026 score scale](/score-scale-2026).
4. Choose your first two skill modules: the section furthest below its target and the task type where you lost the most points.
5. Use [targeted practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) for that task type for a week, then re-test.

A practice test is an estimate, not an official score. Use it to decide what to study, not to predict your exact result.

<!-- rich_text: Common misconceptions -->
**"The TOEFL is still four long sections with long passages and lectures."** Not since 21 January 2026. Texts and recordings are now short and numerous, and Speaking lasts about 8 minutes.

**"I can skip Speaking because my university doesn't care about it."** If you skip any section, none of your sections are scored.

**"If the second Reading module feels easy, I have failed the router."** You cannot judge the module from how it feels. Keep working accurately.

**"Old TOEFL books are fine; the skills are the same."** Some underlying skills are similar, but the task types, timing and scoring are different. Use materials built for the 2026 format.

**"AI scoring means I should use rare words to impress the computer."** The scoring guides reward clear, relevant, well-organised and accurate communication. Unnatural vocabulary usually makes responses less clear.

**"Home Edition is easier."** ETS describes it as the same test. Only the conditions differ, and its room and equipment rules are strict.

<!-- rich_text: Frequently asked questions -->
**How long is the test really?** ETS's section times add up to about 1.5 hours of base testing time, plus directions. ETS describes the test as just under 2 hours and advises planning about 2.5 hours including check-in.

**Can I go back to earlier questions?** ETS's public materials do not describe navigation in detail. Follow the on-screen directions, and practise answering each item before moving on, as in the Schood simulator.

**Is there a minimum score to pass?** No. ETS sets no passing score; institutions set their own requirements.

**How often can I take the test?** As often as you like, but ETS says not more than once in a 3-day period. See [Retakes and MyBest strategy](/retake-and-mybest-strategy).

**How long are scores valid?** Two years from the test date.

**Is there an age limit?** ETS says there is no age restriction, but the content is at the level of a first-year university student.

**Do I need background knowledge for the academic texts and talks?** No. ETS designs them so that all the information you need is in the text or recording.
