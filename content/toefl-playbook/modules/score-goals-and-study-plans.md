<!-- rich_text: Why goals come before study -->
Most TOEFL preparation fails in a predictable way: the learner studies hard but not specifically. They practise whatever feels comfortable, take a test when they feel ready, and discover afterwards that one section was far below the university's minimum. A clear goal prevents this. It tells you which sections need the most work, how much margin you need, and when you must book the test so the score arrives before your deadline.

This module walks through the planning process in order. First you find the exact requirement and translate it, whether it is written on the new 1–6 band scale, the legacy 0–120 scale or as an IELTS equivalent. Then you turn it into section targets, diagnose your current level, choose your priority sections and fit a weekly structure to the time you have. Finally you decide when to book and how to read your progress honestly.

There are no shortcuts or guaranteed timelines here. How fast you improve depends on your starting level, your hours and the quality of your practice. What a plan gives you is direction: every hour goes to the skill that most limits your result.

<!-- checklist: What you will be able to do -->
- Find and record the exact TOEFL requirement for each institution on your list
- Read a requirement written on the 1-6 scale, the 0-120 scale or as an IELTS equivalent
- Turn an overall requirement into realistic section targets with a safety margin
- Diagnose your starting point with a full practice test
- Choose priority sections based on the gap and the type of skill involved
- Pick a 4, 8 or 12 week structure that fits your available hours
- Decide when to book the test so scores arrive before your deadline

<!-- rich_text: Step 1: find the real requirement -->
ETS sets no passing or failing score. Each institution sets its own requirement, and ETS recommends contacting the institution directly when you are unsure how your scores will be used. So your first job is research, not study.

For each institution or programme, record:

- **The overall minimum**, and on which scale it is written.
- **Section minimums**, if any. Many programmes add a minimum for Speaking or Writing, sometimes for every section.
- **Whether MyBest scores are accepted.** MyBest combines your best section scores from valid tests in the last two years, and each institution decides whether to accept it. See [Retakes and MyBest Strategy](/retake-and-mybest-strategy).
- **The deadline** for scores to arrive, not only the application deadline.
- **Whether a Home Edition score is accepted**, if you plan to test at home.
- **The institution's TOEFL code** for sending scores, if it lists one.
- **The date you checked**, because pages are changing during the scale transition.

Keep these in one table. The strictest requirement on your list becomes your planning target, because a score that meets the hardest requirement meets all of them.

<!-- rich_text: Reading a requirement on the 1-6 scale -->
Since 21 January 2026, TOEFL iBT score reports show four section scores and an overall score on a 1–6 scale in half-band steps. The overall score is the average of the four sections rounded to the nearest half band. ETS gives two examples: an average of 5.125 becomes 5, and an average of 5.25 becomes 5.5.

The same number means the same CEFR level in every section and overall:

| Band | CEFR level |
|---|---|
| 6 | C2 |
| 5–5.5 | C1 |
| 4–4.5 | B2 |
| 3–3.5 | B1 |
| 2–2.5 | A2 |
| 1–1.5 | A1 |

So a requirement such as "overall 4.5, with no section below 4" is easy to interpret: upper B2 overall, and at least B2 in every section. If the institution writes its requirement as a CEFR level ("B2 in all skills"), use this table directly. For the full background on the new scale, see [The 2026 Score Scale](/score-scale-2026).

<!-- rich_text: Reading a requirement on the legacy 0-120 scale -->
Universities are updating their pages at different speeds, so many still show requirements on the old scale, such as "TOEFL iBT 90" or "Speaking 22". ETS has addressed this directly. For a two-year transition after January 2026 (through January 2028, according to an ETS blog post), official score reports include a comparable overall score on the 0–120 scale alongside the 1–6 scores. ETS says you do not need to convert anything: compare the 0–120 estimate on your report with the school's listed requirement, and submit your scores as usual.

The ETS score page also says the comparable 0–120 score represents the midpoint of the corresponding total range. In practice, this means you should treat the comparable total on your report as the number the institution will compare, and check with the institution if you are close to its cut-off.

For planning, though, you need to know roughly which band corresponds to an old requirement, especially for section minimums, because the report's comparable estimate is an overall figure. The next section gives the ETS comparison table.

<!-- rich_text: The ETS comparison table -->
ETS publishes this mapping between the 1–6 bands and the original 0–30 section scores and 0–120 totals. The total column shows the minimum original total for each band.

| Band | R | L | W | S | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | 29–30 | 28–30 | 29–30 | 28–30 | 114+ |
| 5.5 | 27–28 | 26–27 | 27–28 | 27 | 107+ |
| 5 | 24–26 | 22–25 | 24–26 | 25–26 | 95+ |
| 4.5 | 22–23 | 20–21 | 21–23 | 23–24 | 86+ |
| 4 | 18–21 | 17–19 | 17–20 | 20–22 | 72+ |
| 3.5 | 12–17 | 13–16 | 15–16 | 18–19 | 58+ |
| 3 | 6–11 | 9–12 | 13–14 | 16–17 | 44+ |
| 2.5 | 4–5 | 6–8 | 11–12 | 13–15 | 34+ |
| 2 | 3 | 4–5 | 7–10 | 10–12 | 24+ |
| 1.5 | 2 | 2–3 | 3–6 | 5–9 | 12+ |
| 1 | 0–1 | 0–1 | 0–2 | 0–4 | 0+ |

R = Reading, L = Listening, W = Writing, S = Speaking.

**Worked examples.**

- A programme asks for **90 overall**. The table's minimum totals are 86+ for band 4.5 and 95+ for band 5. A requirement of 90 sits between them, so plan for an overall 5 to be safe rather than hoping a 4.5 is read as 90 or more.
- A programme asks for **Speaking 22**. In the table, 22 is in band 4 for Speaking (20–22), while 23–24 is band 4.5. A Speaking band of 4 includes 22 but also 20 and 21; aim for Speaking 4.5 to remove the doubt.
- A programme asks for **Writing 24**. Writing 24–26 is band 5. Target Writing 5.

The pattern is simple: when an old number falls inside a band's range or between bands, plan for the band whose whole range is at or above the requirement.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: old-scale requirements -->
Plan for the band that fully clears the old number
If a requirement is written on the 0-120 or 0-30 scale, target the lowest band whose entire range sits at or above it, and confirm with the institution how it treats the new scale. This avoids landing in a band that only partly overlaps the requirement.

<!-- rich_text: Requirements written as IELTS equivalents -->
Some institutions state one requirement, often in IELTS terms, and accept "equivalent" TOEFL scores. ETS has published a comparison between IELTS Academic bands and the TOEFL 1–6 scale for tests taken on or after 21 January 2026. Here is the part most applicants need:

| IELTS | R | L | W | S | TOEFL overall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 5.5 | 5.5 | 6 | 6 | 6 |
| 7.5 | 5 | 5 | 5.5 | 5 | 5.5 |
| 7 | 4.5 | 5 | 5 | 4.5 | 5 |
| 6.5 | 4 | 4.5 | 4.5 | 4 | 4.5 |
| 6 | 3.5 | 3.5 | 4 | 3.5 | 4 |
| 5.5 | 3.5 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3.5 |

The section columns compare IELTS section bands with TOEFL section bands; the last column compares overall scores. ETS explains that for the overall comparison it rounded the average of the section values up to the next half band, to reduce the chance of placing a test taker at a higher IELTS level than their real ability.

Two cautions. First, the institution decides what it accepts as equivalent; some publish their own TOEFL figures, which override any general table. Second, an "IELTS 6.5 with no band below 6" type of requirement means you should check each section against the section column, not just the overall.

<!-- rich_text: Section minimums and why they matter -->
An overall score can hide a weak section. Because the overall is an average, a strong Reading score can lift the overall while Speaking stays below a programme's minimum. If that programme has section minimums, the overall does not save you.

Look at this profile: Reading 5.5, Listening 5, Writing 4.5, Speaking 3.5. The average is 4.625, which rounds to 4.5 overall. If the programme requires "overall 4.5, each section at least 4", the overall passes, but Speaking 3.5 fails the section rule.

This is why your planning target must include every section rule. Write your targets as a set, for example: "Overall 5; Reading 5, Listening 5, Writing 5, Speaking 4.5 minimum." Then plan for each of them.

Section minimums are most common for productive skills. If any programme on your list has a Speaking or Writing minimum, give those sections protected practice time every week, even if they are not your weakest.

<!-- rich_text: Turning an overall requirement into section targets -->
Because the overall is the rounded average of four bands, many combinations produce the same overall score. Some arithmetic helps you set targets that are realistic for your strengths.

The average of four half-band scores always ends in .0, .125, .25, .375, .5, .625, .75 or .875. Rounding to the nearest half band means:

- .125 rounds down (5.125 becomes 5, per the ETS example);
- .25 rounds up to .5 (5.25 becomes 5.5, per the ETS example);
- .375 and .625 round to .5;
- .75 rounds up to the next whole band, by the same logic as the .25 example;
- .875 rounds up to the next whole band.

Some combinations that give an **overall 5**:

| R | L | W | S | Average |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5.0 |
| 5.5 | 5 | 5 | 4.5 | 5.0 |
| 5.5 | 5.5 | 4.5 | 4.5 | 5.0 |
| 5 | 5 | 5 | 4.5 | 4.875 |

The last row shows that one section half a band below 5 can still round to 5 overall. But relying on rounding leaves no margin. A better approach is to set targets that reach the overall without rounding, play to your strengths, and respect every section minimum.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: build a margin -->
Target half a band above the minimum where you can
Test-day performance varies. Aim for section targets that would still meet the requirement if one section came in half a band lower than planned. If that is unrealistic for your timeline, choose the section where an extra half band is most achievable and focus there.

<!-- rich_text: Step 2: diagnose your starting point -->
A plan built on a guess is a guess. Before you choose your weekly structure, take a full practice test under test conditions: one sitting, sections in the real order (Reading, Listening, Writing, Speaking), timed, with no pauses beyond what the test allows. The [full TOEFL mock in the simulator](https://toefl.schood.online/full-exam) is built for this.

Treat the result as practice evidence, not an official score or a prediction. Practice tests differ from the real test in content and conditions, and your performance on any single day varies. What a diagnostic gives you is a profile: which sections are furthest from your targets and which task types inside each section are costing you the most.

Record, for each section:

- your practice result for the section;
- the task types where you lost the most (for example, Complete the Words versus Read an Academic Passage);
- whether you ran out of time;
- for Writing and Speaking, a short note on what was weak: length, organisation, grammar, clarity.

If you cannot take a full test yet, take individual sections in [simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) and add a full mock in your first or second week.

<!-- rich_text: Reading your diagnostic by task type -->
Each section is built from distinct task types, and each task type is trained differently. Use your diagnostic to find which ones are limiting you.

- **Reading:** losses in Complete the Words usually point to spelling, word forms and grammar-in-context; losses in Read in Daily Life point to scanning and informal language; losses in Read an Academic Passage point to inference, vocabulary in context and structure. See [Complete the Words](/reading-complete-the-words) and [Daily Life and Academic Reading](/reading-daily-life-and-academic).
- **Listening:** losses in Listen and Choose a Response point to spoken idioms and implied meaning; losses on announcements and talks point to note-taking and holding main ideas. See [Listening Strategy](/listening-strategy).
- **Writing:** Build a Sentence losses point to word order and grammar; low email or discussion scores point to development, register or accuracy. See [Build a Sentence](/writing-build-a-sentence), [Write an Email](/writing-email) and [Write for an Academic Discussion](/writing-academic-discussion).
- **Speaking:** inexact repetitions point to listening memory and function words; weak interview answers point to structure, development and delivery. See [Listen and Repeat](/speaking-listen-and-repeat) and [Take an Interview](/speaking-take-an-interview).

Also remember that Reading and Listening are multistage adaptive: your performance in the first part of each section decides whether you receive a lower or upper second module. Accuracy early in those sections matters. The [TOEFL Essentials](/toefl-essentials) module explains the design.

<!-- rich_text: Step 3: choose your priority sections -->
With a requirement table and a diagnostic, choose one or two priority sections. Use three questions.

1. **Where is the gap biggest?** Compare each section result with its target. The section furthest below its target is your first candidate.
2. **Is there a section minimum at risk?** A section below a programme's minimum is a priority even if the gap looks small, because it blocks admission on its own.
3. **Is the weakness a format problem or a language problem?** Format problems, such as not knowing how Listen and Repeat is scored, running out of time in Build a Sentence or writing a discussion post that does not respond to the other students, can often be fixed with focused task practice. Language problems, such as limited vocabulary or listening speed, need steady longer-term work. Most learners have both, but recognising which is which helps you plan your weeks.

Give priority sections roughly half of your study time, and keep the other sections moving with lighter, regular practice so they do not slip. Never drop a section completely: every section counts in the overall average, and every section must be attempted for the test to be scored at all.

<!-- rich_text: Step 4: match the plan to your time -->
Choose a structure based on the time until your test date and the hours you can study each week. The three structures below are templates, not guarantees. They describe how to sequence the work, not how much your score will change.

| Weeks | Best for | Weekly hours (guide) |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | Close to target; mostly format work | 10–15 |
| 8 | Moderate gaps in one or two sections | 7–12 |
| 12 | Building language level as well as format | 6–10 |

If your diagnostic shows a large gap and you only have four weeks, the honest options are to study more hours, to move the test date, or to plan for a second attempt. TOEFL allows retakes as often as you like, but not more than once in a 3-day period; the [Retakes and MyBest Strategy](/retake-and-mybest-strategy) module explains how to plan this.

<!-- rich_text: The 4-week sprint structure -->
For learners already close to their targets who mainly need format control and test-day confidence.

**Week 1: format and diagnosis.** Read [TOEFL Essentials](/toefl-essentials) and [The 2026 Score Scale](/score-scale-2026). Take a full mock. Study the modules for your two weakest task types and practise them daily.

**Week 2: priority tasks.** Daily: one timed set of your weakest Reading or Listening task type, one Build a Sentence set, and one Speaking task. Every other day: one Write an Email and one Academic Discussion response, reviewed against the rubric descriptions in [Write an Email](/writing-email) and [Write for an Academic Discussion](/writing-academic-discussion).

**Week 3: mixed timed practice.** Combine task types in timed sections in [simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice). Take a second full mock at the end of the week. Compare task-type losses with week 1.

**Week 4: consolidation.** Lighter practice, focused on accuracy rather than volume. Review [Test Day Guide](/test-day-guide), check your equipment if testing at home, and finalise score recipients. Do short daily warm-ups and rest the day before the test.

<!-- rich_text: The 8-week standard structure -->
For learners with moderate gaps in one or two sections.

**Weeks 1–2: foundation.** Read the overview modules and every module for your priority sections. Take a full mock in week 1. Build daily habits: vocabulary from your reading and listening, 15 minutes of shadowing or Listen and Repeat practice, and one writing task.

**Weeks 3–4: skill building.** Two priority sections get most of the time. Reading or Listening priorities: daily task-type sets with full review of every error. Writing priorities: four to five responses a week with rewrites. Speaking priorities: daily recorded interview answers using the frameworks in [Take an Interview](/speaking-take-an-interview).

**Week 5: checkpoint.** Full mock. Update your requirement table and your priority choice if the profile has changed.

**Weeks 6–7: timed integration.** Full timed sections three to four times a week, with the other days for targeted repair of the error types your log shows most often.

**Week 8: test readiness.** A final full mock early in the week, then lighter, accuracy-focused practice. Follow the [Test Day Guide](/test-day-guide) checklist.

<!-- rich_text: The 12-week foundation structure -->
For learners who need to raise their general English level as well as learn the test.

**Weeks 1–3: language base.** Daily reading of short academic and everyday texts, daily listening with transcripts, a growing vocabulary notebook with word forms and spelling, and grammar review targeted at the errors you make in writing. Start format work with [Complete the Words](/reading-complete-the-words) and [Build a Sentence](/writing-build-a-sentence), which reward accurate grammar and spelling.

**Weeks 4–6: task formats.** Learn and practise every task type, using the modules in order. Take a full mock at the end of week 4 or 5.

**Weeks 7–9: priority focus.** Shift to your two priority sections, with regular lighter practice in the others. Write and speak every day, even briefly.

**Weeks 10–11: timed integration.** Full timed sections and a second full mock. Review errors by task type.

**Week 12: readiness.** Final mock early in the week, light practice, test-day preparation.

Twelve weeks is a planning horizon, not a promise. If your checkpoint mocks show slower progress than you need, adjust your test date or your hours rather than hoping the final weeks will close the gap.

<!-- rich_text: Designing a single study session -->
Whatever structure you choose, sessions work best with a consistent shape. A 60 to 90 minute session might look like this:

1. **Warm-up (5–10 minutes):** shadowing, a short Listen and Repeat set, or reading a short text aloud.
2. **Main task (30–40 minutes):** timed practice in your priority task type, under test-like conditions.
3. **Review (20–30 minutes):** go through every error. For Reading and Listening, write why the right answer is right and why your choice was wrong. For Writing and Speaking, compare with the rubric descriptions and rewrite or re-record one response.
4. **Log (5 minutes):** record the error types in an error log and note one thing to change next time.

The review is where improvement happens. A session with fewer questions and a thorough review is usually worth more than a session with many questions and no review.

<!-- rich_text: The weekly review loop -->
Once a week, look back before you plan the next week.

- **Error log:** which three error types appeared most often? Plan specific practice for each.
- **Timing:** did you finish timed sets? If not, which task type is slow?
- **Writing and Speaking:** reread or re-listen to two responses from the start of the week and two from the end. Are they more developed, clearer, more accurate?
- **Targets:** has anything changed in your requirement table, such as a new programme or an updated page?
- **Next week:** choose the two task types that get the most time.

Write your weekly review in a few lines. Over a month, these notes show you what is working much more reliably than a feeling of progress.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: when to change the plan -->
Change priorities at checkpoints, not every day
Re-evaluate your priority sections after each full mock, using your error log and section profile. Changing focus every few days because one practice set went badly prevents any skill from consolidating.

<!-- rich_text: Mock tests and reading progress honestly -->
Full mocks are expensive in time and energy, so use them deliberately: one at the start for diagnosis, one or two in the middle as checkpoints, and one in the final week as a rehearsal. Between them, use section and task-type practice.

When you compare mocks, look beyond the headline number:

- Are the same task types still causing most losses?
- Did you finish each section on time?
- Did your Writing responses meet the expected length and address every part of the task?
- Did your Speaking answers fill the time with relevant development?

Be careful with conclusions from a single result. Different practice forms vary in difficulty, and your concentration varies from day to day. A trend across two or three mocks, together with your error log, is much more trustworthy than one score. And no practice result, including the simulator's, is an official ETS score or a guarantee of your test result.

<!-- rich_text: Booking the test around your plan -->
Book your test date early enough to leave room for a second attempt before your deadline if you might need one.

- **Delivery options:** the TOEFL iBT is offered at authorized test centres and as the Home Edition, which is the same test taken at home with a live human proctor. ETS lists test centre dates more than 170 times a year and Home Edition appointments 24 hours a day, 4 days a week, with registration at least 24 hours before the appointment.
- **Score timing:** official scores appear in your ETS account 3 days after the test. Delivery to institutions takes longer and depends on how they receive scores.
- **Free score reports:** your fee includes up to four free score recipients. ETS pages disagree on the deadline (10 p.m. on the day of the test on one page; 10 p.m. local test-centre time on the day before the test on another), so finalise your recipients by the day before your test.
- **Retakes:** you can retake as often as you like, but not more than once in a 3-day period.
- **Validity:** scores are valid for 2 years from the test date. Check that your test date keeps the score valid for your whole application window.

Working backwards from your deadline, the latest safe test date leaves time for scores to reach institutions, and ideally time for one more attempt. Details are in the [Test Day Guide](/test-day-guide) and [Retakes and MyBest Strategy](/retake-and-mybest-strategy).

<!-- rich_text: Common planning traps -->
- **Planning from the overall only.** Section minimums can block admission even when the overall is met.
- **Relying on rounding.** Targets that only reach the requirement through favourable rounding leave no margin.
- **Converting old requirements optimistically.** If a 0–120 requirement falls between bands, plan for the higher band.
- **Skipping the diagnostic.** Without one, you will probably spend time on comfortable skills instead of limiting ones.
- **Practice without review.** Volume without error analysis repeats the same mistakes.
- **Neglecting Writing and Speaking because they are hard to self-score.** Use the rubric descriptions in the task modules and record or save every response.
- **Booking too late.** A test date right before the deadline removes the option of a retake.
- **Trusting one practice score.** Look for trends across mocks.

<!-- rich_text: FAQ -->
**What is a good TOEFL score?** ETS's answer is that a good score is the one that meets the requirements of the institutions you are applying to. There is no general pass mark.

**My university lists only a 0–120 requirement. Do I need to convert my score?** No. During the transition, your official report includes a comparable 0–120 overall estimate alongside your 1–6 scores, so you can compare directly with the school's number. For section minimums on the old scale, use the comparison table above and confirm with the institution if you are close.

**Can I meet an overall requirement with one weak section?** Sometimes, because the overall is an average. But if the programme has section minimums, the weak section must meet its own minimum.

**How many weeks do I need?** It depends on your gap, your hours and the quality of your practice. Use a diagnostic mock and the checkpoint mocks in your plan to judge, and adjust the test date if your progress does not support it.

**Should I focus only on my weakest section?** Focus most of your time there, but keep the other sections in regular practice. Every section counts in the overall average.

**Are simulator results official?** No. They are practice evidence to guide your plan. Only ETS score reports are official.
