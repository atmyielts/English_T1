<!-- rich_text: Why Listen and Repeat matters -->
Listen and Repeat opens the Speaking section of the updated TOEFL iBT, and it looks deceptively simple: you hear a sentence and say it back. There is no opinion to form and no idea to develop. Yet it carries a large share of the section. The Speaking section has 11 items: seven Listen and Repeat sentences and four interview questions. Every item is scored from 0 to 5, so the seven repetitions can contribute up to 35 of the 55 raw Speaking points listed in the ETS blueprint. ETS does not publish how raw points are converted into the 1–6 band, but the arithmetic alone tells you that this task deserves serious, specific practice.

The good news is that Listen and Repeat rewards technique more directly than almost any other TOEFL task. Your score depends on two things you can train: how much of the sentence you hold in memory, and how clearly you say it. Learners who already speak fluently often lose points here because they paraphrase instead of repeating. Learners who are still building fluency can gain a lot by learning to hear sentences as a few meaningful chunks rather than as a long string of words.

This module explains exactly what the task asks, how ETS describes each score level, and how to build the listening memory and pronunciation control that produce exact, intelligible repetitions. It ends with original practice sets, drills with answers and a two-week routine you can start today.

<!-- rich_text: The task at a glance -->
Here is the format as ETS describes it in the updated test overview and the 2026 blueprint.

- **Position:** the first task in the Speaking section, which comes last in the test (Reading, then Listening, then Writing, then Speaking).
- **Number of items:** 7 sentences, all inside one scenario.
- **Setting:** an academic or campus-life situation, introduced both aloud and in print. A picture of the setting appears on screen, and as the sentences progress you move through parts of the illustration.
- **What you do:** listen to one sentence, wait through a short pause, then repeat exactly what you heard. The instructions in the ETS sample tell you to repeat only once.
- **Response time:** a maximum of 8 to 12 seconds to record each repetition. Longer sentences get the longer windows.
- **Difficulty:** the sentences get progressively longer and more complex as you move through the scenario.
- **Scoring:** each sentence is scored 0–5; the blueprint lists Speaking tasks as AI scored.
- **Section timing:** the whole Speaking section, including the interview, has an approximate base time of 8 minutes and is linear, which means everyone taking the same form gets the same tasks in the same order.

The scenario gives you a purpose for listening. In a typical set you might be a new assistant learning what to tell visitors, or a student volunteer learning safety rules. The purpose does not change your job: you are not summarising the instructions, you are reproducing them word for word.

<!-- rich_text: What ETS says the task measures -->
ETS frames Speaking around two kinds of evidence. Foundational skills, such as processing spoken language and producing fluent, intelligible speech, are measured when you reproduce what you hear. Communicative ability is measured in the interview, where you speak about your own experiences and opinions.

For Listen and Repeat, the blueprint subskill is repeating spoken sentences with accuracy and intelligibility. The ETS overview says a good response is an accurate repetition and clearly intelligible. The CEFR-linked descriptors in the blueprint show how expectations rise with level:

- At the basic level, a speaker repeats short, simple phrases, may omit or substitute words, but keeps the basic meaning.
- At the independent level, a speaker repeats longer sentences with mostly accurate pronunciation, and small changes in grammar or vocabulary do not block meaning.
- At the proficient level, a speaker repeats complex sentences with high accuracy, and intonation, rhythm and stress carry the meaning so that the listener barely has to work.

Notice what this means in practice. The task is not a pure memory test and not a pure pronunciation test. It is a test of how efficiently you process English. A proficient listener does not store twelve separate words; they understand the sentence, so they can rebuild it. That is why the strategies in this module start with meaning and chunks, not with memorising word lists.

<!-- rich_text: How Listen and Repeat is scored -->
ETS publishes a 0–5 scoring guide for this task. In our own words, the levels work like this:

| Score | What the repetition looks like |
|---|---|
| 5 | Exact repetition, fully intelligible |
| 4 | Meaning kept, but small changes |
| 3 | A full sentence, but meaning not accurately captured |
| 2 | Large part missing; fragmentary |
| 1 | A few words, or mostly unintelligible |
| 0 | Nothing, no English, unrelated, or only "I don't know" |

The difference between 5 and 4 is the most important line to understand. A 4-level response can still contain several kinds of small change: one or two function words missing or changed, a content word replaced by a related word, a missing or wrong tense or number marker, two words swapped, or one or two words that are unclear because of imprecise pronunciation. A self-correction is acceptable as long as you complete the sentence.

A 3-level response usually keeps most of the content words but changes several function words, loses or substantially changes a content word, or has stretches where the listener struggles to understand. A 2-level response typically starts well, then stops or fills the gap with inaccurate words, perhaps catching the last few words of the sentence. The result is not a complete, self-standing sentence.

Two practical conclusions follow. First, the small words matter: "the", "a", "to", "has been", the plural "-s" and the past "-ed" are exactly the details that separate 5 from 4. Second, clarity matters as much as memory. A perfectly remembered sentence that is mumbled, rushed or run together can drop a level.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: repeat, never improve -->
Exact beats impressive
Never improve, paraphrase or "correct" the sentence you hear. If the speaker says "can be borrowed", do not say "you can borrow". A more elegant version is still a changed version, and changed versions cannot earn the top score.

<!-- rich_text: How a set grows from short to complex -->
The blueprint explains how difficulty increases inside a set. Early sentences are short and simple, often with one independent clause. Later sentences add features such as compound verbs, dependent clauses, relative clauses and complex noun phrases. Sentences also include natural spoken features such as contractions.

You can expect roughly this shape, although exact lengths vary by form:

1. **Short, one clause:** a subject, a verb and a little more. "The gym opens at six."
2. **Longer, one clause with a phrase:** "Towels are available at the front desk."
3. **One clause with more detail:** location, time or a modal verb. "Lockers must be emptied at the end of each day."
4. **Two actions or a list:** "Please wipe the machines and return the weights to the rack."
5. **A dependent or conditional clause:** "If the pool is closed, you can still use the sauna."
6. **A relative clause or long noun phrase:** "Members who forget their card can sign in at the reception desk."
7. **Several clauses and a long noun phrase:** "Anyone who wants to join a fitness class should register online at least a day before the class begins."

The first three sentences are where you secure your points with precise, relaxed delivery. Sentences five to seven are where chunking and function-word control earn the difference between 3, 4 and 5.

<!-- rich_text: The listening half: hold the sentence -->
Most learners think of this as a speaking task, but a large part of the score is decided while you are listening. If the sentence is not fully understood when it ends, you are already repeating from a damaged copy.

Train three listening habits:

- **Listen for the frame first.** Every sentence has a core: who does what. In "Students who arrive late won't be allowed to begin the experiment", the frame is "Students won't be allowed to begin". Catch the frame and you have a skeleton to hang the details on.
- **Listen to the end.** Many learners stop listening after the first half because they start rehearsing it. The last chunk is often the longest and the most specific. Keep your attention on the speaker until the voice stops.
- **Use the picture and the scenario.** If the scenario is a laboratory, words like "goggles", "bench" and "sink" are likely. Predicting the vocabulary of the setting makes each word easier to recognise and remember.

Do not take written notes during the sentence. The pause before recording is short, and writing pulls your attention away from the sound. Your memory of the rhythm is more useful than a half-written word.

<!-- rich_text: Chunking: hear sentences as meaning units -->
Short-term memory holds a small number of units, but a unit can be a word or a whole phrase. Chunking means grouping words into phrases that belong together, so a 16-word sentence becomes four or five units instead of sixteen.

Natural chunk boundaries usually fall:

- between the subject and the verb when the subject is long: "Students who need help with research / can make an appointment";
- before prepositional phrases: "/ at the front desk", "/ on the second floor";
- before clauses beginning with "if", "when", "because", "until", "which", "who" or "that";
- around list items: "a desk, / a bed, / and a small closet".

Here is a long sentence broken into chunks:

> If the self-checkout machine isn't working, / take your books / to the desk / near the main entrance.

Four chunks. Each chunk is a picture you can see: a broken machine, you carrying books, a desk, an entrance. When you repeat, you replay the pictures in order, and the words come with them.

Speakers also mark chunks with their voice. They pause slightly, lengthen the last stressed syllable of a chunk, or change pitch. Listening for these signals is one of the fastest ways to improve. In your practice, mark chunk boundaries with a slash after you listen, then compare your marks with the transcript.

<!-- rich_text: Stress, rhythm and the weak words -->
English is a stress-timed language. The stressed syllables of content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs) are longer, louder and higher. The function words between them (articles, auxiliaries, prepositions, pronouns) are squeezed and reduced, often to a quick schwa sound.

Look at this sentence with stressed syllables in capitals:

> LAUNdry ma-CHINES are in the BASEment of EVery BUILDing.

The words "are in the" and "of" pass very quickly. This creates two problems. First, the weak words are hard to hear, so they are the first to disappear from a repetition. Second, if you say them with full, stressed vowels, the rhythm changes and the sentence becomes harder to follow.

The solution is to learn the reduced forms on purpose:

- "to" often sounds like "tuh": "GO tuh the DESK";
- "and" often sounds like "n": "BREAD n BUTter";
- "can" (positive) is usually weak: "you kn BORrow", while "can't" keeps a full vowel: "you CAN'T BORrow";
- "has been", "will be" and "should have" shrink in fast speech.

When you repeat, aim to copy the beat: strong syllables on the beat, weak ones squeezed between. This sounds more natural and, just as important, it makes every word, including the weak ones, more recognisable to the scorer.

<!-- rich_text: One sentence at four score levels -->
Take sentence 6 from the lab set: *"Students who arrive late won't be allowed to begin the experiment until they've watched the safety video."* Here is how repetitions at different levels might sound, with our explanation based on the ETS level descriptions.

**5-level:** "Students who arrive late won't be allowed to begin the experiment until they've watched the safety video." Every word is present, in order, and clearly pronounced.

**4-level:** "Students who arrive late won't be allowed to start the experiment until they watched the safety video." The meaning is intact. "Begin" became the related word "start", and the "-'ve" of "they've" disappeared, changing the tense marker. These are minor changes, so the response is not exact but keeps the meaning.

**3-level:** "Students who are late can't begin the experiment before they watch the video." It is a full sentence with most of the content, but several function words have changed, "safety" is gone, "won't be allowed" has become "can't", and the time relationship is weaker. The original meaning is not accurately captured.

**2-level:** "Students who arrive late... won't be... the safety video." The speaker holds the opening and catches the ending, but the middle is missing. The result is a fragment, not a self-standing sentence.

The lesson: the gap between 3 and 5 is not about talent. It is mostly about keeping the frame ("won't be allowed to begin") and the small words ("they've", "the").

<!-- callout: Decision rule: protect the frame -->
If you lose the middle, keep the frame
When part of a long sentence slips away, say the whole sentence frame you are sure of, using the speaker's words, rather than stopping or inventing. A complete sentence with one changed detail scores better than a fragment that trails off.

<!-- rich_text: Pronunciation focus for Uzbek and Russian speakers -->
Intelligibility is half of this task, and the sounds that cause trouble are fairly predictable. Not every learner has every issue, so record yourself and check which of these apply to you.

- **Articles.** Neither Uzbek nor Russian uses articles, so "a", "an" and "the" are often dropped or swapped, especially when they are reduced in fast speech. In Listen and Repeat, a missing article is a changed function word. Practise hearing "the" as a quick "thuh" before nouns.
- **The "th" sounds.** "Think" and "this" often become "sink" and "zis", or "tink" and "dis". Put the tip of your tongue lightly between your teeth and let air pass.
- **W and V.** "West" and "vest" can merge. For "w", round your lips and keep your teeth away from your lower lip.
- **Final consonants.** Russian speakers in particular may devoice final consonants, so "bag" sounds like "back" and "prize" like "price". Hold the voicing to the end of the word.
- **Endings -s and -ed.** These carry number and tense, so dropping them is a scoring issue, not only an accent issue. "Collected" has an extra syllable (/ɪd/); "booked" does not (/t/).
- **Weak vowels.** Many learners give every syllable a full vowel. English reduces unstressed vowels to schwa: "a-BOUT", "po-LICE", "of the". Full vowels everywhere make the rhythm heavy and blur the stressed words.
- **Word stress.** Misplaced stress can make a word unrecognisable: "deVELop", not "DEvelop"; "PHOtograph" but "phoTOGraphy". When you learn a new word, learn its stress.
- **The "h" sound.** A rough, throaty "kh" for English "h" can make words like "help" and "hall" harder to recognise. English "h" is just breath.

You do not need a perfect native accent. The ETS descriptors talk about intelligibility and appropriate rhythm and intonation, not about a particular accent. Your goal is speech that a proficient listener understands without effort.

<!-- rich_text: Common traps -->
- **Paraphrasing.** Saying the meaning in your own words. It is natural for fluent speakers and expensive in this task.
- **Correcting the speaker.** If the sentence contains a structure you would not use, repeat it anyway.
- **Expanding contractions.** "Isn't" is not the same as "is not" for an exact repetition. Keep what you hear.
- **Starting too early or too late.** Starting before the recording is ready can cut off your first word; waiting too long can run out of time on long sentences.
- **Rushing.** Fast speech feels fluent but often merges words. Speak at the speaker's pace or slightly slower.
- **Restarting from the top.** One small self-correction is acceptable; restarting the whole sentence twice wastes your window and adds noise.
- **Losing focus after a hard sentence.** One weak repetition does not change the next one. Each sentence is scored separately, so reset immediately.
- **Practising only from text.** Reading sentences aloud trains pronunciation, not listening memory. Most of your practice must start from audio.

<!-- rich_text: Timing inside the 8 to 12 second window -->
ETS gives a maximum of 8 to 12 seconds to record each repetition. That is generous for the sentence itself: even a 17-word sentence takes only a few seconds at a natural pace. The time is there so you can start calmly and finish cleanly, not so you can fill it.

A practical timing pattern:

1. **Listen** until the speaker finishes. Do not mouth the words while listening.
2. **Breathe in** during the pause. This one breath stops you from rushing.
3. **Start cleanly** when recording begins, with the first chunk at normal volume.
4. **Pause briefly at chunk boundaries**, as the speaker did. These pauses help the listener and give you a micro-moment to retrieve the next chunk.
5. **Stop** when the sentence is complete. Do not add comments, apologies or a second attempt.

If you notice an error immediately, you may correct the word once and continue. Do not go back to the beginning.

<!-- rich_text: Shadowing and memory techniques -->
These techniques build the listening memory and rhythm that the task depends on. Use short audio clips with transcripts, such as campus announcements, podcast introductions or instructional videos.

**Shadowing.** Play a sentence and speak along with it, a fraction of a second behind the speaker, copying rhythm, stress and intonation. Shadowing trains your mouth to produce English rhythm and your ear to hear weak forms.

**Delayed repetition.** Play a sentence, pause the audio, wait two seconds, then repeat. This is the real task. Gradually increase the delay to three or four seconds, which makes your memory work harder than it will on the test.

**Back-chaining.** For a sentence you keep failing, practise from the end: "by the door", then "on the shelf by the door", then "leave your lab notebook on the shelf by the door", and so on. Building from the end keeps the hardest final chunk fresh.

**Visualise each chunk.** Turn each chunk into a quick mental picture, as described above. Concrete scenes are easier to hold than abstract word strings.

**Transcribe after you speak.** Record your repetition, then write down exactly what you said and compare it word by word with the transcript. This is the most honest feedback you can get, because it shows you the function words you are losing.

<!-- rich_text: Priorities by level -->
**If you are around B1 (bands 3–3.5).** Your first goal is complete sentences. Focus on holding the frame of each sentence, even if some details change. Practise sentences of 6 to 10 words until you repeat them exactly, then extend. Work on the two or three pronunciation issues that most affect your intelligibility.

**If you are around B2 (bands 4–4.5).** You probably repeat short sentences well and lose function words in long ones. Chunking and weak forms are your main tools. Transcribe your repetitions of sentences with relative clauses and conditionals, and count the function words you drop. Aim to keep contractions and endings exactly as heard.

**If you are around C1 (bands 5–5.5) and aiming higher.** Your risk is paraphrasing because your English is flexible. Discipline is the skill now: repeat precisely, copy the speaker's rhythm and intonation, and keep delivery clean on the longest sentences. Use delayed repetition with longer delays and faster audio to build a margin above test difficulty.

These level descriptions come from the CEFR alignment that ETS uses (B1 = 3–3.5, B2 = 4–4.5, C1 = 5–5.5, C2 = 6). They describe typical patterns, not fixed rules about your score.

<!-- rich_text: A two-week Listen and Repeat routine -->
Fifteen to twenty minutes a day is enough to make visible progress on technique. Use audio with transcripts.

**Days 1–3: rhythm.** Shadow ten short sentences daily. Mark stress on the transcript first, then shadow until your rhythm matches. Record one sentence a day for comparison.

**Days 4–6: chunks.** Take ten sentences of 10 to 15 words. Listen once, mark chunk boundaries from memory, then check the transcript. Do delayed repetition with a two-second pause.

**Days 7–9: function words.** Do delayed repetition and transcribe every response. Keep an error log of the small words you lose: articles, auxiliaries, prepositions, endings.

**Days 10–12: test conditions.** Build or find seven-sentence sets that grow in length, like the worked examples above. Repeat each sentence once, with no replay, and record all seven. Then score yourself against the level descriptions.

**Days 13–14: simulation.** Take the Speaking section in the [TOEFL simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice), then review your recordings with the checklist below.

<!-- checklist: Self-review after every recording -->
- Did I repeat every content word in the right order
- Did I keep every article, preposition and auxiliary verb
- Did I keep plural -s, past -ed and contractions exactly as heard
- Did I avoid replacing any word with a synonym
- Is every word clear if I imagine a listener who never saw the transcript
- Did my stressed syllables match the speaker's
- Did I start calmly and stop as soon as the sentence was complete

<!-- callout: Decision rule: make recording the default -->
Record, do not just rehearse
Silent or whispered practice feels productive but hides your real errors. If you did not record and transcribe it, you do not yet know whether it was exact. Make recording the default for every practice session.

<!-- rich_text: FAQ -->
**Will I hear each sentence more than once?** No. Treat every sentence as a single opportunity. The ETS sample instructions tell you to repeat only once, so do not plan on a second attempt either.

**Is my accent a problem?** Not in itself. ETS describes the target as intelligible speech with appropriate rhythm and intonation. A clear accent that listeners understand easily is fine; sounds that change words, such as "sink" for "think", are what to fix.

**Should I copy the speaker's accent?** Copy the rhythm, stress and intonation, which are what make the sentence clear. You do not need to imitate a particular regional accent.

**What if I hear a word I don't know?** Repeat its sound as closely as you can and keep going. A close attempt at one unknown word inside a complete sentence is far better than stopping.

**Can I take notes during Listen and Repeat?** Notes are allowed during the test, but for this task writing usually hurts more than it helps. Your attention belongs on the sound.

**How much does this task count?** Each of the seven sentences is scored 0–5, out of 55 raw Speaking points in total. ETS does not publish the raw-to-band conversion, so think of the task as a large, trainable part of the section rather than as a precise number of band points.
