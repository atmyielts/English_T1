<!-- rich_text: Why this task matters -->
Build a Sentence opens the Writing section of the TOEFL iBT as updated on 21 January 2026. You see one complete sentence from one speaker and a reply from a second speaker whose words and phrases have been scrambled. Your job is to put the reply back together so that it is grammatical and makes sense as an answer to the first sentence.

It looks like a small puzzle, and that is exactly why many test takers underestimate it. There are **ten** of these items, they are scored automatically, and each one is worth a full point. The whole Writing section has 20 raw points (10 from Build a Sentence and up to 5 each from the email and the academic discussion), so Build a Sentence carries half of the raw points in the section. A learner who builds nine or ten sentences correctly protects a large part of the Writing score before the longer tasks even begin.

The task also rewards a skill that pays off everywhere else on the test: noticing how English sentences are put together. Word order, question formation, relative clauses and the position of small words such as *not*, *already* or *it* are exactly the things that also cost points in your email, your discussion post and your spoken answers. Practising this task is grammar practice with an immediate, checkable answer.

- Use the first speaker's sentence to predict what the reply must do.

- Recognise the six grammar patterns that appear most often in reply sentences.
- Handle a chunk that does not fit anywhere without panicking.
- Finish ten items in roughly six minutes and still check each one.
- Review your mistakes by pattern instead of by item.

<!-- rich_text: The task at a glance -->
| Feature | Build a Sentence |
|---|---|
| Position | First task in Writing |
| Number of items | 10 |
| Points | 1 per item, machine scored |
| Section time | 23 min for all 12 Writing items |
| Likely share of time | about 6 min (see below) |
| Context | Social, interpersonal exchanges |

ETS gives one time limit for the whole Writing section: **23 minutes** for 12 items. The email task has 7 minutes and the academic discussion has 10 minutes, so simple arithmetic leaves about **6 minutes** for the ten Build a Sentence items. ETS does not publish a separate Build a Sentence timer in its overview, so treat six minutes as a planning figure, not an official rule, and always follow the timer on your screen.

The Writing section is **linear**: everyone who takes a particular test form gets the same tasks in the same order. Unlike Reading and Listening, it does not adapt to your performance, so an early mistake does not change the difficulty of later items.

<!-- rich_text: What ETS says the task measures -->
In the 2026 blueprint, Build a Sentence belongs to the Writing claim about reconstructing sentences with appropriate grammar. ETS describes the subskill as reconstructing *a range of sentence structures*, places the items in a social interpersonal context, and spans the CEFR range from A1 to C2. In plain words:

- **Easy items** use short replies with one clause and familiar words. They check that you know basic word order: subject, verb, object, time phrase.
- **Middle items** add question formation, auxiliaries, negatives, frequency adverbs and phrasal verbs.
- **Hard items** contain relative clauses, embedded questions, passives, reported speech and long noun phrases, where one misplaced chunk breaks the sentence.

ETS's own description of the Writing section says the task measures your understanding of English sentence structure, and that you arrange words or phrases to form a complete, grammatical *sentence or question*. Keep that last phrase in mind: some replies are questions, and the punctuation at the end of the frame is a strong clue.

The exchange format matters too. The reply must be an *appropriate* response to the first speaker. Two orders can both be grammatical, but only one may answer what was actually asked.

<!-- rich_text: How Build a Sentence is scored -->
The blueprint lists Build a Sentence as **machine scored**: a rule-based check of your arrangement against the expected answer, with a maximum of **1 point** per item. There is no human rater and no partial-credit rubric described by ETS. In practice this means:

- A sentence that is nearly right is scored as wrong. Ninety per cent correct earns nothing.
- There is no credit for creativity. You are reconstructing an intended sentence, not writing your own.
- Spelling is not your problem here, because the words are given. Order is everything.

Because each item is all-or-nothing, a careful final read of every sentence is worth more than speed. A two-second check that catches a swapped pair of words earns a full point.

Your raw Writing score (0 to 20) is converted to the 1 to 6 Writing band by ETS. ETS publishes the band scale and its comparison with the old 0 to 30 scale, but it does not publish a table that converts a specific number of correct Build a Sentence items into a band, so be suspicious of anyone who claims to know one.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: meaning first -->
Meaning first, grammar second
Before you move a single chunk, say to yourself what the reply probably means as an answer to the first speaker. Then build the grammar around that meaning. Learners who start by matching words mechanically often build a grammatical sentence that answers the wrong question.

<!-- rich_text: Reading the screen: context, frame and chunks -->
Every item gives you three pieces of information. Learn to read all three before you start dragging.

1. **The context sentence.** This is the first speaker's line. Is it a question or a statement? Is it a yes/no question, a *wh*- question, a complaint, a piece of news? The reply has to fit it.
2. **The frame.** In the ETS sample, the reply already shows a first word and a final word with blanks between them, and the final punctuation is visible. A capital letter tells you which word starts the sentence; a question mark at the end tells you the reply is a question.
3. **The chunks.** Some chunks are single words; others are phrases that stay together, such as *showed us around* or *as PDF files*. A multi-word chunk is a gift: it is already correctly ordered inside, so you only need to place it as a block.

Count the blanks and count the chunks. In the official ETS sample there is one more chunk than there are blanks, so one chunk is left over. ETS does not state that every item has a leftover chunk, so count every time: if the numbers are equal, everything must be used.

<!-- rich_text: A five-step method for every item -->
Use the same five steps on every item until they become automatic.

**Step 1: Predict the meaning.** From the context sentence, guess the reply's job: giving a reason, answering *where* or *when*, agreeing, asking back, making a suggestion.

**Step 2: Find the verb group.** Look for the main verb and any auxiliaries (*is being*, *had already*, *must be*, *did you*). The verb group is the skeleton of the sentence.

**Step 3: Find the subject.** Who or what does the verb? In a statement the subject comes before the verb. In a question the first auxiliary usually comes before the subject.

**Step 4: Attach everything else.** Objects follow the verb. Time and place phrases usually go at the end. Relative clauses go directly after the noun they describe. Adverbs of frequency go before the main verb but after *be*.

**Step 5: Read the whole sentence silently, once, as a reply.** Does it answer the first speaker? Is every chunk used (or exactly one left over, if the counts tell you so)? Does the final punctuation match a statement or a question?

Step 5 is the step people skip under time pressure, and it is the step that catches the most errors.

<!-- rich_text: Pattern 1: questions and do-support -->
When the reply is a question, English needs an auxiliary before the subject. If the sentence has no other auxiliary, *do*, *does* or *did* appears.

- *Where did you park?* not *Where you parked?*
- *Have you seen the new schedule?* The auxiliary *have* moves before *you*.
- *Which country are you focusing on?* The preposition can stay at the end in natural speech.

Two signals help you: a question mark at the end of the frame, and a chunk that contains an auxiliary plus a pronoun, such as *did you* or *are you*. When you see *did you*, the main verb must be in the base form (*think*, *go*, *finish*), never *thought* or *went*.

A common trap is a *wh*- word that is the subject itself. In *Who organized the party?* there is no *did*, because *who* is the subject. If the chunks contain *who* and a past-tense verb but no *did*, the question probably has this shape.

<!-- rich_text: Pattern 2: embedded and indirect questions -->
This is the single most frequent source of errors for learners whose first language forms questions differently. When a question is placed inside another sentence, it uses **statement word order** and no *do*-support.

- Direct: *Where does the shuttle stop?*
- Embedded: *I'm not sure where the shuttle stops.*
- Direct: *Can she come today?*
- Indirect: *Could you ask her if she can come today?*

Signals: introductory phrases such as *I wonder*, *I can't remember*, *Do you know*, *Nobody knows*, *Could you tell me*, followed by a *wh*- word, *if* or *whether*. After that word, put the subject first and the verb second. If the whole sentence is itself a question (*Do you know where it is?*), the question mark belongs to the outer sentence, but the inner part still keeps statement order.

<!-- rich_text: Pattern 3: relative clauses -->
A relative clause describes a noun and sits **directly after it**: *the students who live in Maple Hall*, *the one that our professor recommended*, *the room where we met*. Two checks make these items easier:

- **Subject or object?** In *the students who live in Maple Hall*, *who* is the subject of *live*. In *the one that our professor recommended*, *that* is the object and *our professor* is the subject inside the clause.
- **Which verb belongs to the main sentence?** Long relative clauses push the main verb far from its subject: *The tour leaders who met us at the station were friendly.* The main verb *were* agrees with *leaders*, not with *station*.

When an item has two verbs, decide which one belongs inside the relative clause and which one belongs to the main sentence before placing anything else.

<!-- rich_text: Pattern 4: passives and causatives -->
Passive replies appear when the speaker cares about what happens to something rather than who does it: *The filtration system is being repaired this week.* Build the verb group carefully, because passives have several pieces:

- *is being repaired* (present continuous passive)
- *must be uploaded* (modal passive)
- *has been moved* (present perfect passive)

The causative *have something done* is related: *I had it replaced last month* means someone else replaced it for me. The order is fixed: *have* + object + past participle. A tempting wrong order is *I had replaced it*, which changes the meaning to "I replaced it myself, earlier".

<!-- rich_text: Pattern 5: adverbs, negatives and small words -->
Small words cause big losses because the scorer does not forgive a misplaced *not*.

- **Frequency adverbs** (*always, usually, rarely, never*) go before the main verb (*I rarely go there*) but after *be* (*She is usually late*).
- **Already, still, just** sit between the auxiliary and the main verb: *She had already visited the site.*
- **Negatives with think, believe, expect**: natural English prefers *I don't think I'll make it* to *I think I won't make it*.
- **Enough** follows an adjective (*experienced enough*) but comes before a noun (*enough experience*).
- **Too** comes before an adjective (*too crowded*).
- **Sentence adverbs** such as *unfortunately* or *actually* usually open the sentence or close it after a comma; when a chunk with a capital letter appears (*Unfortunately*), it opens the sentence.

<!-- rich_text: Pattern 6: phrasal verbs, comparatives and tags -->
Three more patterns appear regularly in conversational replies.

**Phrasal verbs.** With a noun object, separable phrasal verbs allow two orders (*took back the projector* or *took the projector back*). With a pronoun object, only one order works: *took it back*, never *took back it*.

**Comparatives.** The shape is intensifier + comparative + *than* + second item: *much more spacious than the old one*, *a lot cheaper than the bus*. *Much* and *a lot* go before the comparative, not after it.

**Question tags.** A tag repeats the auxiliary of the main clause with the opposite polarity: *That's earlier than last year, isn't it?* / *You haven't registered yet, have you?* The tag chunk goes at the very end, after a comma, and the frame will end in a question mark.

<!-- rich_text: Worked examples: five items step by step -->
These items are original Schood practice items written in the style of the task. The chunks are separated by slashes.

**Example 1**
> Speaker 1: Is the career fair in the main hall this year?
> Chunks: whether / I can't remember / it moved / to the gym / or not

Meaning: the speaker doesn't know. Verb group: *can't remember*, and inside, *moved*. Pattern 2 (embedded question): after *whether*, use statement order, *it moved*.
**Answer:** *I can't remember whether it moved to the gym or not.*

**Example 2**
> Speaker 1: Which textbook should I buy for statistics?
> Chunks: the one / recommended / get / our professor / that

Meaning: a recommendation, so an imperative is likely. Verb: *get*. Object: *the one*. Relative clause after *the one*: *that our professor recommended*.
**Answer:** *Get the one that our professor recommended.*

**Example 3**
> Speaker 1: Why is the swimming pool closed?
> Chunks: is being / the filtration system / this week / repaired

Meaning: a reason. Verb group: *is being repaired* (Pattern 4). Subject: *the filtration system*. Time phrase at the end.
**Answer:** *The filtration system is being repaired this week.*

**Example 4**
> Speaker 1: Did you return the projector to the media office?
> Chunks: I / back / it / took / yesterday afternoon

Meaning: yes. Verb: *took back*, a phrasal verb. Pronoun object *it* must sit between *took* and *back* (Pattern 6).
**Answer:** *I took it back yesterday afternoon.*

**Example 5**
> Speaker 1: I'm writing my paper on renewable energy policy.
> Chunks: which / are you / focusing on / country

Meaning: a follow-up question. The chunk *are you* shows a question with auxiliary before subject. *Which* takes a noun directly: *which country*. The preposition stays inside its chunk at the end.
**Answer:** *Which country are you focusing on?*

<!-- rich_text: When one chunk is left over -->
In the official ETS sample item, the reply contains six blanks but seven chunks: two forms of the same verb are offered, and only one of them agrees with the subject. ETS does not say how many items contain a leftover chunk, so treat this as something that *can* happen, not something that always happens.

When you have an extra chunk, it is almost always one of these:

- **A competing verb form** (*was* / *were*, *live* / *lives*, *go* / *went*), testing agreement or tense.
- **A competing auxiliary** (*do* / *did*), testing whether *do*-support is needed at all, for example in an embedded question.
- **A competing function word** (*that* / *what*), testing relative clauses.

The method does not change. Build the sentence with the chunks that clearly belong, then decide the competing pair by asking which form agrees with the subject and fits the time frame of the conversation. Never force a leftover chunk into the sentence just because it is on the screen.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: two possible orders -->
Two orders that both look right
If two arrangements both seem grammatical, choose the one that answers the first speaker most directly and sounds like something a person would say in that conversation. If both still work, prefer the neutral order: subject, verb, object, then place, then time.

<!-- rich_text: Common traps -->
- **Copying the order of the context sentence.** The reply is a new sentence. If speaker 1 asked *Where does it stop?*, your reply might be *I'm not sure where it stops*, not *where does it stop*.
- **Leaving do-support inside an embedded question.** *Nobody knows how long will the work take* is wrong; *Nobody knows how long the work will take* is right.
- **Splitting a chunk mentally.** Multi-word chunks stay together. If you find yourself wanting to put a word in the middle of a chunk, your structure is wrong.
- **Agreement across a long clause.** The verb agrees with the head noun (*The students who live in Maple Hall organized it*), not with the nearest noun.
- **Adverb drift.** *I go rarely there* sounds plausible to many learners but is not natural English; frequency adverbs go before the main verb.
- **Pronoun position with phrasal verbs.** *I took back it* is never correct.
- **Ignoring final punctuation.** A question mark means a question or a tag at the end; a full stop means a statement or a request.
- **Spending three minutes on one item.** Each item is worth one point, the same as the easiest item.

<!-- rich_text: Timing: about six minutes for ten items -->
With roughly six minutes available, your average is about **35 seconds per item**. A practical rhythm:

| Phase | Time per item |
|---|---|
| Read context and frame | 5 seconds |
| Find verb and subject | 10 seconds |
| Place remaining chunks | 10 to 15 seconds |
| Final silent read | 5 seconds |

Easy items will take 20 seconds; hard ones may take a minute. That is fine as long as the total stays near six minutes, because the email (7 minutes) and the discussion post (10 minutes) need every second of their own time. Check the on-screen timer after item 5: if you have used more than about three and a half minutes, speed up on the remaining items and trust your first structure.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: always finish -->
Never leave an item unbuilt
Because scoring is all-or-nothing and there is no published penalty for a wrong arrangement, a complete best guess always beats an empty or half-built item. If an item is still unclear after about a minute, place the chunks in the most natural order you can see, read it once, and move on.

<!-- rich_text: Drill set A -->
Build each reply. Answers follow in the next section. Try to finish all seven in about four minutes.

1. Speaker 1: *Why are you leaving the lab so early today?*
   Chunks: my advisor / to finish / told me / the report / at home
2. Speaker 1: *Do you study in the library?*
   Chunks: I / go there / rarely / because / it gets / too crowded
3. Speaker 1: *Is the new gym better than the old one?*
   Chunks: is / much / than / the old one / it / more spacious
4. Speaker 1: *Are you coming to the study group tonight?*
   Chunks: I / think / don't / I'll / make it / Unfortunately,
5. Speaker 1: *The deadline for the scholarship application is Friday.*
   Chunks: isn't it / That's / earlier / than last year,
6. Speaker 1: *The printer on the second floor is broken again.*
   Chunks: Could you / ask / the technician / if / she can / come today
7. Speaker 1: *How long have you been a member of the debate club?*
   Chunks: I've / since / been / my first semester / a member

<!-- rich_text: Drill set A answers -->
1. **My advisor told me to finish the report at home.** Pattern: verb + object + *to*-infinitive (*tell someone to do something*). The time/place phrase *at home* goes last.
2. **I rarely go there because it gets too crowded.** Pattern 5: the frequency adverb comes before the main verb; *too* comes before the adjective.
3. **It is much more spacious than the old one.** Pattern 6: *much* + comparative + *than*.
4. **Unfortunately, I don't think I'll make it.** Pattern 5: the capitalised chunk with a comma opens the sentence, and English prefers the negative on *think*. (In free writing, *I don't think I'll make it, unfortunately* is also fine; here the capital letter and comma decide.)
5. **That's earlier than last year, isn't it?** Pattern 6: the tag repeats *is* in negative form and goes at the end.
6. **Could you ask the technician if she can come today?** Pattern 2: a polite request containing an indirect yes/no question. After *if*, statement order: *she can come*.
7. **I've been a member since my first semester.** Present perfect with *since* + a starting point, answering *How long*.

<!-- rich_text: Drill set B -->
Eight more items, a little harder. One item contains a leftover chunk; find it.

8. Speaker 1: *Can I borrow your notes from Monday's lecture?*
   Chunks: If / you / want, / I'll / send them / to you / tonight
9. Speaker 1: *Why didn't Maya join the field trip?*
   Chunks: She told me / that she / had already visited / the site
10. Speaker 1: *Your laptop screen looks brand new.*
    Chunks: I / had / replaced / it / last month
11. Speaker 1: *Are you going to apply for the research assistant position?*
    Chunks: I'm not sure / if I'm / experienced / enough / yet
12. Speaker 1: *Who organized the welcome party?*
    Chunks: The students / who / live / lives / in Maple Hall / organized it
13. Speaker 1: *When will the renovated cafeteria reopen?*
    Chunks: Nobody / how long / knows / the work / will take
14. Speaker 1: *Can I submit my essay as a photo?*
    Chunks: must be / All essays / as PDF files / uploaded
15. Speaker 1: *I just got back from the conference in Toronto.*
    Chunks: did you / of / think / What / the keynote speech

<!-- rich_text: Drill set B answers -->
8. **If you want, I'll send them to you tonight.** A conditional offer. The capital *If* and the comma after *want* fix the order; the object *them* follows *send*, then *to you*, then the time phrase.
9. **She told me that she had already visited the site.** Reported speech: the earlier action uses the past perfect, and *already* sits inside the verb group.
10. **I had it replaced last month.** Pattern 4: causative *have* + object + past participle. *I had replaced it* would mean you did it yourself.
11. **I'm not sure if I'm experienced enough yet.** Pattern 5: *enough* follows the adjective; *yet* closes the sentence.
12. **The students who live in Maple Hall organized it.** Leftover chunk: *lives*. The relative pronoun *who* refers to the plural *students*, so the verb is *live*. This is a practice item modelled on the leftover-chunk idea in the ETS sample.
13. **Nobody knows how long the work will take.** Pattern 2: embedded question with statement order after *how long*.
14. **All essays must be uploaded as PDF files.** Pattern 4: modal passive. The answer politely says no without using the word *no*, which is typical of these exchanges.
15. **What did you think of the keynote speech?** Pattern 1: *wh*- word + *did you* + base verb *think*; the fixed phrase *think of* keeps its preposition.

Score yourself: 13 to 15 correct is a strong base; 10 to 12 means one or two patterns need work; below 10, return to the pattern sections and rebuild the items you missed out loud.

<!-- rich_text: Make your own items -->
The fastest way to master this task is to create items yourself, because writing a scrambled reply forces you to see its structure.

1. Take any two-line exchange from a podcast transcript, a textbook dialogue or your own life.
2. Write the reply on paper and split it into 5 to 8 chunks. Keep natural phrases together (*as soon as possible*, *showed us around*).
3. Optionally add one competing form (*was/were*, *do/did*) as a leftover chunk.
4. Shuffle the chunks and come back to the item the next day.
5. Swap items with a study partner; their sentences will contain structures you would not have chosen.

Aim for replies that use the six patterns in this module. Ten self-made items a week is enough to keep the patterns active.

<!-- rich_text: Priorities by level -->
**Moving from B1 to B2 (Writing bands around 3 to 4).** Secure basic word order and question formation. Most lost points at this level come from *do*-support, frequency adverbs and pronoun objects with phrasal verbs. Do drill set A daily until every item takes under 30 seconds.

**Moving from B2 to C1 (bands around 4 to 5).** Your remaining errors are probably embedded questions, relative clauses with long subjects and passives. Practise drill set B and make your own items using *who*, *that*, *whether* and *has been*.

**Moving from C1 to C2 (bands around 5 to 6).** At this level the risk is not grammar knowledge but carelessness and time. Use Build a Sentence as a warm-up for the whole section: aim for ten correct items in five minutes so that you bank time for the discussion post.

These level descriptions are guidance based on the CEFR descriptors ETS publishes; ETS does not publish how many Build a Sentence items correspond to each band.

<!-- rich_text: How to review your mistakes -->
After every practice set, do not just read the correct answer. Classify each mistake:

- **Q** for question formation or *do*-support
- **E** for embedded questions
- **R** for relative clauses and agreement
- **P** for passives and causatives
- **A** for adverbs, negatives and small words
- **V** for phrasal verbs, comparatives and tags
- **T** for time pressure (you knew the rule but rushed)

After three sets, count your letters. The most frequent letter is your study plan for the next week. If **T** is your most frequent letter, the fix is not more grammar but a stricter routine: steps 2, 3 and 5 of the method on every single item.

Finally, say the correct sentence aloud twice. Hearing the right order builds the instinct you need when time is short, and it doubles as practice for the [Listen and Repeat](/speaking-listen-and-repeat) task.

<!-- rich_text: Frequently asked questions -->
**Is there partial credit if most of my sentence is right?**
ETS describes the task as machine scored with a maximum of 1 point per item and does not describe any partial credit. Plan as if each item is right or wrong.

**Do I need to type anything?**
ETS describes the task as moving given words and phrases into place. You arrange chunks; you do not write new words.

**Will every item have an extra chunk?**
The official sample has one leftover chunk. ETS does not say whether all items work that way, so count blanks and chunks on every item.

**Can I go back to an earlier item?**
Follow the on-screen navigation and directions on test day. Plan your work so that you do not need to come back: finish and check each item before moving on.

**Does a mistake here make the email harder?**
No. The Writing section is linear, so every test taker on a form gets the same email and discussion tasks regardless of Build a Sentence performance.

**Is this task related to the old TOEFL?**
Build a Sentence was introduced with the January 2026 update. The academic discussion task, by contrast, was kept from the previous version of the test.
