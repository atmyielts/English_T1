<!-- rich_text: Why Complete the Words matters -->
Complete the Words is the first thing you meet in the updated TOEFL iBT, and it is unlike any reading task in the old test. Instead of answering questions about a long passage, you repair a short academic paragraph by typing the missing halves of words. It looks like a spelling game. It is not. To finish a word correctly you have to understand the sentence around it: its grammar, its topic and the logic that links one clause to the next.

Three facts make this task worth serious preparation:

- **It carries a lot of weight.** The ETS 2026 blueprint lists 30 target Complete the Words items inside a Reading section of 50 items. No other Reading task type has a fixed target that large.
- **It sits in the router stage.** Reading is two-stage adaptive. How well you do in the first stage helps decide whether your second stage is the lower or the upper module. Careless errors here can cost you more than the points themselves.
- **It rewards skills you can train quickly.** Most blanks are solved by grammar knowledge, collocations and spelling accuracy. Those improve with focused daily practice far faster than broad reading speed does.

This module explains exactly how the task is built, gives you a repeatable method, works through four original paragraphs line by line and ends with drills and answer keys. If you are new to the 2026 format, read [TOEFL Essentials](/toefl-essentials) first for the big picture.

<!-- checklist: What you will be able to do -->
- Explain how a Complete the Words paragraph is built and which words lose letters.
- Use the number of missing letters to work out the exact length of each hidden word.
- Solve function-word blanks (articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliaries) in seconds.
- Choose the correct word form for content-word blanks using grammar and suffix clues.
- Avoid the most common traps: wrong tense, wrong number, near-miss spellings and empty blanks.
- Finish a paragraph with time left to reread it as a whole.
- Run a short daily routine that steadily raises your accuracy.

<!-- rich_text: The task at a glance -->
Here is what ETS publishes about the task in the updated TOEFL iBT Test Overview and the 2026 blueprint.

| Feature | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Text | One academic paragraph, about 70–100 words |
| First sentence | Left complete, to give you the topic |
| Deletions | The second half of every second word, after the first sentence |
| Blanks per paragraph | 10 words with missing letters |
| Your action | Type the missing letters |
| Scoring | Machine scored; 1 point per scored item |
| Section | Reading (first section of the test) |

A few more details matter for strategy:

- **The whole paragraph stays on screen.** The blueprint says reading texts remain visible throughout the task, so you can move back and forth as often as you need.
- **The paragraphs are academic.** Topics come from the same broad areas as the rest of the Reading section, such as history, the arts, business, life science, physical science and social science. No specialist knowledge is needed; everything you need is in the text.
- **The difficulty range is wide.** The blueprint places Complete the Words at CEFR B1 to C1+. Some paragraphs use everyday academic language; others use denser vocabulary and longer sentences.
- **Blueprint target: 30 items.** Because every paragraph carries 10 blanks, that equals about three paragraphs across the router and the second module. ETS publishes the target item count, not a guaranteed number of paragraphs per stage, so treat "about three" as an estimate.

<!-- rich_text: What ETS says the task measures -->
ETS describes the task in two ways, and both are useful.

On its test-taker pages, ETS says Complete the Words measures your vocabulary knowledge and your ability to understand meaning from context. In the blueprint, the underlying skill is described more technically: you use word-order rules, lexical knowledge and reading comprehension to construct meaningful text. The task belongs to the Reading claim about processing academic written texts "for meaning and form".

In practice, this means three abilities work together:

1. **Form.** You know how English words are built: plural endings, verb endings, suffixes that turn verbs into nouns or adjectives into adverbs, and the spelling rules behind them.
2. **Grammar.** You know which kind of word can appear in a given slot. After "the" you need a noun or an adjective. After "can" you need a base verb. Between two clauses you may need a conjunction.
3. **Meaning.** You follow the argument of the paragraph well enough to pick the word that makes sense, not just a word that fits the letters.

The blueprint's CEFR evidence statements show how this changes by level. At the basic level, test takers apply spelling and word-form knowledge to complete predictable gaps. At the independent level, they use morphological and syntactic cues to infer missing words. At the proficient level, they integrate vocabulary, word structure and discourse-level knowledge across longer, more complex texts. Your goal is to move up that ladder: from recognising easy words to reading the whole paragraph as an argument.

<!-- rich_text: How the task is scored -->
Complete the Words is **machine scored**. The blueprint explains that machine-scored task types use rule-based algorithms that evaluate responses by exact matches or predefined scoring logic. Each scored item is worth a maximum of 1 point.

What this means for you:

- **Spelling counts.** Plan on the assumption that a word must be spelled correctly to earn the point. "Recieve", "goverment" and "begining" are exactly the kind of near misses that cost points.
- **There is no partial credit to rely on.** Do not count on being rewarded for "almost right". Treat each blank as right or wrong.
- **Not every item is scored.** The blueprint notes that Reading and Listening include non-scored items used to collect data for future tests. You cannot tell which ones they are, so give every blank full attention.
- **A blank left empty is certainly wrong.** A reasonable guess at least has a chance.

ETS does not publish whether alternative spellings, such as British and American variants, are accepted. In most items the letters already shown decide the spelling for you. When they do not, write the most standard form you know and move on.

<!-- rich_text: The deletion rule, decoded -->
The deletion pattern is mechanical, and once you understand it, it gives you free information.

**Which words are cut?** The first sentence stays whole. Starting with the second sentence, every second word loses its second half, until ten words have been cut. After the tenth blank, the rest of the paragraph is complete again.

**How much of each word is shown?** In the ETS sample, a word keeps the first half of its letters, rounded down, and loses the rest. So a word with an odd number of letters loses one more letter than it shows.

| Letters in word | Shown | Missing | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1 | 1 | is → i_ |
| 3 | 1 | 2 | the → t_ _ |
| 4 | 2 | 2 | that → th_ _ |
| 5 | 2 | 3 | could → co_ _ _ |
| 6 | 3 | 3 | before → bef_ _ _ |
| 7 | 3 | 4 | between → bet_ _ _ _ |
| 8 | 4 | 4 | although → alth_ _ _ _ |
| 9 | 4 | 5 | important → impo_ _ _ _ _ |

**The free information.** If the screen shows one slot per missing letter, as the ETS sample does, you know the word's exact length: shown letters plus missing letters. Even without counting slots, the rule tells you that the missing part is either the same length as the shown part or one letter longer. A fragment "th_ _" can only be a four-letter word starting with "th": that, them, then, they, this, thus. Grammar then picks one in a second.

This is why strong test takers rarely "think of a word and hope". They narrow the choice by length first, then by grammar, then by meaning.

<!-- rich_text: A five-step method for every paragraph -->
Use the same five steps on every paragraph until they are automatic.

1. **Read the first sentence carefully.** It is complete, and it names the topic. Notice the key nouns. They often return later, sometimes in a blank.
2. **Skim the whole paragraph once.** Do not fill anything yet. Get the shape of the argument: is it describing a process, comparing two things, explaining a cause, or giving a history?
3. **Fill the function words first.** Articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliary verbs and conjunctions are short and highly predictable. Clearing them first reveals the structure of each sentence.
4. **Solve the content words with grammar plus meaning.** Decide the word class the slot needs (noun, verb, adjective, adverb), then the form (plural, past tense, -ing, comparative), then the meaning that fits the argument.
5. **Reread the entire paragraph as normal prose.** Read it as if you were proofreading someone else's work. Wrong tenses, missing plural endings and words that do not quite fit jump out on a full reread.

The reread in step 5 is the step most learners skip under time pressure, and it is the step that catches the most errors.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: short words first -->
Fill the short words first
If a blank belongs to a word of four letters or fewer, solve it before anything else. These are almost always function words, they take seconds, and every one you fill makes the longer blanks easier to see.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example 1: the waggle dance -->
Here is an original practice paragraph built exactly by the published rule.

> Honeybees communicate the location of food through a movement known as the waggle dance. When o_ _ forager ret_ _ _ _ to t_ _ hive, s_ _ walks i_ a repe_ _ _ _ loop wh_ _ _ shaking h_ _ body. T_ _ angle o_ the straight run shows the direction of the flowers relative to the sun, and its length signals the distance. Other bees follow the dancer closely and then fly out to find the source themselves. Researchers have decoded these signals well enough to predict where a colony will search for food.

**Step 1: topic.** Bees, food, and a "dance" that communicates location.

**Step 3: short words first.**

- "t_ _ hive" needs a determiner before a noun: **the**.
- "s_ _ walks" needs a subject pronoun. The forager is a bee already treated as female in many texts, and the later "h_ _ body" confirms it: **she**.
- "i_ a" before a noun phrase needs a preposition: **in**.
- "h_ _ body" needs a possessive: **her**.
- "T_ _ angle" starts a sentence with a determiner: **The**.
- "o_ the straight run": "the angle ___ the straight run" needs **of**.
- "o_ _ forager" is a three-letter word before a singular noun: **one**. ("Any" and "the" are three letters too, but "the" would leave "t" as the first letter, and "any" does not start with "o".)

**Step 4: content words.**

- "ret_ _ _ _ to" is a seven-letter verb after a singular subject in a present-tense "when" clause: **returns**. "Returned" would be eight letters and the wrong tense for a general description.
- "repe_ _ _ _ loop" is an adjective before a noun, eight letters: **repeated**.
- "wh_ _ _ shaking" is a five-letter word introducing an -ing phrase that happens at the same time: **while**.

**Answer key:** 1 one, 2 returns, 3 the, 4 she, 5 in, 6 repeated, 7 while, 8 her, 9 The, 10 of.

Notice that seven of the ten blanks were function words. Because every second word is cut and English uses short function words constantly, they often make up a large share of the blanks, which is why step 3 is so powerful.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example 2: railway time -->
> Before standard time zones were introduced, most towns set their clocks by the position of the sun. This me_ _ _ that no_ _ in o_ _ city co_ _ _ differ b_ several min_ _ _ _ from no_ _ in t_ _ next to_ _. For far_ _ _ _ the difference hardly mattered, but the expansion of railways made it a serious problem. Train timetables became confusing because each station kept its own local time. In response, railway companies agreed on shared zones, and governments later made them official.

**Topic:** before time zones, towns used the sun to set clocks.

**Walk-through:**

- "This me_ _ _ that" is a five-letter verb meaning "had the result": **meant**. Past tense matches the historical frame of the first sentence. "Means" is also five letters, but the paragraph is about the past, and the reread confirms "meant".
- "no_ _ in o_ _ city": the first sentence is about the sun's position, so the time of day is **noon**; "one city" contrasts with "the next town": **one**.
- "co_ _ _ differ" needs a modal before a base verb, five letters: **could**.
- "b_ several minutes": "differ ___ several minutes" takes **by**.
- "min_ _ _ _" is plural after "several", seven letters: **minutes**.
- "from no_ _ in t_ _ next to_ _": the parallel structure repeats "noon": **noon**, **the**, **town**.
- "For far_ _ _ _" is a seven-letter plural noun for people who would not care about minutes: **farmers**.

**Answer key:** 1 meant, 2 noon, 3 one, 4 could, 5 by, 6 minutes, 7 noon, 8 the, 9 town, 10 farmers.

**Lesson:** parallel structure ("noon in one city ... noon in the next town") is a gift. When a sentence compares two things, the second half often mirrors the first.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example 3: Renaissance pigments -->
> Many painters of the Renaissance prepared their own colours by grinding minerals into fine powder. The pow_ _ _ was th_ _ mixed wi_ _ egg yo_ _ or o_ _ to cre_ _ _ a pa_ _ _ that co_ _ _ be spr_ _ _ smoothly. So_ _ pigments were extremely expensive, especially a deep blue made from a stone imported from distant mountains. Because of its cost, artists often reserved this blue for the most important figures in a painting. Lighter blues came from cheaper plant dyes and were used for skies and backgrounds.

**Walk-through:**

- "The pow_ _ _" repeats the last word of the first sentence: **powder**. Repetition of key nouns from the first sentence is one of the most reliable patterns in the task.
- "was th_ _ mixed" needs a time adverb showing sequence: **then**.
- "mixed wi_ _" takes the preposition **with**.
- "egg yo_ _" is a fixed collocation: **yolk**. Note the silent "l"; this is a classic spelling trap.
- "or o_ _" is a three-letter substance that can be mixed with pigment: **oil**.
- "to cre_ _ _" is a six-letter base verb after "to": **create**.
- "a pa_ _ _" is a five-letter noun; the topic is painting: **paint**.
- "that co_ _ _ be" needs a modal: **could**.
- "be spr_ _ _ smoothly" needs a past participle (passive after "be"). The six-letter word is **spread**, which has the same form in the present, past and past participle.
- "So_ _ pigments" is a four-letter determiner before a plural noun: **Some**.

**Answer key:** 1 powder, 2 then, 3 with, 4 yolk, 5 oil, 6 create, 7 paint, 8 could, 9 spread, 10 Some.

**Lesson:** after "be", "been" or "being", expect a past participle or an -ing form. Irregular verbs such as spread, cut, set and put look the same in several forms, which makes them easy to miss.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example 4: working memory -->
> Psychologists use the term working memory for the small amount of information we can hold in mind at one moment. Most adu_ _ _ can ke_ _ only fo_ _ or fi_ _ items act_ _ _ before so_ _ of th_ _ begin t_ fade. Th_ _ limit expl_ _ _ _ why a long phone number is easier to remember when it is divided into groups. Teachers can apply the same idea by presenting new material in short steps and giving learners time to practise each one.

**Walk-through:**

- "Most adu_ _ _" is a six-letter plural noun for people: **adults**.
- "can ke_ _" needs a base verb that means "hold", four letters: **keep**. The first sentence used "hold", and the paragraph now paraphrases it. Paraphrase of the first sentence is common.
- "only fo_ _ or fi_ _ items": two numbers joined by "or", four letters each: **four**, **five**.
- "items act_ _ _" is a six-letter adjective describing items held in mind: **active**.
- "before so_ _ of th_ _": "some of them" is a fixed pattern: **some**, **them**.
- "begin t_ fade": "begin to" plus a base verb: **to**.
- "Th_ _ limit" refers back to the limit just described, four letters: **This**.
- "expl_ _ _ _ why" is an eight-letter verb with a singular subject in the present: **explains**.

**Answer key:** 1 adults, 2 keep, 3 four, 4 five, 5 active, 6 some, 7 them, 8 to, 9 This, 10 explains.

**Lesson:** subject-verb agreement decides many endings. "This limit" is singular, so the verb needs "-s". Check every verb blank against its subject during your reread.

<!-- rich_text: The grammar clues that solve most blanks -->
Most blanks can be solved by asking one question: *what kind of word can stand here?* These are the signals to look for.

#### Before a noun
- Determiners: the, a, an, this, that, these, those, some, many, each, every, their, its, our.
- Adjectives often end in -al, -ous, -ive, -ful, -ic, -able, -ent, -ant.

#### After a determiner or adjective
- A noun. Check whether it should be singular or plural from the determiner ("several", "many", "these" demand plurals; "each", "every", "one" demand singulars).

#### After a modal (can, could, may, might, must, should, will, would)
- A base verb with no ending: can **keep**, could **differ**, might **explain**.

#### After "to"
- Usually a base verb (to **create**), but sometimes a noun when "to" is a preposition (similar to, due to, in addition to, contribute to).

#### After be, been, being
- A past participle for the passive (was **published**) or an -ing form for the progressive (was **growing**).

#### After have, has, had
- A past participle: has **increased**, had **become**.

#### Between two clauses
- A conjunction or linking word: because, although, while, when, but, so, which, that, whereas, since.

#### After a preposition
- A noun, pronoun or -ing form: by **presenting**, without **using**, of **them**.

Train yourself to name the slot before you think of words. "This slot is a plural noun" eliminates thousands of possibilities in one step.

<!-- rich_text: Word families and suffixes -->
When the grammar tells you the word class, suffixes tell you the spelling. Learn words in families, not alone.

| Base | Noun | Adjective | Adverb |
|---|---|---|---|
| explain | explanation | explanatory | — |
| depend | dependence | dependent | dependently |
| vary | variation, variety | various, variable | variously |
| signify | significance | significant | significantly |
| produce | production, product | productive | productively |

Useful patterns to drill:

- **-tion / -sion nouns:** information, explanation, decision, expansion. Notice how "explain" loses the "i" in "explanation".
- **-ment nouns:** movement, development, government (with the often-forgotten "n").
- **-ly adverbs:** extremely, closely, especially, automatically (not "automaticly").
- **-ed and -ing with doubling:** begin → beginning, occur → occurred, refer → referred, but open → opened, visit → visited.
- **Silent letters:** yolk, island, design, foreign, doubt, column.
- **Easily confused endings:** dependent/dependant, independent, consistent, relevant, significant, permanent, existence, occurrence.

Each time you get a Complete the Words item wrong because of spelling, add the whole word family to your notebook, not just the one word.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: length decides -->
Let length decide between candidates
When two words fit the meaning, count the letters. Shown plus missing must equal the word's length, and the missing part is either equal to the shown part or one letter longer. Length eliminates the wrong candidate faster than meaning does.

<!-- rich_text: Common traps and how to avoid them -->
**1. The right word in the wrong form.** "The expansion of railways creat_ _ problems" needs "created" or "creates" depending on time frame, and only one will match the slot count. Always check tense against the paragraph's time frame and number against the subject.

**2. The capital letter at the start of a sentence.** When a blank begins a sentence, the shown part is capitalised ("T_ _", "Th_ _", "So_ _"). It is still an ordinary function word in most cases: The, This, These, Some, Such, Many, Most.

**3. Ignoring the first sentence.** The complete first sentence is not decoration. It introduces the nouns that later blanks repeat or paraphrase (powder, hold → keep, noon).

**4. Near-miss spelling.** Words like "receive", "government", "environment", "necessary", "occurred", "beginning" and "separate" are where machine scoring is merciless.

**5. A word that fits the letters but not the meaning.** "co_ _ _" could be could, count, coast or color, but only one fits "___ differ". Read the whole sentence before typing.

**6. Leaving a blank empty.** A blank with no answer is certainly wrong. If you are stuck, type the most likely common word of the right length and move on.

**7. Forgetting the reread.** Under pressure, learners fill ten blanks and immediately continue. A ten-second reread catches agreement and tense errors that a blank-by-blank approach misses.

**8. Spending too long on one blank.** One hard word is worth one point, the same as an easy one. If a blank resists for more than about 20 seconds in practice, guess and come back during the reread.

<!-- rich_text: Timing plan -->
ETS publishes timings for the whole Reading section, not for each task type. The approximate base time for Reading is 30 minutes; the 2026 blueprint gives about 18–21 minutes for the router stage and about 9 minutes for the second module. ETS also notes that as the test adapts, test time and items may vary.

Because Complete the Words shares that time with Read in Daily Life and Read an Academic Passage, a practical training target is:

- **About two minutes per paragraph** in practice, including the reread. This is our training benchmark, not an ETS rule.
- **Under 90 seconds** once you are comfortable, which frees time for the academic passage questions that follow.

Build speed in this order: accuracy first, then speed. Timing yourself before your accuracy is reliable just trains you to make errors faster.

A simple progression:

1. Weeks 1–2: untimed, aiming for 9 or 10 correct out of 10.
2. Weeks 3–4: three minutes per paragraph, still aiming for 9 or 10.
3. Week 5 onward: two minutes per paragraph, then work toward 90 seconds.

Use the [TOEFL simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) for timed sets so that you feel the real on-screen pressure.

<!-- rich_text: How Complete the Words fits the adaptive Reading section -->
The Reading section is a two-stage adaptive test. The first stage, called the router, is the same kind of mix for everyone. Your performance in it is used to select the second stage: a lower module or an upper module. ETS explains that this matches the difficulty of the second part to your ability, so the test can measure you accurately in less time.

Three practical conclusions follow:

- **Router accuracy matters doubly.** Points earned in the router count, and they also influence which module you see next. You cannot control the routing directly, so the only sensible strategy is to be as accurate as possible from the very first paragraph.
- **A harder second module is normal for strong readers.** If the paragraphs in the second part feel more difficult, that may simply reflect the adaptive design. Do not let it shake your confidence.
- **Do not save effort for later.** Some learners treat the first paragraph as a warm-up. In an adaptive test, there is no warm-up.

For the full picture of how the router, the modules and the scoring fit together, see [TOEFL Essentials](/toefl-essentials) and [The 2026 score scale](/score-scale-2026).

<!-- callout: Decision rule: no empty blanks -->
Never submit an empty blank
If you cannot solve a word, type the most common word of the correct length that fits the grammar. A guess may be right; an empty blank cannot be.

<!-- rich_text: Drill 1: glaciers -->
Complete the paragraph. Count the slots, name the word class, then fill. Answers are in the Drill answers section below.

> Glaciers move even though they appear solid and still. Under th_ _ _ own enor_ _ _ _ weight, t_ _ lower lay_ _ _ of i_ _ slowly def_ _ _ and fl_ _ downhill li_ _ an extr_ _ _ _ _ thick liq_ _ _. Meltwater at the base can also act as a lubricant, allowing the glacier to slide across the rock beneath it. As they travel, glaciers carry stones that scrape the ground and leave long scratches. Geologists study these marks to reconstruct the paths of ancient ice sheets.

**Hints if you need them:** one possessive, one adjective of size, one article, one plural noun, one substance, two verbs, one preposition of comparison, one intensifying adverb, one noun of state of matter.

<!-- rich_text: Drill answers -->
**Drill 1 (glaciers):** 1 their, 2 enormous, 3 the, 4 layers, 5 ice, 6 deform, 7 flow, 8 like, 9 extremely, 10 liquid.

Why: "their own" is a fixed pattern; "enormous weight" is a strong collocation; "layers of ice" is plural after "the lower"; "deform and flow" are two base-form verbs sharing the plural subject "layers"; "like an extremely thick liquid" is a comparison with an adverb modifying an adjective. Note that "extremely" keeps the "e" before "-ly".

**Drill 2 (saving):** 1 explanation, 2 that, 3 tend, 4 value, 5 rewards, 6 more, 7 future, 8 Buying, 9 today, 10 real.

Why: "One explanation is that ..." is a standard academic frame; "tend to value" uses "tend" with a plural subject; "rewards" is plural because of "future ones" later; "far more than" is a fixed comparative; "Buying something today feels real" uses an -ing subject, and "today" contrasts with "distant". Watch the spelling: explain → explanation drops the "i".

**Drill 3:** 1 published, 2 Although, 3 between, 4 which, 5 their, 6 increased, 7 because, 8 important, 9 were, 10 through.

Trap check on item 6: "incr_ _ _ _" with five missing letters could be "increases" or "increased". After "has", only the past participle "increased" is grammatical.

<!-- rich_text: Priorities by level -->
#### Moving from band 3 to band 4 (B1 to B2)
- Get every function-word blank right, every time. These are the easiest points in the section.
- Master agreement: singular subject plus "-s" in the present; plural after several, many, these.
- Learn the 300 or so most common academic verbs and their past forms.

#### Moving from band 4 to band 5 (B2 to C1)
- Use word families: when the slot needs a noun, produce the noun form confidently (variation, significance, dependence).
- Read for argument: notice contrast words (however, although, whereas) and cause-effect words (because, therefore, as a result) and let them guide content-word choices.
- Eliminate spelling near-misses with a personal list of words you get wrong.

#### Moving from band 5 to band 6 (C1 to C2)
- Handle dense paragraphs with abstract nouns and long noun phrases without slowing down.
- Recognise less frequent collocations (exert pressure, pose a challenge, draw a conclusion).
- Make the reread step automatic and fast, so that you catch the one subtle error per set that separates a 5.5 from a 6.

These bands refer to the 1–6 TOEFL scale and its CEFR alignment, explained in [The 2026 score scale](/score-scale-2026). Your Reading band reflects the whole section, not this task alone.

<!-- rich_text: Building the vocabulary this task rewards -->
Complete the Words rewards vocabulary you can *spell and use*, not vocabulary you merely recognise. Build it this way:

- **Read short academic texts daily.** Encyclopedia-style articles, university news pages and popular-science magazines are ideal. Ten minutes a day beats an hour once a week.
- **Collect collocations, not single words.** Write "exert influence", "a significant increase", "rely heavily on", "a growing body of evidence". The partner word is often what lets you solve a blank.
- **Study word families.** For every new verb, write its noun, adjective and adverb forms where they exist.
- **Write words from memory.** Recognition is not enough. Cover the word and type it. Spelling under time pressure comes from production practice.
- **Make your own C-tests.** Take any paragraph, keep the first sentence, then cut every second word yourself following the rule. Swap with a study partner, or come back to it two days later.

<!-- rich_text: Frequently asked questions -->
**Is Complete the Words a vocabulary test or a reading test?**
Both. ETS describes it as measuring vocabulary and understanding meaning from context. You cannot solve the harder blanks without understanding the paragraph.

**Can I go back and change an answer within the paragraph?**
The paragraph stays on screen while you work, so you can revise within the task. Use the reread step to do exactly that.

**Do I lose points for wrong answers compared with empty ones?**
ETS awards a maximum of 1 point per scored item. Nothing in the published materials suggests a penalty for wrong answers, so a guess is always better than a blank.

**Does capitalisation matter?**
The first letters are already shown, including any capital, so you only type the lowercase remainder. Focus on spelling the rest of the word correctly.

**How many paragraphs will I see?**
The blueprint targets 30 Complete the Words items, which equals about three paragraphs of ten blanks across the section. ETS does not publish an exact paragraph count for each stage.

**Should I learn British or American spelling?**
Learn the spelling you use consistently, but notice that the shown letters usually remove the choice. ETS does not publish a policy on spelling variants for this task.
