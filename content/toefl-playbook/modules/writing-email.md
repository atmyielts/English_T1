<!-- rich_text: Why this task matters -->
Write an Email is the second task in the Writing section of the TOEFL iBT as updated on 21 January 2026. It comes straight after the ten Build a Sentence items and before the academic discussion post. You read a short scenario, usually from university or social life, and write an email that does three specific things listed in the prompt. You have **7 minutes**.

The task is worth up to **5 raw points**, a quarter of the 20 raw points in the Writing section. It is also the most practical thing you will write on the test: a real message to a real kind of reader, such as a professor, a housing office, a club organiser or a friend. Universities care about exactly this skill, because international students write emails like these from their first week on campus.

The good news is that the email task is highly learnable. The prompt tells you what to write about, the structure is predictable, and a small set of polite formulas covers most situations. What separates a 3-level email from a 5-level email is not vocabulary showing off; it is how completely and clearly you do the three things you were asked, in a tone that suits the reader, with few errors.

<!-- checklist: What you will be able to do -->
- State the format, timing and scoring of the email task accurately.
- Read a prompt for audience, purpose and the three required points in under a minute.
- Use a five-part email structure that works for any scenario.
- Choose a formal or friendly register and keep it consistent.
- Elaborate each required point with a reason, detail or example.
- Recognise what separates 3-, 4- and 5-level emails on the ETS scoring guide.
- Follow a 7-minute plan that leaves time to proofread.

<!-- rich_text: The task at a glance -->
| Feature | Write an Email |
|---|---|
| Position | Task 2 of 3 in Writing |
| Time | 7 minutes |
| Input | A short written scenario |
| Required content | Usually 3 bullet points |
| Score | 0 to 5, AI scored |
| Length | No minimum stated by ETS |

The prompt gives you a situation, names the person you are writing to, and lists the things your email must do, for example *explain a problem*, *describe how it affects you* and *suggest a solution*. The *To* and *Subject* lines may already be filled in. ETS's instruction in its sample is to write as much as you can, in complete sentences.

The Writing section is linear: all test takers on the same form get the same email prompt, regardless of how they did on Build a Sentence.

<!-- rich_text: What ETS says the task measures -->
In the 2026 blueprint, the email belongs to the Writing claim about writing effective responses to common situations in academic contexts. Its subskill is writing appropriate multi-sentence text, in an **academic navigational** context: the language you use to get things done around a university, such as asking for an extension, reporting a problem or arranging a meeting. The CEFR range is B1 to C2.

ETS's overview lists four things the email should show. In our own words:

1. **It achieves the goal** in the prompt, following the normal social conventions of an email.
2. **It is elaborated enough**, clear and connected, not a list of bare statements.
3. **It uses grammar and vocabulary accurately and appropriately**, with some range.
4. **It follows mechanical conventions**: spelling, punctuation and capitalisation.

Notice that the first criterion is about communication, not language. A beautifully written email that forgets one of the three bullet points does not achieve the goal.

<!-- rich_text: How the email is scored -->
The blueprint lists Write an Email as **AI scored**: ETS evaluates constructed responses with natural language processing and machine learning that look at features such as fluency, coherence and grammar. The maximum is **5 points**. ETS publishes a scoring guide with six levels. Here is a summary in our words, not ETS's:

- **5, fully successful.** The email clearly does its job. Every point is developed in a way that serves the purpose. Sentences vary naturally and word choice is precise and idiomatic. The tone, politeness and organisation fit the reader throughout. Errors are rare and of the kind any competent writer makes when typing fast.
- **4, generally successful.** The email works and is easy to understand. Elaboration is adequate, there is some variety, the tone is mostly right, and there are a few errors.
- **3, partially successful.** The email mostly does the task, but some of the message is not fully clear or developed. Range is moderate and errors are noticeable, in structure, word forms, idioms or tone.
- **2, mostly unsuccessful.** There is an attempt, but the message is limited or hard to follow, with little or irrelevant elaboration and many errors.
- **1, unsuccessful.** Very little development, short disconnected phrases, frequent serious errors, or language mostly copied from the prompt.
- **0.** Blank, off topic, not in English, copied entirely from the prompt, or random keystrokes.

Two details from ETS's own sample commentary are useful. First, a 4-level sample was held back from 5 mainly by a verb-form error that made part of a sentence unclear. Second, the guide explicitly tolerates the kind of slips competent writers make under time pressure. The lesson: one consequential grammar error in a key sentence can matter more than three small typos.

ETS converts your raw Writing points to a 1 to 6 Writing band, but it does not publish a conversion from a single email score to a band. Use the levels above as targets, not as band predictions.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: all three points -->
Cover every bullet, in order
Before you write anything, number the three required points. Give each one its own paragraph or at least its own two or three sentences, in the order the prompt lists them. A missing point caps your email no matter how good the language is.

<!-- rich_text: Reading the prompt in 45 seconds -->
Read the prompt once, slowly, and answer three questions.

**Who is the reader?** A professor, a department office, a landlord, a club leader, a classmate, a friend? This decides your greeting, your tone and how direct you can be.

**What is the purpose?** Most prompts fall into a few families: reporting a problem and asking for help, requesting permission or information, making a recommendation, inviting someone, apologising and proposing a fix, or giving feedback. Name the family; it tells you which formulas to use.

**What are the three things?** Underline the verbs in the bullets: *explain*, *describe*, *ask*, *suggest*, *invite*, *recommend*, *tell*. Each verb is a job. *Describe* asks for details; *suggest* asks for a concrete proposal; *ask* asks for an actual question with a question mark.

Then make a 10-second plan with one detail for each point. You do not need to write the plan down if you are fast, but many test takers find a few words on their notes helpful: *quiz closed 10:40 / counts for grade / reopen or new version*.

<!-- rich_text: A reliable five-part structure -->
This structure fits nearly every prompt and lets you spend your time on content, not layout.

1. **Greeting.** *Dear Dr. Ramos,* / *Hello Ms. Chen,* / *Hi Jamie,*
2. **Opening line with purpose.** One or two sentences that say why you are writing. *I am writing about last night's quiz, which closed before the deadline.*
3. **Body: the three points.** One short paragraph per bullet, each with a main sentence and one or two supporting details (a time, a reason, a consequence, an example).
4. **Closing line.** Thanks, an offer of further information, or a look forward. *Thank you for considering my request.*
5. **Sign-off and name.** *Best regards,* / *Kind regards,* / *See you soon,* followed by a name.

Paragraph breaks help the reader and cost nothing. A 4- or 5-level email usually runs to somewhere around 120 to 180 words in 7 minutes, but ETS sets no word target; a complete, clear 110-word email beats a rambling 220-word one.

<!-- rich_text: Register: matching the reader -->
Social conventions are part of the scoring guide, so tone is not decoration. Decide early which of three registers you need, and do not drift.

| Reader | Register | Example request |
|---|---|---|
| Professor, office, official | Formal and polite | Would it be possible to... |
| Club leader, classmate you know slightly | Neutral and friendly | Could you let me know... |
| Close friend | Informal | Can you grab... for me? |

Formal does not mean old-fashioned. Avoid stiff phrases like *I hereby inform you*. Use full sentences, modal verbs for requests (*could*, *would*), and no slang. Informal does not mean careless: even to a friend, spell correctly and finish sentences.

A frequent problem for learners is directness. In many languages a direct imperative is polite enough, but in English *Send me the form* to a staff member sounds abrupt. *Could you send me the form?* or *I would be grateful if you could send me the form* sounds right. Equally, avoid over-apologising; one apology is enough.

<!-- rich_text: Useful language: openings and purpose -->
**Greetings**
- Formal: *Dear Professor Okafor,* / *Dear Dr. Ramos,* / *Dear Housing Office team,*
- Neutral: *Hello Ms. Chen,* / *Hi Professor Lee,* (when you know the professor uses first-name friendliness, but *Dear* is always safe)
- Informal: *Hi Jamie,* / *Hey Lina,*

**Opening sentences**
- *I hope you are doing well.* (Optional; keep it to one line.)
- *I am writing about / regarding...*
- *I am writing to let you know that...*
- *I wanted to ask whether...*
- *Thank you for your email about...*
- Informal: *Great to hear from you!* / *Quick question about Saturday.*

A strong opening tells the reader in one sentence what the email is about. Compare *I have a problem* with *I am writing about last night's quiz, which closed twenty minutes before the deadline.* The second version already contains a detail.

<!-- rich_text: Useful language: requests, suggestions and apologies -->
**Requests (most to least formal)**
- *I would be grateful if you could...*
- *Would it be possible to...?*
- *Could you please let me know whether...?*
- *I was wondering if you could...*

**Suggestions and solutions**
- *One option might be to...*
- *Perhaps I could...*
- *If it is convenient, I could...*
- *Would you be open to...?*

**Apologies and explanations**
- *I apologise for the late notice.*
- *Unfortunately, I was unable to... because...*
- *I realise this may cause some inconvenience.*

**Describing effects**
- *This means that I...*
- *As a result, I may not be able to...*
- *This is important to me because...*

Use one or two of these per paragraph, not five. A formula that is followed by a real detail (*Would it be possible to reopen the quiz for one hour tomorrow evening?*) does far more than a formula on its own.

<!-- rich_text: Useful language: invitations, recommendations and closings -->
**Invitations**
- *I would love for you to join us for...*
- *We are planning to meet at... on... Would you be able to come?*
- *It would be great if you could...*

**Recommendations**
- *I would recommend... because...*
- *If I were you, I would...*
- *The main advantage is that...*
- *One thing to keep in mind is...*

**Closings**
- *Thank you very much for your help.*
- *Please let me know if you need any further information.*
- *I look forward to hearing from you.*
- Informal: *Let me know what you think!* / *Can't wait to see you.*

**Sign-offs**
- Formal: *Kind regards,* / *Best regards,* / *Sincerely,*
- Informal: *Best,* / *Cheers,* / *See you soon,*

Use any name in the sign-off; you do not need your real name. Keep the closing short so your time goes into the body.

<!-- rich_text: Prompt 1: a technical problem with a quiz -->
This is an original Schood prompt in the style of the task.

> You are taking an online course in environmental economics. Last night, the weekly quiz closed twenty minutes before the posted deadline while you were still answering questions, and your answers were not saved. Write an email to your professor, Dr. Ramos. In your email, do the following:
>
> - Explain what happened when you were taking the quiz.
> - Describe how this problem affects you.
> - Suggest a way to solve the problem.

**Purpose family:** reporting a problem and proposing a solution. **Reader:** a professor, so formal and polite. **Plan:** *10:15 start, closed 10:40, nothing saved / quizzes count toward grade, I prepared / reopen or alternative version, can send screenshot.*

The next three sections show three complete responses to this prompt at different levels on the ETS scoring guide.

<!-- rich_text: Model A: a 3-level response -->
> Dear Dr. Ramos,
>
> I am student in your environmental economics class. Yesterday I was doing the weekly quiz but it close before the time. The deadline was 11 pm but at 10:40 the page was closed and my answers was not saved. I was very surprise.
>
> This quiz is important for me because it is 10 percents of the grade and I study very hard for it. I am worry that I will get zero.
>
> Can I do the quiz again? Or maybe you can open it for me. Please answer me soon.
>
> Thank you
> Aziz

**Why this is about a 3.** It does all three things, and a reader understands the situation, so it *generally accomplishes the task*. But the language limits it:

- Several noticeable errors in structure and word forms: *I am student* (missing *a*), *it close* (tense), *my answers was* (agreement), *very surprise* and *I am worry* (word forms), *10 percents*.
- Elaboration is thin. The solution is two short options with no detail.
- The final line, *Please answer me soon*, sounds demanding to a professor, which is a social-convention problem.
- Sentences are mostly short and similar in shape.

The figure *10 percent* is invented by the writer, which is fine: you may add plausible personal details to elaborate.

<!-- rich_text: Model B: a 4-level response -->
> Dear Dr. Ramos,
>
> I hope you are doing well. I am writing about last night's weekly quiz in Environmental Economics. I started the quiz at about 10:15 p.m., but at 10:40 the system suddenly closed it and showed a message that the time is over, even though the deadline on the course page is 11:00 p.m. When I logged in again, none of my answers had been saved.
>
> This is a problem for me because the weekly quizzes count toward our final grade, and I had prepared carefully to this one. I am worried that the system will give me a zero.
>
> Would it be possible to reopen the quiz for me, or let me to take a different version this week? I can also send you a screenshot of the error message if it helps.
>
> Thank you for your understanding.
>
> Best regards,
> Aziz Karimov

**Why this is about a 4.** The email is *generally successful*: clear purpose, all three points developed with details (times, the deadline, the grade), polite requests and an appropriate closing. It has some syntactic variety (*even though*, *When I logged in again*, a past perfect). The few errors do not block meaning:

- *showed a message that the time is over* should be *was over* (tense sequence).
- *prepared carefully to this one* should be *for this one* (preposition).
- *let me to take* should be *let me take* (verb pattern).

What keeps it from a 5 is the combination of these slips and slightly general word choice (*a problem for me*, *give me a zero*). None of them is serious alone; together they make the language adequate rather than consistently precise.

<!-- rich_text: Model C: a 5-level response -->
> Dear Dr. Ramos,
>
> I hope this message finds you well. I am writing about last night's Week 6 quiz, which closed unexpectedly while I was still working on it. I began the quiz at around 10:15 p.m., well before the 11:00 p.m. deadline posted on the course page, but at 10:40 the screen froze and a message announced that the submission window had ended. When I logged back in, none of my answers had been saved.
>
> I am concerned because the weekly quizzes make up a significant part of our final grade, and I had reviewed the chapter on carbon pricing carefully for this one. I would hate for a technical error to be recorded as a missed assessment.
>
> Would it be possible to reopen the quiz for me for a short period, or to let me complete an alternative version before Friday? I have a screenshot of the error message showing the time, which I would be happy to forward to you or to the IT help desk.
>
> Thank you very much for considering my request.
>
> Kind regards,
> Aziz Karimov

<!-- rich_text: What makes Model C fully successful -->
Model C is not longer for the sake of length. It is better because every sentence works for the purpose.

- **Elaboration that serves the goal.** Each detail strengthens the request: starting *well before* the deadline shows it was not the student's fault; the screenshot offers evidence; *before Friday* makes the solution concrete and easy to approve.
- **Precise, idiomatic word choice.** *Closed unexpectedly*, *the submission window had ended*, *make up a significant part of*, *recorded as a missed assessment*. These are the words a fluent student would actually use.
- **Syntactic variety without strain.** A non-defining relative clause (*which closed unexpectedly*), contrast with *but*, a time clause, a past perfect, a conditional-flavoured *I would hate for...*, and two polite question forms.
- **Consistent social conventions.** Polite opening, concern expressed without complaint, two options offered rather than one demand, a gracious close.
- **Almost no errors.** Nothing distracts the reader.

Compare the three solution paragraphs side by side and you will see the scoring guide in action: *Can I do the quiz again?* versus *Would it be possible to reopen the quiz, or let me take a different version?* versus a request with a time frame and evidence.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: one detail per point -->
Every point gets at least one specific detail
After each main sentence, ask: when, why, how much, or what exactly? Add one answer. A time, a reason, a consequence or a concrete proposal is what the scoring guide calls elaboration, and it is the cheapest way to move from a 3 to a 4.

<!-- rich_text: Prompt 2: an invitation to a friend -->
> Your friend Lina is finishing her exchange semester and returning home next week. You are organising a small farewell dinner for her with classmates. Write an email to your classmate Jamie. In your email, do the following:
>
> - Invite Jamie to the dinner.
> - Give the details of the plan.
> - Ask Jamie to help with one thing.

Reader: a classmate, so friendly and neutral to informal. Here is a 5-level response.

> Hi Jamie,
>
> I hope your exams are going well! As you probably know, Lina flies home next Thursday, so a few of us are organising a small farewell dinner for her, and it wouldn't feel complete without you. Would you like to join us?
>
> We're planning to meet at 7 p.m. on Tuesday at Saffron House, the Persian restaurant near the main library. I've booked a table for eight, and we're keeping it a surprise, so please don't mention it to her if you see her before then.
>
> I also have a small favour to ask. Since you're the best photographer in our group, could you bring your camera and take a few pictures? We'd like to print one and give it to Lina in a card at the end of the evening.
>
> Let me know if you can make it!
>
> Best,
> Daniel

**Notes.** The register is warm but still correct: contractions, an exclamation mark or two, no slang that could confuse. Each bullet has its own paragraph with details (time, place, the surprise, why Jamie is asked). The favour is phrased as a polite question and explained, which is the social convention for asking a friend.

<!-- rich_text: Prompt 3: a recommendation to a new student -->
> A new international student, Mei, will join your department next semester. She has emailed you to ask about housing. Write an email to Mei. In your email, do the following:
>
> - Recommend a type of housing.
> - Explain why you recommend it.
> - Tell her about one thing she should do early.

Reader: a future student you have not met, so friendly but slightly more careful. A 5-level response:

> Dear Mei,
>
> Thank you for your message, and welcome to the department! I'm glad to share what I learned when I arrived last year.
>
> I would recommend living in one of the university residence halls for your first semester rather than renting a private apartment straight away. Westbrook Hall is the one most graduate students choose.
>
> The main reason is convenience. The rent includes electricity and internet, the hall is a ten-minute walk from our building, and you don't need to sign a year-long contract or find furniture. It's also much easier to make friends there, which helped me a lot during the first few weeks.
>
> One thing to keep in mind is that rooms fill up quickly. I'd suggest submitting your housing application as soon as the portal opens, because the deadline is earlier than most people expect.
>
> Feel free to write again if you have other questions.
>
> Best wishes,
> Farrukh

**Notes.** *Recommend* gets a clear recommendation; *explain why* gets three reasons plus a personal touch; *tell her one thing to do early* gets one concrete action and the reason for urgency. The tone welcomes a stranger without being too casual.

<!-- rich_text: Common traps -->
- **Dropping a bullet.** The most expensive mistake. Tick each point off your plan as you finish it.
- **Copying the prompt.** Reusing whole sentences from the scenario adds no credit, and an email made mostly of borrowed language is described at the bottom of the scoring guide. Paraphrase: *the weekly quiz closed twenty minutes before the posted deadline* becomes *the quiz closed at 10:40, even though the deadline was 11:00*.
- **Wrong register.** *Hey prof* or *Answer me quickly* to a professor; *I hereby request your kind attendance* to a friend.
- **Requests without question marks.** *I want to ask can I take the quiz again* is neither a direct nor an indirect question. Use *Could I take the quiz again?* or *I would like to ask whether I could take the quiz again.*
- **Vague solutions.** *Please help me* is not a suggestion. *Could you reopen the quiz for an hour tomorrow?* is.
- **One giant paragraph.** It still counts, but it hides your organisation.
- **Memorised templates that do not fit.** A generic paragraph about how much you love the university, pasted into every email, wastes time and may read as irrelevant.
- **No proofreading.** Agreement and tense errors in key sentences are exactly the consequential errors that hold an email at 4.

<!-- rich_text: The 7-minute plan -->
| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:45 | Read; identify reader, purpose, three points; note one detail each |
| 0:45 to 1:30 | Greeting, purpose sentence, point 1 |
| 1:30 to 4:30 | Points 2 and 3, with details |
| 4:30 to 5:45 | Closing, sign-off, any extra detail |
| 5:45 to 7:00 | Proofread |

If you are a slow typist, shorten the body, not the proofreading. Typing speed matters on this test: if you type fewer than about 25 words a minute in English, add ten minutes of typing practice to your daily routine; it is one of the most direct ways to free up time for thinking.

The email follows Build a Sentence, so arrive at this task with a calm start: take a breath, read, plan, then type.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: protect the last minute -->
Stop writing at about 5:45
Whatever you have written by then, use the remaining time to reread. Fixing one tense or agreement error in a key sentence is worth more than adding one more sentence of content, because the scoring guide weighs how clearly and accurately the message comes across.

<!-- checklist: Proofreading checklist -->
- Greeting matches the reader and ends with a comma.
- The first two sentences state why you are writing.
- All three bullet points are clearly covered, in order.
- Each point has at least one specific detail.
- Requests are polite questions with question marks.
- Verbs agree with their subjects and stay in a consistent time frame.
- Articles are present before singular countable nouns.
- No sentence is copied from the prompt.
- Closing line and sign-off are present.

<!-- rich_text: Drills -->
**Drill 1: fix the register.** Rewrite each line for a professor.
1. *Send me the slides from today.*
2. *I can't come Monday, sorry.*
3. *Tell me if the deadline is changed or not.*

**Drill 2: add elaboration.** Add one specific detail to each sentence.
4. *The library printer is not working.*
5. *I would like to change my seminar group.*
6. *I recommend the campus gym.*

**Drill 3: find the three errors** in this 4-level paragraph.
7. *I am writing to ask about the internship fair. I have heard that it will held in March, but the website don't show the date. Could you tell me when is it?*

**Drill 4: plan in 45 seconds.** Write a three-line plan for this prompt.
8. *You borrowed a camera from your university's media centre and accidentally damaged the lens. Write to the media centre manager: explain what happened, apologise, and propose how you will fix the situation.*

<!-- rich_text: Drill answers -->
1. *Could you please send me the slides from today's lecture?* or *I would be grateful if you could share today's slides.*
2. *Unfortunately, I won't be able to attend on Monday because I have a medical appointment. I apologise for any inconvenience.*
3. *Could you let me know whether the deadline has been changed?* (Note the embedded question with statement order, and the passive.)
4. *The printer on the second floor of the library has shown a paper-jam error since Monday, so nobody can print.*
5. *I would like to move from the Thursday seminar group to the Tuesday group, because my lab session now clashes with Thursday afternoons.*
6. *I recommend the campus gym because student membership is included in our fees and it is open until 11 p.m.*
7. Errors: *will held* should be *will be held*; *the website don't* should be *doesn't*; *when is it* should be *when it is* (embedded question). Corrected: *I have heard that it will be held in March, but the website doesn't show the date. Could you tell me when it is?*
8. Sample plan: *dropped it at Saturday's football match, lens cracked / sincere apology, my responsibility / pay for repair or replacement, bring it in Monday, receipt.* Any plan works if it has one concrete detail for each of the three jobs.

<!-- rich_text: Priorities by level -->
**From B1 toward B2.** Focus on completeness and control: three paragraphs, three points, a polite request formula, correct verb forms. Learn ten formulas from this module by heart and practise one email a day.

**From B2 toward C1.** Focus on elaboration and precision. Every point needs a detail; replace general words (*a problem*, *good*, *help*) with precise ones (*a scheduling conflict*, *convenient*, *reopen the quiz*). Add one complex sentence per paragraph: a relative clause, a contrast, a condition.

**From C1 toward C2.** Your risk is not language but consistency and tone. Keep register stable, make solutions easy for the reader to say yes to, and proofread for the one consequential error that can hold a strong email at 4.

These priorities draw on the CEFR writing descriptors ETS publishes for its bands; they are guidance, not a score conversion.

<!-- rich_text: A weekly practice routine -->
- **Two days a week:** one timed email (7 minutes), then a 10-minute self-review with the proofreading checklist.
- **One day a week:** rewrite an earlier email to move it one level up: more precise words, one more detail per point, one more complex sentence.
- **Every day:** 10 minutes of English typing practice and five formula sentences from memory.
- **Once a week:** the full Writing section in the [TOEFL simulator practice area](https://toefl.schood.online/practice), so the email sits between Build a Sentence and the discussion post as it does on test day.

Vary purpose families each week: problem and solution, request, invitation, recommendation, apology, feedback. Keep a notebook of the best sentences you write; they become your personal formula bank.

<!-- rich_text: Frequently asked questions -->
**Is there a word limit?**
ETS does not state a minimum or maximum for the email. Its instruction in the sample is to write as much as you can in complete sentences. Aim for a complete, well-developed message; for most strong writers that is somewhere around 120 to 180 words in 7 minutes.

**Can I invent details?**
Yes. Names, times, places and reasons you add to elaborate the scenario are normal and expected, as long as they fit the situation.

**Do I need a subject line?**
In ETS's sample the address and subject lines are already provided. Follow what is on screen; focus your time on the body.

**Will memorised templates help?**
Memorised formulas for greetings, requests and closings help. A memorised body paragraph that does not answer the bullets does not.

**Is the email scored by a person?**
The blueprint lists the email as AI scored. Write for a human reader anyway; the scoring guide describes what an effective message to a person looks like.
