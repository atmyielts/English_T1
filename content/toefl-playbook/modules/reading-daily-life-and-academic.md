<!-- rich_text: Why these two tasks matter -->
After Complete the Words, the rest of the TOEFL iBT Reading section is made of two multiple-choice task types. **Read in Daily Life** gives you short, practical texts of the kind you meet every day: notices, emails, text messages, menus, timetables and posts. **Read an Academic Passage** gives you a short expository text of about 200 words, the kind of paragraph you would find in an introductory university textbook.

They test different reading habits. Daily Life texts reward fast scanning, an eye for purpose and comfort with informal or abbreviated language. Academic passages reward careful reading of how ideas connect: cause and effect, contrast, examples and the reason a sentence is there at all.

Both tasks are machine scored, both appear in an adaptive section, and both are very learnable. This module shows you the format, works through four original Daily Life texts and two original academic passages with every question explained, and finishes with traps, timing and drills. If you have not yet studied the first Reading task, start with [Complete the Words](/reading-complete-the-words).

<!-- checklist: What you will be able to do -->
- Describe the structure of the Reading section, including the router stage and the lower and upper modules.
- Recognise the main question kinds in Read in Daily Life and answer each efficiently.
- Read timetables, forms and message chains without getting lost in the layout.
- Identify the five question kinds in Read an Academic Passage and use the right reading strategy for each.
- Eliminate wrong options systematically instead of choosing by instinct.
- Plan your time across the whole section so that no question is left unanswered.

<!-- rich_text: The Reading section at a glance -->
These numbers come from the ETS updated test overview, the ets.org timing table and the 2026 blueprint.

| Feature | Reading section |
|---|---|
| Position | First section of the test |
| Items | 50, including some unscored items |
| Approximate base time | 30 minutes |
| Design | Two-stage adaptive |
| Task types | Complete the Words, Read in Daily Life, Read an Academic Passage |
| Scoring | Machine scored, 1 point per scored item |

The blueprint gives target item counts for each task type:

| Task type | Target items | CEFR range |
|---|---|---|
| Complete the Words | 30 | B1–C1+ |
| Read in Daily Life | 5–15 | A1–C1 |
| Read an Academic Passage | 5–15 | B1–C2 |

Two details from the blueprint are worth remembering. First, all texts remain visible while you answer, so you never have to rely on memory. Second, text length varies from short messages of about 15–50 words up to passages of around 200 words, and ETS calibrates text difficulty with automated readability tools. The ranges for Daily Life and Academic items are wide because the adaptive design gives different test takers different mixes.

<!-- rich_text: How the two-stage adaptive section works -->
ETS describes Reading and Listening as multistage adaptive sections. In plain terms:

1. **The router.** Everyone starts with a first stage of mixed tasks. The blueprint estimates about 18–21 minutes for this stage.
2. **The second module.** Based on how you performed in the router, the test selects either a lower module or an upper module, so that the difficulty matches your level. The blueprint estimates about 9 minutes for this stage.
3. **The score.** Your Reading score comes from the whole section. ETS explains that tailoring difficulty in this way supports accurate scores in less time.

ETS also notes that as the test adapts, test time and items may vary, so do not be surprised if your experience does not match a friend's exactly.

What this means for strategy:

- **The router is not practice.** Accuracy there influences what you see next, as well as counting toward your score.
- **A harder second module is not a bad sign.** Strong readers are supposed to meet more demanding texts. Stay calm and keep using the same method.
- **There is no benefit in rushing early.** Speed only helps if it does not cost accuracy. A steady, accurate pace through the router is the best plan.
- **Answer everything.** Each scored item is worth up to 1 point, and nothing in the published materials suggests a penalty for wrong answers.

For how section scores become a band from 1 to 6, see [The 2026 score scale](/score-scale-2026).

<!-- callout: Decision rule: the router -->
Treat the router as the whole test
You cannot see or control routing, so the only good strategy is maximum accuracy from the first item. Never treat the opening tasks as a warm-up.

<!-- rich_text: Read in Daily Life: format and what it measures -->
In Read in Daily Life, you read a short non-academic text and answer two or three multiple-choice questions about it. ETS says the texts range from about 15 to 150 words and lists examples such as:

- posters, signs and notices
- menus and advertisements
- emails and chains of text messages
- social media posts and web pages
- schedules, forms, invoices and receipts
- short news articles

Shorter texts usually come with two questions and longer ones with three; the blueprint refers to these as 2-item and 3-item sets.

ETS says the questions require you to understand information in common non-linear formats (tables, lists, forms), identify the main purpose of a written communication, understand informal language including common idioms, make inferences, understand telegraphic language (the shortened style of notes and messages, such as "Room booked till 5"), and skim and scan for information. On the test-taker pages, ETS summarises this as understanding main ideas, key details and implied meaning.

The blueprint places these texts in the Social Interpersonal context and spans A1 to C1, so a single section can include a simple sign and a message chain full of implication.

<!-- rich_text: The question kinds in Read in Daily Life -->
Most Daily Life questions fall into six kinds. Recognising the kind tells you how to read.

| Question kind | Typical wording | How to read |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Why was this written? | Look at the opening and the request or action |
| Detail | What time does ...? | Scan for the key word, then read around it |
| Non-linear | According to the schedule ... | Read headings, rows and footnotes first |
| Informal meaning | What does X mean by ...? | Use tone and the next message as clues |
| Inference | What can be inferred ...? | Find two facts that combine into the answer |
| Next action | What will X probably do? | Look at the last instruction or plan |

Two habits help with all of them:

- **Read the question before the details.** For a short text, skim the whole thing once (it takes seconds), then read the question, then go back to the exact part you need.
- **Pay attention to small print.** Footnotes, dates and conditions ("except Sundays", "members only", "by 2 March") are where the answers often hide.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example 1: a notice -->
> **Northgate Recreation Centre: Pool Update**
> The main pool will close for resurfacing from 3 to 16 March. During this period, lane swimming moves to the training pool at the Eastfield site, open 6:30 to 21:00 on weekdays. Members can use the free shuttle from the main gate every 30 minutes. Lockers at Northgate must be emptied by 2 March. Items left after that date will be donated.

**Question 1. Why was this notice written?**
- A. To announce new opening hours for the main pool
- B. To tell members about a temporary change of location
- C. To advertise a new swimming course
- D. To explain an increase in membership fees

**Answer: B.** The pool closes for a fixed period and swimming "moves to" another site. A is tempting because times are mentioned, but those are the Eastfield hours, not new main-pool hours. C and D are not mentioned at all.

**Question 2. What should a member with a Northgate locker do?**
- A. Remove their belongings before the closure begins
- B. Collect a new key at Eastfield
- C. Pay to keep the locker during the repairs
- D. Give unwanted items to the centre

**Answer: A.** Lockers must be emptied by 2 March, the day before the closure starts on 3 March. D twists the last sentence: items left behind *will be* donated, but the notice does not ask members to donate anything.

**Skill shown:** purpose questions ask about the whole text; detail questions often depend on one date or condition.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example 2: a text-message chain -->
> **Malika** (09:12): Are we still on for the presentation run-through at 4?
> **Tom** (09:15): Should be. Room 2.14 is booked till 5.
> **Malika** (09:16): Great. Dana says her slides are "basically done".
> **Tom** (09:18): Ha. So, half done then. I'll message her.
> **Tom** (11:40): Update: Dana's stuck at the clinic, can't make 4. She can do 6 on video.
> **Malika** (11:42): 6 works for me, but we lose the room at 5.
> **Tom** (11:43): Library study pods are open late. I'll book one. Bring your charger, the pods only have two sockets.

**Question 1. What does Tom imply when he writes "So, half done then"?**
- A. He suspects Dana's slides are not really finished.
- B. He has already finished half of the slides himself.
- C. He wants to divide the presentation into two parts.
- D. He expects the run-through to be short.

**Answer: A.** Tom's "Ha" and his decision to message Dana show friendly doubt about "basically done". This is the informal-meaning question kind: the literal words are about fractions, but the meaning is about Dana's habits.

**Question 2. Why does the meeting time change?**
- A. The room booking was cancelled.
- B. One group member cannot attend at the original time.
- C. The library closes early.
- D. Malika has another class at 4.

**Answer: B.** Dana is "stuck at the clinic" and cannot make 4. A is a distortion: the room is still booked, only until 5. C contradicts "open late".

**Question 3. What does Tom ask Malika to do?**
- A. Book a study pod
- B. Contact Dana
- C. Bring a charger
- D. Finish the slides

**Answer: C.** Tom books the pod himself and messages Dana himself. The only request to Malika is the charger.

**Skill shown:** in message chains, track *who* does *what*. Wrong options often assign the right action to the wrong person.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example 3: an email -->
> **Subject:** Fieldwork deposit: action needed
>
> Dear second-year students,
> Thank you to everyone who has registered for the coastal fieldwork trip (14 to 18 May). Places are confirmed only once the 40-pound deposit has been received, and eleven of you have registered but not yet paid. The payment link closes at noon on Friday 25 April. After that, unpaid places will be offered to students on the waiting list. If cost is a barrier, please contact me before the deadline: the department has a small hardship fund, and requests are confidential. Waterproof boots are essential; a full kit list will follow next week.
> Ruth Kovacs, Undergraduate Office

**Question 1. What is the main purpose of the email?**
- A. To remind registered students to pay so that they keep their places
- B. To announce that the fieldwork trip has been cancelled
- C. To invite students to join the waiting list
- D. To explain what equipment students need

**Answer: A.** The deadline, the consequence and the action all concern the deposit. D is a minor point mentioned at the end; the full kit list is still to come.

**Question 2. What will happen to an unpaid place after the deadline?**
- A. It will be held until the kit list is sent.
- B. It will be offered to another student.
- C. It will be paid for by the hardship fund.
- D. It will be confirmed automatically.

**Answer: B.** "Unpaid places will be offered to students on the waiting list."

**Question 3. What can be inferred about the hardship fund?**
- A. It pays for waterproof boots.
- B. Students who need it should make contact before Friday at noon.
- C. Only students on the waiting list can use it.
- D. The names of applicants are shared with the class.

**Answer: B.** The writer asks students to contact her "before the deadline", and the deadline is noon on Friday 25 April. Combining these two facts gives the inference. D contradicts "confidential"; A and C are not supported.

**Skill shown:** an inference answer is not stated in one sentence, but it is fully supported by two or more pieces of the text.

<!-- rich_text: Worked example 4: a timetable -->
> **Campus Shuttle: Weekday Service**
>
> | Stop | First bus | Last bus | Frequency |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Main Gate | 07:00 | 23:00 | every 15 min |
> | Science Park | 07:10 | 22:10 | every 30 min |
> | Riverside Halls | 07:20 | 23:20 | every 15 min |
>
> Weekend service: hourly from Main Gate only, 09:00 to 20:00.

**Question 1. A student finishes work at Science Park at 22:30 on a Wednesday. What is true?**
- A. A bus will arrive within 15 minutes.
- B. The last bus from that stop has already left.
- C. The student must wait until 23:00.
- D. The student should walk to Riverside Halls.

**Answer: B.** The last Science Park bus is at 22:10. A uses the Main Gate frequency. C confuses the stops. D is advice the timetable never gives.

**Question 2. What is true about weekend service?**
- A. Buses run every 15 minutes from all stops.
- B. Buses leave only from Main Gate.
- C. There is no service on Sundays.
- D. The first bus leaves at 07:00.

**Answer: B.** The note says "from Main Gate only". C goes beyond the text; the note covers the weekend generally.

**Skill shown:** in non-linear texts, read the headings and the footnote before the rows. The footnote changes the rules.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: text over life -->
Answer from the text, not from experience
In Daily Life texts, the right answer is what this text says or clearly implies, even if your own campus or bank works differently. If an option needs knowledge that is not on the screen, eliminate it.

<!-- rich_text: Read an Academic Passage: format and what it measures -->
In Read an Academic Passage, you read a short expository text of about 200 words and answer five multiple-choice questions. ETS says the passages are typical of secondary and higher education, are designed so that no background knowledge is required, and come from subject areas such as history, art and music, business and economics, life science, physical science and social science.

ETS lists the question focus as factual information, vocabulary in context, inferences, the relationship between ideas, and the purpose of all or part of the text. The underlying skills include identifying main ideas and key details, understanding the grammar academic writers use, inferring what is not stated, understanding academic, figurative and idiomatic vocabulary, and recognising the rhetorical structure of a text.

The blueprint places these passages at B1 to C2. At the higher end, the claim is that you can connect information across a text and infer what is implied but not stated, which is exactly what the harder questions ask.

<!-- rich_text: The five question kinds and how to approach each -->
#### 1. Factual information
*According to the passage, why ...?* The answer is stated, usually in different words. Find the sentence, then match meaning, not vocabulary. Options that repeat exact words from the passage are often traps.

#### 2. Vocabulary in context
*The word X is closest in meaning to ...* Read the whole sentence, replace the word with each option, and choose the one that keeps the meaning of *this* sentence. Common dictionary meanings are frequent traps.

#### 3. Inference
*What can be inferred about ...?* The answer is not stated directly but follows necessarily from what is stated. If you need to imagine extra facts, the option is wrong. The right answer usually combines two pieces of information.

#### 4. Relationship between ideas
*How does paragraph 2 relate to paragraph 1?* or *What is the relationship between X and Y?* Think in labels: cause and effect, problem and solution, claim and example, general and specific, method and limitation, past and present.

#### 5. Purpose
*Why does the author mention X?* or *What is the main purpose of the passage?* Ask what job the sentence does in the argument. Authors mention details to give an example, to explain a cause, to introduce a contrast or to support a claim.

A useful order of work: read the whole passage once at normal speed (about one minute for 200 words), noting the job of each paragraph in two or three words. Then answer the questions, returning to the text every time.

<!-- rich_text: Worked passage 1: seed banks -->
> **Saving Seeds for the Future**
>
> For thousands of years, farmers protected the diversity of their crops simply by saving seeds from one harvest to plant the next. Each region developed its own varieties, adapted to local soil, rainfall and pests. In the twentieth century, however, many farmers switched to a small number of high-yield varieties, and thousands of traditional types disappeared from the fields.
>
> Seed banks were created to slow this loss. In a seed bank, seeds are dried until they contain very little moisture and are then stored at temperatures well below freezing. Under these conditions, the seeds of many species remain alive for decades, and some may survive for centuries. Periodically, staff plant a sample to check that the seeds can still germinate and to produce a fresh supply.
>
> Not every plant cooperates, though. The seeds of some tropical trees, such as cacao, are killed by drying, so they cannot be stored in the usual way. For these species, scientists maintain living collections in fields or freeze small pieces of plant tissue instead. Seed banks are therefore best understood not as a single solution but as one tool among several for protecting the genetic heritage of crops.

**1. According to the passage, why did many traditional crop varieties disappear?**
A. Seed banks failed to store them. B. Farmers adopted a few high-yield varieties. C. Local pests destroyed them. D. Their seeds could not survive drying.
**Answer: B** (factual). Paragraph 1 links the disappearance to the switch to high-yield varieties. C misuses "pests", which appears in a different sense. A reverses the timeline: seed banks were the response.

**2. The word "cooperates" in paragraph 3 is closest in meaning to:**
A. agrees with others B. behaves in the way the method requires C. grows more quickly D. works as part of a team
**Answer: B** (vocabulary). Plants cannot literally agree or work in teams. In context, the sentence means not all seeds respond as the drying-and-freezing method needs.

**3. What can be inferred about seeds kept in a seed bank?**
A. They remain alive forever. B. Their ability to grow can decline, so samples are renewed. C. They are planted every year. D. They are all from tropical trees.
**Answer: B** (inference). Staff test whether seeds "can still germinate" and "produce a fresh supply"; both actions only make sense if viability can fall. A contradicts "decades" and "may survive". C overstates "periodically".

**4. How does paragraph 3 relate to paragraph 2?**
A. It gives a second example of the same method. B. It describes a limitation of the method and some alternatives. C. It explains the history of the method. D. It argues the method should be abandoned.
**Answer: B** (relationship). "Not every plant cooperates, though" signals a limitation, followed by alternatives. D is too strong: the author calls seed banks "one tool among several".

**5. Why does the author mention "a small number of high-yield varieties"?**
A. To praise modern farming methods B. To explain why seed banks became necessary C. To describe how seeds are stored D. To compare tropical and temperate crops
**Answer: B** (purpose). The detail explains the loss that seed banks were created to slow, as the first sentence of paragraph 2 confirms.

<!-- rich_text: Worked passage 2: insurance from a coffeehouse -->
> **Insurance from a Coffee Cup**
>
> In the late seventeenth century, London's coffeehouses were far more than places to drink coffee. Each tended to attract a particular crowd, and one, run by a man named Edward Lloyd, became popular with ship captains, merchants and sailors. Because these customers brought the latest news from the docks, the coffeehouse became a reliable place to learn which ships had arrived, which had been delayed and which had been lost.
>
> That information had obvious value to anyone willing to share the risks of a voyage. A merchant sending cargo overseas could find wealthy individuals at the coffeehouse who agreed to cover part of a possible loss in return for a fee. Each one wrote his name under the terms of the agreement, which is why such insurers came to be called "underwriters".
>
> Over time, the informal gatherings developed into an organised market with its own rules and membership. The coffeehouse itself eventually closed, yet the institution that grew from it survives and still carries Lloyd's name. Its history shows how a new kind of business can emerge when people with complementary needs and good information are brought together in one place.

**1. Why was Lloyd's coffeehouse a good place to learn about ships?**
A. Lloyd owned many ships. B. Its customers brought news from the docks. C. It was next to the harbour office. D. Newspapers were printed there.
**Answer: B** (factual). The passage says the customers "brought the latest news from the docks". C and D add facts the passage never gives.

**2. The word "complementary" in paragraph 3 is closest in meaning to:**
A. free of charge B. that fit together and complete each other C. polite and flattering D. very different and opposed
**Answer: B** (vocabulary). Merchants needed protection and investors wanted fees: the needs complete each other. A and C belong to the similar-looking word "complimentary", a classic spelling-based trap.

**3. What can be inferred about the individuals who agreed to cover losses?**
A. They were sailors. B. They used the shipping news to judge which risks to accept. C. They never lost money. D. They owned the coffeehouse.
**Answer: B** (inference). Paragraph 2 opens by saying the shipping information "had obvious value" to people sharing risks; combined with their role, the inference is that they used it to decide. C cannot be inferred: covering losses means they sometimes paid out.

**4. What is the function of the first sentence of paragraph 2?**
A. It introduces a new topic unrelated to paragraph 1. B. It connects the information described in paragraph 1 to a commercial use. C. It contradicts paragraph 1. D. It summarises the whole passage.
**Answer: B** (relationship). "That information" points back to paragraph 1, and "value to anyone willing to share the risks" points forward to insurance.

**5. What is the main purpose of the passage?**
A. To describe what people drank in seventeenth-century London B. To show how an organised institution grew out of an informal setting C. To argue that insurance is too expensive D. To explain how ships were built
**Answer: B** (purpose). The final sentence states the general point. A is a detail from the opening, which is a common trap for purpose questions.

<!-- rich_text: How to eliminate wrong options -->
In both tasks, wrong options are built in predictable ways. Name the flaw and the option disappears.

| Flaw | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Not mentioned | Plausible, but nowhere in the text |
| Distortion | Uses real words from the text with changed meaning |
| Wrong person or thing | Right action, wrong subject |
| Too strong | always, never, abandon, prove, all |
| Reversed | Cause and effect or sequence turned around |
| True but irrelevant | A real detail that does not answer this question |
| Dictionary trap | Common meaning of a word that does not fit the sentence |

The correct answer often uses *different* words from the passage with the *same* meaning. Options that copy a phrase word for word deserve extra suspicion.

<!-- callout: Decision rule: two options left -->
When two options survive, test each against one sentence
Find the single sentence that the question depends on and check both options against it. Choose the option that sentence fully supports; reject the option that needs something extra to be true.

<!-- rich_text: Common traps -->
1. **Answering from memory instead of the text.** The text stays visible. Look back every time, especially for numbers, dates and names.
2. **Choosing the first plausible option.** Read all four. The second-best answer is often the first one listed.
3. **Mixing up who did what in messages.** Track each person's actions separately.
4. **Ignoring footnotes in tables.** Conditions such as "weekdays only" decide many answers.
5. **Over-inferring.** If an inference needs facts not in the text, it is wrong, however reasonable it sounds.
6. **Treating a detail as the main purpose.** Main-purpose answers cover the whole text, not the first sentence.
7. **Dictionary meanings in vocabulary questions.** Replace the word in the sentence and test each option.
8. **Being thrown by an unfamiliar topic.** ETS designs academic passages so that no background knowledge is needed. Everything required is on the screen.

<!-- rich_text: Timing plan for the Reading section -->
ETS gives about 30 minutes of approximate base time for the 50 Reading items. The blueprint breaks this into about 18–21 minutes for the router and about 9 minutes for the second module. ETS does not publish timings per task type, so use these as training targets of our own:

| Task | Training target |
|---|---|
| Complete the Words paragraph | about 2 minutes |
| Daily Life text, 2 questions | about 1 minute |
| Daily Life text, 3 questions | about 1.5 minutes |
| Academic passage, 5 questions | about 5 minutes |

For an academic passage, spend about one minute reading and about four minutes on the five questions. If one question is taking more than a minute, choose the best remaining option and continue; every item is worth the same.

Practise under realistic conditions in the [TOEFL simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice), and take a [full mock exam](https://toefl.schood.online/full-exam) at least once before test day so that the section length feels familiar.

<!-- rich_text: Drill 1: Daily Life texts -->
**Text A: a social media post**

> **Hilltop Community Garden** · 2 h
> Huge thanks to everyone who turned up in the rain on Saturday! 40 new fruit trees are in the ground. Watering rota for the next month is pinned by the shed door. Can't make your slot? Swap with someone, don't just skip it. Young trees won't survive a dry week.

1. What is the main purpose of the post?
   A. To recruit new members B. To thank volunteers and organise watering C. To announce that the garden will close D. To sell fruit trees
2. What does the writer imply about missing a watering slot?
   A. It is acceptable in rainy weather. B. It could cause young trees to die. C. It will lead to a fine. D. It must be reported to the shed.

**Text B: a menu notice**

> **Café Lingua: Lunch specials (12:00–14:30)**
> Soup of the day + bread: 3.50
> Lentil curry with rice: 5.20 (V)
> Grilled chicken wrap: 5.80
> Student card holders: 10% off before 13:00
> (V) = vegetarian. Ask staff about nut allergies.

3. A vegetarian student with a student card buys lunch at 12:30. Which dish fits, and does the discount apply?
   A. Chicken wrap, with discount B. Lentil curry, with discount C. Lentil curry, without discount D. Soup, without discount
4. What should a customer with a nut allergy do?
   A. Avoid the café B. Order only soup C. Speak to the staff D. Choose dishes marked V

<!-- rich_text: Drill 2: a short academic passage -->
> **Why Bridges Sway**
>
> Engineers once assumed that a bridge strong enough to carry heavy loads would also stay still. Experience proved otherwise. Wind, traffic and even crowds of pedestrians can make a bridge move, and when the push arrives at the same rhythm as the structure's natural motion, small movements can grow larger with each cycle. This effect, called resonance, is similar to pushing a child on a swing at exactly the right moment.
>
> Modern designers therefore study a bridge's natural rhythms before it is built. Wind-tunnel tests on scale models reveal shapes that are likely to catch the wind badly. Where movement cannot be designed out, engineers add dampers, devices that absorb energy, much as shock absorbers smooth the ride of a car. The goal is not a bridge that never moves but one whose movement never grows.

5. According to the passage, when can small movements grow larger?
6. The word "badly" in paragraph 2 is closest in meaning to: A. seriously B. in a way that causes harmful movement C. rarely D. clumsily
7. What can be inferred about early engineers?
8. Why does the author mention a child on a swing?
9. How does paragraph 2 relate to paragraph 1?

<!-- rich_text: Drill answers -->
1. **B.** The post thanks people who came and points to the watering rota. Nothing about recruitment or sales.
2. **B.** "Young trees won't survive a dry week" explains why skipping is a problem. A misreads the rain on Saturday.
3. **B.** The lentil curry is the only main dish marked V (soup is not marked V, so its suitability is unknown), and 12:30 is before 13:00, so the 10% discount applies.
4. **C.** "Ask staff about nut allergies." D is a distortion: V means vegetarian, not nut-free.
5. When the push arrives at the same rhythm as the structure's natural motion (factual).
6. **B.** Here "badly" describes shapes that catch the wind in a way that produces dangerous movement, not severity in general (vocabulary in context).
7. They focused on strength and did not expect strong bridges to move dangerously (inference from "once assumed ... Experience proved otherwise").
8. To explain resonance with a familiar comparison (purpose).
9. Paragraph 1 explains a problem; paragraph 2 describes the solutions engineers use (relationship: problem and solution).

<!-- rich_text: Priorities by level -->
#### Band 3 to band 4 (B1 to B2)
- Read the question type first and use the matching strategy.
- Scan accurately for dates, times, prices and names in Daily Life texts.
- Always check every option against the text before choosing.

#### Band 4 to band 5 (B2 to C1)
- Master inference: the answer must be fully supported, never imagined.
- Label paragraph functions while reading academic passages (problem, example, limitation).
- Learn the informal language of messages: "should be", "can't make it", "stuck at", "grab a room", "basically done".

#### Band 5 to band 6 (C1 to C2)
- Handle the upper module's denser passages without slowing down: read for structure first, details second.
- Resolve close two-option choices by finding the single sentence each option depends on.
- Recognise tone and implied attitude, such as irony in messages and caution in academic claims ("may", "suggests").

The bands are on the 1–6 TOEFL scale explained in [The 2026 score scale](/score-scale-2026).

<!-- rich_text: A weekly practice routine -->
This routine takes about 25–30 minutes a day. Keep it regular; steady daily reading does more than an occasional long session.

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| Mon | Four Daily Life texts from real life: notices, emails, timetables; write two questions for each |
| Tue | One academic passage, untimed, with paragraph labels |
| Wed | Timed mixed set: two Daily Life texts and one academic passage |
| Thu | Vocabulary in context: ten words, test each in its sentence |
| Fri | One academic passage timed at five minutes, then error review |
| Sat | Reading section in the [TOEFL simulator practice](https://toefl.schood.online/practice) |
| Sun | Rest or light reading for pleasure |

Writing your own questions about real texts on Monday is one of the best ways to learn how question writers think.

- Did I finish within my training time targets?

If you are routed to the upper module, you should expect more demanding material. That is the design working as intended.
